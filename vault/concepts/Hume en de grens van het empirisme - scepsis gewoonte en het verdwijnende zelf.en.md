# Hume and the limit of empiricism: scepticism, habit and the disappearing self

## Core
David Hume (1711-1776) pushed empiricism further than Locke or Berkeley dared, and arrived at a deep scepticism. If all knowledge comes from experience, he argues, then we can know less than we think. We see no real causal connections — only sequences. We have no experience of a persistent self — only a bundle of changing perceptions. We have no rational justification for induction — only a habit of thinking. Hume thereby replaced rational knowledge with **psychological habit** as the foundation of human understanding. Russell sees Hume as the peak and the bankruptcy of empiricism: he carried Locke's programme to its logical end and thereby showed that this end is a dead-end street. At the same time Russell values him as the sharpest thinker of the British empiricist tradition.

## Explanation
Hume was born in Edinburgh, studied law without enthusiasm, and turned to philosophy from his twentieth year. His first major work, *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739-40), he wrote before he was thirty — a philosophical masterpiece he later described as "stillborn from the press". It found few readers. Hume later reworked the material into more accessible books, especially *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), which did have influence. Russell notes that after his philosophical work Hume mainly became a successful historian — his *History of England* was more popular than his philosophy, an ironic consequence for someone whose philosophical arguments were so penetrating.

Hume was known for his cheerful character and social charm. He died calmly after a long illness, without religious conversion despite the urging of concerned friends — his friend Adam Smith wrote a moving piece about Hume's stoic death. For Russell, Hume was proof that scepticism and inner peace need not conflict.

**Impressions and ideas.** Hume's entire philosophy builds on one distinction. Our mind contains **impressions** (powerful, vivid perceptions — pain, colour, sound at the moment of experience) and **ideas** (weaker copies of impressions in memory or imagination). Every real idea must be traceable to an earlier impression. If we use a word without finding a corresponding impression, the word is meaningless.

Russell notes how sharp this empiricist principle is, and how destructive. Hume applies it systematically to concepts philosophers took for granted — substance, cause, self, God, soul — and discovers that many of them have no real meaning, or a much weaker meaning than we thought.

**The causality problem.** Hume's most famous analysis concerns causality. When we say that A causes B — that the billiard ball sets the second ball in motion — what do we actually perceive? Russell follows Hume's analysis step by step.

We see A move. We see afterwards B move. We see them touch each other. But we see no **necessary connection** between the two events. We see no "caused" as a perceivable relation. It is simply one event, then another. Our idea of causality, Hume claims, does not come from a perception of a real connection but from the fact that we have repeatedly seen A followed by B. Our mind forms through habit an **expectation** that B will follow again when A occurs. That expectation is psychologically real, but does not correspond to a perceivable fact in the world.

Russell finds this a startling result. Causality, the backbone of science, is according to Hume not a rational structure in reality but a psychological habit in our thinking. Science is not based on rational insights but on habits of expectation. This undermines the self-confidence of the Enlightenment: reason does not rule our knowledge, habit does.

**The problem of induction.** Related to the causality problem is the [[Hume's problem of induction|problem of induction]] — a separate note in this cluster that goes deeper into this aspect. Briefly: how do we justify the step from "these A's have always been followed by B's" to "all A's will be followed by B's"? Hume shows this step is not logically deductive and cannot be empirically validated without becoming circular. We do it from habit, not from reason.

**The disappearing self.** A second famous analysis concerns the self. Descartes had taken the existence of a thinking self as indubitable certainty. Hume asks: when I look into myself, what do I find? Pain, colour, thoughts, memories, desires — but never an **I** that has these perceptions. The self is not a perceivable object beneath the perceptions; it is only a bundle of changing perceptions.

Hume's **bundle theory of the self**: there is no substantial I, only a continuously changing stream of mental events that we wrongly interpret as "one person". Russell notes that this is a radical conclusion — not only is Descartes' cogito undermined, but our entire self-experience appears as an illusion of thinking. What remains is a collection of perceptions, not an owner of those perceptions.

Yet Hume himself acknowledged this conclusion goes against our deepest intuitions. In an appendix to the *Treatise* he admitted he had not been able to develop a fully satisfying theory of the self — a rare moment of philosophical modesty Russell appreciates.

**Is-ought.** A third important contribution is Hume's **is-ought** distinction. In a short passage of the *Treatise* Hume notes that philosophers often unnoticedly move from statements about what **is** to statements about what **ought**. He points out that such a transition is never logical — from a factual description no moral duty can be derived without adding an implicit moral premise.

Russell sees here one of the most influential philosophical remarks ever made. Twentieth-century meta-ethics, ecological philosophy, evolutionary ethics — all these fields wrestle with the Humean challenge that facts cannot produce values. The is-ought gap is perhaps Hume's most lasting legacy.

**Naturalism as way out.** Hume's own reaction to his scepticism is remarkable. He does not claim that we should therefore stop living or thinking as we usually do. On the contrary: he accepts that we live according to habit, instinct and feeling, not according to reason. The philosopher in his study can come to sceptical conclusions; the philosopher at dinner with friends forgets them and enjoys the company. Russell notes that this **naturalism** — the surrender to our natural human inclinations despite philosophical scepticism — contains a deep wisdom. Philosophy can undermine our foundations, but life goes on, and must go on.

At the same time Russell warns about the price of this position. If reason is ultimately powerless to correct our habits, we also have no rational foundation to combat superstition, prejudice or fanaticism. Hume himself wrote brilliant critiques of religion and miracles, but his naturalistic philosophy offers no strong argument why rational criticism should take precedence over habit. That is a tension Hume does not resolve.

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell treats Hume with great admiration and equally great concern. Admiration because Hume represents the sharpest thinking in the British empiricist tradition — no one carried the empiricist premises through so consistently. Concern because the outcome is a form of scepticism that threatens science and reasonableness. Russell writes explicitly that Hume brought empiricism to its logical end, and thereby showed that this end is unlivable. We cannot really live as if we know nothing about causes, as if we have no self, as if our induction is unjustified.

What Russell takes from this impasse is a spur. If consistent empiricism is unlivable, we must reconsider the premises. Something in our description of what experience is, or what valid justification is, must be different. Hume's scepticism is thus not a conclusion but a challenge — proof that a naive empiricism does not work and that a better theory of knowledge is needed. Kant would later pick up precisely this challenge.

## Why this matters
Hume marks perhaps the most dramatic moment in modern philosophy. He showed that empiricism — an attractive and intuitive philosophical position — leads to scepticism about precisely the knowledge we most depend on. Science, daily life, moral judgements, personal identity: all rest on assumptions Hume's analysis cannot justify. This was no small technical question but a fundamental challenge. How do you go on when your most trustworthy foundation turns out to be untrustworthy? Hume's own answer was: live according to habit and accept philosophical scepticism as a curiosity. But for many after him this was unsatisfying, and it led to new philosophical programmes — Kant's transcendental philosophy, pragmatism, logical positivism — all of which in some way reacted to the Humean challenge. In that sense Hume is the philosopher who forced modern philosophy to think more deeply about what it actually does, and that question is still productive. Whoever does not have their foundations questioned does not know how solid they are — Hume ensures we keep questioning them.

## Open questions
- Is Hume's scepticism about causality definitive, or are there ways to ground causal connections empirically that he had not foreseen? Some modern philosophers hold that the combination of scientific explanation and counterfactual analysis largely solves Hume's problem; others find Hume is still right.
- Does the bundle theory of the self hold up? Modern neuroscience shows the self has no separate location or substance in the brain, which seems to confirm Hume — but also that there are stable patterns of self-organisation, which suggests the bundle theory may miss something.
- Is Hume's naturalism — living according to habit despite philosophical scepticism — a deep wisdom or a philosophical capitulation? Russell deliberately leaves the tension open.
