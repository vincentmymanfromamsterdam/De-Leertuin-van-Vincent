# Socrates and methodical doubt: 'I know that I know nothing'

## Core
Socrates (470-399 BCE) wrote nothing and founded no school, yet permanently changed western philosophy through his method. He shifted the focus of philosophical questions from nature to man — not "what does everything consist of?" but "how should we live?". His method was simple and revolutionary at once: by asking questions, testing definitions, and exposing assumptions, he showed that what his interlocutors held as certain knowledge was often untenable. His confession "I know that I know nothing" was no false modesty but a methodological position — starting from one's own ignorance as the point of departure for inquiry. For this method he was sentenced to death in 399 BCE by his fellow citizens.

## Explanation
What we know about Socrates we do not know from himself. He left no writings, and what we know comes mainly via Plato's dialogues and Xenophon's memoirs. Russell points out that the "Socrates problem" — distinguishing the historical person from the literary character — is insoluble, but that we still get a fairly sharp picture of his influence.

For Socrates, philosophy was not an academic pursuit but a way of life. He walked through Athens and struck up conversations with anyone who had philosophical pretensions: politicians about justice, generals about courage, priests about piety, artists about beauty. His approach followed a recognisable pattern. First he asked for a definition of the concept ("what is justice?"). The interlocutor gave a definition. Socrates then posed counter-examples or consequences that undermined the definition. The interlocutor refined, Socrates undermined again. The process usually ended not with a watertight definition but with the acknowledgement that the original interlocutor had the concept less clearly than he had thought.

This method got the name **elenchus** — literally "refutation" — and is the original form of what we today call the Socratic method. Russell sees in it a new kind of intellectual honesty: the deliberate exposure of one's own ignorance, not as weakness but as starting point for genuine inquiry. He notes that Socrates is akin in this to the modern scientific attitude — the willingness to question one's own assumptions — while his contemporaries experienced him as confusing and irritating.

**The ethical turn.** Russell emphasises that Socrates' great shift lay in subject matter. The pre-Socratics (Thales, Heraclitus, Parmenides) had asked about the nature of reality — what things really are. Socrates shifted the focus to how we should live. Not cosmology but ethics became the central question. Russell sees here a turn that would dominate western philosophy for centuries: from cosmos to polis, from physics to morality.

Socrates' ethical position was remarkably intellectualistic. He believed that **no one deliberately acts badly** — if someone truly saw the good, he would choose it. Bad action arises from ignorance about what is truly good. Virtue is therefore a form of knowledge, and virtue can be taught (though Socrates himself was sceptical whether he had ever achieved it). Russell finds this too optimistic a view of human nature — he points out that people often know quite well what is good and still fail to do it — but acknowledges that the Socratic identification of virtue and knowledge has had a deep influence on western moral philosophy.

**The daimon.** Socrates claimed to possess an **inner voice** that warned him when he was about to do something wrong. This "daimonion" — no god but a kind of personal warning sign — was for him a more reliable guide than the official oracles of Athens. Russell treats this aspect carefully: he points out that it was either a metaphor for a strong conscience, or a religious-mystical experience we cannot reconstruct well from modern distance.

**The trial.** In 399 BCE Socrates was charged with corrupting the youth and introducing new gods. A jury of 501 Athenians heard the case. Socrates did not defend himself by seeking reconciliation; he stuck to his method and argued that he had done the city a service by questioning its complacency. He was found guilty and given the cup of poison. He refused to flee when friends made it possible, because he held he should obey the laws of Athens even when they did him wrong — a position argued extensively in the *Crito*.

Russell has mixed feelings about Socrates' death. On the one hand he admires Socrates' steadfastness and intellectual honesty. On the other he notes that the mythologising of Socrates as martyr for philosophy sometimes obscures how irritating he must have been to his fellow citizens — a man who constantly questioned authorities and satisfied no one with his answers. Athenian democracy had its faults, but Socrates' condemnation was not only tyranny against an innocent man; it was also a community marking its limits against someone who questioned its foundations.

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell sees Socrates as the first philosopher to embody philosophy as a way of life rather than a doctrine. Not his answers were important — Russell finds many of them untenable — but his method. The habit of testing one's own assumptions, of questioning definitions, of starting from modesty before the complexity of human questions: that is what Socrates added to the western tradition. Through this method runs a straight line to Descartes' doubt, to Hume's scepticism, to the modern scientific attitude. What began as an irritating Athenian habit became a foundation of rational culture.

## Why this matters
Socrates marks a transformation in what philosophy can be. Before him, philosophy was something you did with the world; after him, it became also something you did with your own thinking. The inquiry into definitions, the willingness to take one's own ignorance as starting point, the habit of methodical doubt — these elements are still recognisable in how scientists, lawyers and philosophers work today. Perhaps more than any other thinker, Socrates has shown that philosophy is not a discipline alongside others, but an attitude that can be applied to any other activity. Whoever assumes without reflection lives an unexamined life — and such a life, Socrates said in his defence, is not worth living. Russell sees this position as exaggerated but acknowledges the productive tension in it. We have no duty to examine everything, but we do have a duty to be willing to examine assumptions when they are questioned.

## Open questions
- What was Socrates' own position, apart from Plato's literary construction? The Socrates problem remains insoluble because he wrote nothing, but it does determine how we interpret the entire western tradition.
- Is virtue really a form of knowledge, as Socrates claimed, or does virtue also require character, habit and will — things not reachable through teaching alone?
- Was the Athenian condemnation of Socrates an unjustified tyranny, or a legitimate reaction of a community to someone who undermined its foundations? Russell suggests it can be both.

- Bertrand Russell, *History of Western Philosophy* (1945), Book 1 Part 2 chapter 11 — Russell's own treatment with his typical combination of admiration and critical distance
