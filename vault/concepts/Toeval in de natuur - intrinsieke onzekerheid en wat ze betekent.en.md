# Chance in nature - intrinsic uncertainty and what it means

## Core
In classical physics, chance is always **epistemic** — a consequence of our limited knowledge about a system that itself evolves deterministically. A die appears random because we do not know the exact initial conditions; in principle the outcome is predestined. Quantum mechanics, in contrast, introduces something fundamentally new: **ontological chance**, that is, uncertainty that does not stem from our lack of knowledge but lies in reality itself. Radioactive decay is the textbook example: there is no "clock" in a uranium atom that says when it will decay, and no possible measurement can reveal this information because it is not there. John Bell's theorem (1964) and the subsequent experiments by Aspect, Zeilinger and others have convincingly shown that no local hidden-variables theory can reproduce the observed quantum correlations — chance really lies in nature, not just in our description. This has deep consequences: it undermines an age-old philosophical certainty about determinism, it shows that statistical prediction can be fundamental rather than derived, and it makes visible that different kinds of "uncertainty" have different metaphysical statuses.

## Explanation
An age-old philosophical intuition holds that everything that happens has a cause, and that if we knew enough causes, everything would in principle be predictable. Pierre-Simon Laplace formulated this picture sharply in his famous "demon" thought experiment of 1814: an intellect that knows all positions and velocities of all particles in the universe could survey the entire future and entire past at once. Chance in this picture does not really exist; what we call chance is merely practical ignorance.

This is a natural attitude for those raised on classical physics. A die rolls in a way that is deterministically determined by its initial conditions — the angle at which it is thrown, the surface it falls on, air resistance, and so on. That we do not know which side will come up is due to our lack of information, not to an essential indeterminacy in the world. A god who knows all the relevant variables would predict the outcome.

Chance in this classical sense is **epistemic** — it exists at the knowledge level, not in reality itself. For two centuries this conception seemed entirely adequate for all of physics.

**The quantum revolution.**

Quantum mechanics broke with this conception in a way that is still philosophically astonishing. The theory predicts fundamentally statistical outcomes for experiments at the atomic level, and the proof that this cannot be explained by hidden ignorance but by an intrinsic aspect of reality has become robust.

Consider radioactive decay. A gram of uranium-238 contains about 2.5 × 10²¹ atoms. We can predict with great precision how many atoms will decay in a given second — about 1.3 × 10¹¹ decays per second in this gram, given the half-life of 4.5 billion years. But **which specific atoms** decay in which specific microsecond turns out to be principally unpredictable.

This seems at first sight like classical statistics. Perhaps there is a hidden variable in each atom — a "clock" that says when it will decay — and we just do not know it? Albert Einstein originally thought something like this ("God does not play dice"). But physics of the past sixty years has systematically undermined this position.

**Bell's theorem and the elimination of local hidden variables.**

In 1964, John Bell published a mathematical result that would prove to be one of the most shocking discoveries of the twentieth century. He showed that if quantum mechanics is correct, then every theory that explains the observed quantum correlations via hidden variables that are local (that is, that do not assume signals travel faster than light) must violate specific mathematical inequalities — the "Bell inequalities".

The crucial statement: these inequalities are experimentally testable. If you make entangled particle pairs, measure the polarisation of both particles at different angles, and record the correlations between the outcomes, then every local hidden-variables theory predicts correlations that are weaker than quantum mechanics predicts. Who is right? Nature, in her specific experimental outcome.

The first serious test was performed by Alain Aspect in 1982, and later experiments have done increasingly precise versions — Anton Zeilinger, Hans Krenn, and many others have gradually closed the "loopholes" in the original experiments. In 2015, three independent groups published "loophole-free" Bell tests that excluded all known possibilities for classical explanation simultaneously. In 2022, Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger received the Nobel Prize in physics for this work.

The conclusion is empirically overwhelming. Quantum correlations **cannot be explained by local hidden variables**. This does not mean there can be no hidden variables (the Bohmian interpretation postulates them, but makes them non-local), but it does mean that the type of "they already know the outcome but we don't"-explanation that works in classical statistics is not available here. Chance is not just epistemic but appears fundamental.

**How ontological chance differs from epistemic chance.**

The difference between these two kinds of chance is philosophically important. Epistemic chance is ordinary ignorance — information that is in principle available but not known by us. When you throw a die, the outcome is predetermined but unknown to us. Better measurement would give more information; perfect knowledge would reveal the outcome.

Ontological chance is different. No measurement, however precise, can reveal the outcome before the decay takes place — because the information is not there. The atom has no "secret clock" we cannot read; there is no clock. The outcome is determined at the moment of the decay itself, from fundamentally probabilistic dynamics, and not from pre-existing but hidden values.

This sounds strange to those accustomed to classical thinking, but it is a direct consequence of Bell tests. If there can be no local hidden variables explaining the observed correlations, then the exact outcome of an individual decay cannot be fixed by hidden local variables either. Chance must lie elsewhere — in nature itself, not in our ignorance.

**Examples of fundamental chance.**

Various processes show ontological chance. Radioactive decay is the most familiar example: which atom decays when is fundamentally unpredictable. Spontaneous emission of photons by excited atoms works similarly — the moment of emission is statistical but individually unpredictable.

Double-slit detection follows the same pattern. A photon that goes through both slits and interferes with itself has a specific probability distribution for being detected at each place on the screen. Which place becomes the actual detection point is intrinsically unpredictable — not through our ignorance but because the information is fixed only at detection.

Entanglement experiments are perhaps the sharpest example. When two entangled photons are measured, their outcomes are correlated in ways that no individual measurement can predict. The individual outcome (photon 1 passed or blocked) is random; the correlation with photon 2 is exact. Something in nature regulates the correlation without the individual outcomes being predetermined.

**Different conservation laws, different statuses.**

Here our broader discussion touches on something that was noted in [[Valid versus useful - why an irrefutable argument need not explain anything|our methodological reflection]]. Not all conservation laws and not all lawful relations in physics have the same status.

Energy conservation follows from time-translation symmetry via Noether's theorem and can break when that symmetry is absent — for example in an expanding universe. It is a **derived** law that depends on a specific background structure.

Determinism in classical physics is of similar nature — it follows from specific mathematical properties (continuity, smooth solutions of differential equations) and can fail in various ways.

But the fundamentally statistical nature of quantum phenomena does not appear derivable from a symmetry or structure, and cannot be removed by better knowledge. It seems **built into the mathematical structure** of quantum theory itself, and Bell tests show that no classical "retrieval procedure" is possible.

This suggests that ontological chance is no derived property of a deeper-lying deterministic substrate. It appears fundamental — a property of nature at the deepest level we have been able to investigate. Whether there are deeper levels still where chance disappears is an open question, but in every mathematically consistent formulation that respects Bell tests, fundamental chance remains present.

**Determinism as emergent property.**

What this means for the classical world is interesting. We experience our world as largely deterministic — predictable, with causes producing effects, with statistics arising from ignorance. This is true on macroscopic scale, and it is why Laplace's thought experiment seemed so natural for classical physicists.

But this deterministic appearance is **emergent**. Below the macroscopic smoothness lies fundamental quantum chance. That we do not see it is due to [[Decoherence and the transition from quantum to classical|decoherence]]: the exponentially fast averaging that smears quantum fluctuations into statistical distributions, and those statistical distributions into sharply peaked expected values that we experience as deterministic.

A baseball does not follow quantum trajectories because all possible quantum trajectories the baseball could follow lie almost all close to each other — averaging over the nearly undetectable quantum fluctuations of its overwhelming many constituent atoms gives you a practically deterministic classical trajectory. But fundamental quantum chance is still there, beneath the surface; it is just practically invisible through scale effects.

**Philosophical consequences.**

The existence of ontological chance has consequences extending beyond physics.

First, for the determinism debate. For centuries, determinism was considered a natural assumption; free will existed despite physics. With fundamental quantum chance, determinism is no longer a self-evident starting point. This does not mean free will is simply "saved" by quantum uncertainty — chance is not the same as free choice — but it changes the philosophical basis on which these debates are conducted.

Second, for scientific methodology. Classical science was built on the ideal of precise prediction — a model predicts a specific outcome, the experiment tests that prediction. Quantum science has a different ideal: statistical prediction as fundamental. A good quantum theory predicts distributions, not individual outcomes. This is no shortcoming but an adjustment to the actual structure of nature.

Third, for our metaphysical intuitions. The classical assumption that everything that happens has a reason is not correct without qualification. Some events — the exact moment when this atom decays — have no "reason" in the sense of a prior cause that made them inevitable. They happen within statistical boundary conditions, but the specific moment is fundamentally undetermined.

## Why this matters
The discovery of ontological chance is perhaps the deepest revision of our metaphysical intuitions that modern physics has forced. Two centuries of Laplacean certainty about a fundamentally deterministic reality is made virtually untenable by Bell tests. What remains is a reality in which chance is no artifact of our ignorance but a fundamental structural property. This changes how we should think about predictability, causality, and the relationship between mathematical description and lived experience. It is moreover an exhortation to be careful with intuitions that feel "self-evident". Determinism seemed self-evident because our experience takes place mostly on classical scales where quantum chance is averaged out. When we look at deeper-lying reality, this intuition turns out incorrect. It is a good example of what became visible in our other discussions about the valid-versus-useful distinction: rational intuitions that work on one scale are not necessarily universally valid. For the practice of physics, it is important to realise that statistical prediction is not a second-rate version of "real" prediction. Statistical laws can be just as fundamental as deterministic laws — and in the case of quantum nature, they are. This is no temporary limitation that will someday be worked away by better measuring instruments or more complete theories. It is, as far as we can establish, a feature of reality itself. Anyone who wants to understand science as it works in the twenty-first century must come to terms with this fundamentally statistical nature of nature.

## Open questions
- Is ontological chance a final destination, or are there even deeper layers where it disappears? Some speculative programmes (certain interpretations of the simulation hypothesis, some string-theory approaches) suggest chance might be emergent from a more fundamental level. Bell tests do not fully exclude this; they only exclude specific forms of local hidden-variables theories.
- How does fundamental chance relate to the laws of logic? Some philosophers have asked whether classical logic (in which every proposition is either true or false) is compatible with intrinsic uncertainty about future outcomes. Modal logics and quantum logics have been developed as alternatives, but none is universally accepted.
- What does ontological chance say about free will and moral action? Classical free-will debates assumed a choice between determinism and non-determinism. Quantum chance is non-deterministic but also not "free choice" in the ordinary sense — it is random. How does human action relate to this kind of uncertainty?

- John Bell, *Speakable and Unspeakable in Quantum Mechanics* (1987) — collected papers by Bell himself on his theorem and its philosophical implications
- Tim Maudlin, *Quantum Non-Locality and Relativity* (2011) — philosophically careful analysis of what Bell tests actually show and what they do not show
- Anton Zeilinger, *Dance of the Photons* (2010) — accessible description of loophole-free Bell tests by one of the experimenters
- David Albert, *Quantum Mechanics and Experience* (1992) — clear philosophical exposition of the difference between classical and quantum chance
- Nicolas Gisin, *Quantum Chance* (2014) — popularly written introduction to fundamental chance by a renowned experimenter
