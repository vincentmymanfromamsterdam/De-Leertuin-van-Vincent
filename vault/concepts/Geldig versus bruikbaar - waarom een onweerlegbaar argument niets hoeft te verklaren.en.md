# Valid versus useful - why an irrefutable argument need not explain anything

## Core
In logic we learn that an argument is **valid** when the conclusion necessarily follows from the premises. This is a formal criterion that says nothing about the truth or usefulness of the argument — a valid argument with false premises can lead to a false conclusion, and a valid argument with arbitrary premises can lead to an arbitrary conclusion without teaching us anything new. An important and often neglected second criterion is therefore **epistemic usefulness**: whether an argument really tells us something about the world, or whether it only repeats what was already contained in the assumptions. An argument consistent with every possible observation gives us no handle to learn anything. Karl Popper called this characteristic **non-falsifiability**, and he saw it as the dividing line between science and pseudo-science. The pattern repeats itself in various philosophical and scientific contexts: arguments that explain everything explain nothing specific. Whoever sharpens this distinction obtains a knife to cut through refined but empty thought constructions. But the tool requires **careful application**: not every claim that is currently untestable is thereby empty. The distinction between structural unfalsifiability (no conceivable observation can touch the claim), temporal untestability (technology has not yet made the right experiments possible) and empirical indistinguishability (competing claims predict identical outcomes) is essential to keep the criterion from overextending itself — otherwise we would have wrongly rejected many of the most successful modern theories during their gestation.

## Explanation
When we learn logic, we usually begin with deductive validity. An argument is valid when there is no way the premises can be true and the conclusion simultaneously false. Classic examples are syllogisms like "all humans are mortal, Socrates is a human, therefore Socrates is mortal" — perfectly valid and informative. But this form of validity is a rather narrow property. It says nothing about the truth of the premises, and nothing about the usefulness of the conclusion. Whoever chooses the premises well can validly derive virtually any desired conclusion.

**The difference between validity and usefulness.**

Consider three arguments:

The first: "All bachelors are unmarried. John is a bachelor. Therefore John is unmarried." This is valid and true, but utterly useless as information. The conclusion was already in the definition of "bachelor". We learn nothing new about John that we did not already know once we had classified him as a bachelor. This is a **tautological** reasoning.

The second: "When it rains, the street gets wet. It rains. Therefore the street gets wet." Also valid, but now we do learn something — namely that the street is wet. The conclusion was not definitionally contained in the premises; it describes a fact we did not first know. This is **synthetic** and informative.

The third: "God is perfect. A perfect being chooses the best from available options. Therefore the world that God created is the best possible world." This is valid — given the premises the conclusion follows. But the conclusion is empty in a peculiar way. Whatever we observe in the world can be classified as part of the best whole. The argument yields no testable prediction, excludes no possible observation, and gives us no handle to learn anything about the world that we could not also have known without the argument.

The third example shows what is at stake. Logical validity is not sufficient for epistemic usefulness. An argument must not only follow from its premises, it must also tell us something we did not know without the argument, in a way that can be tested by observations.

**Popper and falsifiability.**

Karl Popper formulated in *Logik der Forschung* (1934) and later in *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963) a sharp criterion for what makes a hypothesis epistemically useful: it must be **falsifiable**. That is, there must be at least one conceivable observation that would refute it. A hypothesis consistent with every possible observation does no real statement about the world; it is compatible with whatever happens and thereby has no informative value.

Popper originally developed this criterion to distinguish science from pseudo-science. He was irritated by certain theories he encountered in Vienna — Freud's psychoanalysis, Adler's individual psychology, the history-philosophy of Marx in its dogmatic version — that according to their adherents could explain everything. Every patient, every historical course, every political development could afterwards be fitted within the framework. Popper realised that this explanatory richness was no strength but a weakness: a theory that explains everything actually describes the world not at all. Compare Einstein's general theory of relativity, which made specific predictions (about the bending of light by gravity) that could have failed during observations during a solar eclipse in 1919 — but did not. The strength of such a theory comes precisely from the fact that it could be falsified by observations and survived.

The criterion of falsifiability is not absolute — Popper himself acknowledged that it works gradually in practice, and that there are problems with holistic theory testing — but it gives a useful diagnostic for when an argument is empty. Whoever wants to know whether a claim epistemically works can ask: what observation would refute it? If the answer is "none", the claim is not really informative, however valid or suggestive it may sound.

**The pattern repeats.**

Once recognised, the pattern of valid-but-not-useful argumentation turns out to occur surprisingly often. Four examples from different contexts.

First [[Leibniz and the monads - the best of all possible worlds|Leibniz' optimism]]. The argument that we live in the best of all possible worlds is logically valid when we accept God's perfection as premise, but it is consistent with every possible reality. Earthquakes, infant mortality, wars — none of these can refute the argument because everything that happens in the world can afterwards be fitted as necessary part of the best whole. Voltaire's *Candide* shows more effectively than any formal argument how this kind of thinking works: Pangloss continues to insist in the face of every disaster that everything is for the best, and his invulnerability itself becomes the proof of his emptiness.

Then [[Augustine and the inward turn - time memory and the seeking soul|Augustine's predestination]]. The doctrine that salvation depends on God's inscrutable will — not on human action — has the same structural problem. Whatever happens in someone's life can be interpreted as a consequence of God's providential choice. Exemplary pious life? Proof of God's grace. Deeply sinful life? Proof of God's just exclusion. The doctrine predicts nothing specific about how lives will unfold or what features will accompany salvation, and is thereby compatible with every possible lived experience.

Then **Dot Theory** — a contemporary example from Vincent's own analysis. The claim that "context matters" and that the value of each datum depends on its position in a higher-dimensional space ℳ sounds advanced but is on closer inspection tautological. No observation can refute it, because every outcome can be explained by referring to the specific contextual configuration that produced it. Vincent had identified the Popperian weakness independently of Popper — suggesting the criterion is intuitively accessible to whoever reads sharply.

Finally **certain versions of the simulation hypothesis**. The claim that we live in a simulation can be formulated in two ways. A testable version postulates that the simulation has finite computational power, and predicts detectable artefacts in physical measurements — discretisation effects, rounding errors, a resolution limit. This version is science, even if exotic. The other version says: "everything you observe is made by the simulation so that it appears consistent." This version is consistent with every possible observation, because any deviation can again be explained as part of the simulation design. The difference between the two versions is not what they claim about reality, but whether they leave room for testing. The first opens a research programme, the second closes it.

**The common structure.**

What all these examples share is a specific rhetorical move. They formulate a general principle (God's perfection, God's providence, contextual dependence, simulation control) that is set up so that it can accommodate every specific observation. The explanatory richness — apparently the characteristic of a strong theory — turns out on closer inspection to be the characteristic of emptiness. When a theory can explain every outcome, it actually explains none.

There is a subtle variant of this problem worth mentioning separately: theories that are not absolutely unfalsifiable, but that can be saved with **ad hoc adjustments** whenever they meet contradicting data. The Ptolemaic model of the solar system, with its epicycle upon epicycle to explain ever more precise planetary motions, is the classic example. Strictly speaking, each separate prediction was testable; but the theory could be extended with yet another epicycle whenever a new deviation was observed. The consequence was that the theory never definitively failed, but had so many parameters that it could describe any motion without predicting anything specific. This is valid-but-not-useful in second instance — not through unfalsifiable formulation but through endless rescue operations.

**Three kinds of untestability — when the criterion bites and when it doesn't.**

When we begin applying the criterion, it quickly becomes clear that "non-falsifiable" is too coarse a category. Without further refinement, we would have to reject many of the most successful scientific theories of the twentieth century during crucial periods of their development. Einstein's general relativity predicted the bending of light by gravity in 1915, but experimental evidence only came four years later with Eddington's expedition of 1919. Dirac predicted the existence of antimatter in 1928; the positron was only discovered in 1932. The Higgs boson was proposed in 1964; experimental confirmation only followed in 2012 — nearly half a century later. Gravitational waves were predicted in 1916; direct detection only in 2015. A strict Popperian application would have to classify all these theories as "epistemically empty" during their waiting periods — which is nonsensical, because some of them were among the most justified theoretical positions of their time.

The distinction the criterion needs is a threefold distinction between different kinds of untestability.

**Structurally unfalsifiable** is what we call hypotheses for which no conceivable observation could ever refute them, regardless of technology or future progress. Leibniz' best-possible-world falls here — whatever the world contains, it can always be fitted as part of the optimal whole. Augustine's predestination falls here — whatever someone's life brings, it can always be seen as a consequence of God's providential choice. Strong versions of Dot Theory fall here — every outcome can be explained by referring to the specific contextual configuration. This type of untestability is what the criterion primarily aims to exclude. No future technology or smarter measurement would help, because the problem lies in the **structure** of the claim, not in our measurement capacity.

**Temporally untestable** is what we call hypotheses that are in principle testable, but currently still beyond technical reach. General relativity was temporally untestable between 1915 and 1919 — the right observations had not yet been carried out, but they were in principle possible. String theory's specific predictions about extra dimensions or supersymmetry are temporally untestable — they produce effects that would be measurable in principle with sufficiently advanced techniques, but the relevant energy scales lie for now beyond our reach. Quantum gravity predictions at the Planck scale fall in the same category. This type of untestability is **fundamentally different** from structural unfalsifiability and should not be treated as the same. Hypotheses in this category are legitimate science-in-the-making, provided we stay honest about what remains unproven.

**Empirically indistinguishable** is what we call pairs or groups of hypotheses that each individually seem testable, but that give identical empirical predictions and therefore cannot be experimentally distinguished. The various interpretations of quantum mechanics are the classic example — Copenhagen, many-worlds, Bohm, QBism all give identical predictions for every experiment that can be performed. This is no failure of the individual hypotheses but a property of the group. Strictly speaking, these interpretations are **metaphysical** rather than scientific — they offer different stories about what the mathematics means, but predict the same lived reality. The criterion does not exclude them as meaningless; it shows that they must be weighed on non-empirical grounds (elegance, simplicity, philosophical coherence) rather than via experiment.

**The distinction in action.**

This threefold distinction makes the criterion sharper and more useful. When someone presents a claim, ask first: **what conceivable observation would refute it?** If the answer is really "no observation, regardless of technology", we have structural unfalsifiability — the primary target of the criterion. If the answer is "this specific observation, which we cannot perform now but is in principle possible", we have temporal untestability — a different type of situation that does not justify rejection. If the answer is "no observation that distinguishes the claim from its alternatives", we have empirical indistinguishability — a metaphysical rather than scientific issue.

Imre Lakatos formulated in 1978 a related correction on Popper that is relevant here. Lakatos saw that individual hypotheses are rarely tested in isolation; they are part of **research programmes** with a hard core and a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses. What we evaluate is not the individual hypothesis but the entire programme, and what we evaluate is not "testable or not" but **progressive or degenerative**. A progressive programme makes new predictions that are later confirmed, extends its empirical reach, and provides explanations for phenomena it was not designed to explain. A degenerative programme only stays standing through continuous ad hoc adjustments and makes no new predictions.

With this Lakatosian perspective you can evaluate string theory not by asking "is it testable now?" but by asking "does it make progressive predictions, extend its theoretical reach, produce new insights?" The answer is more nuanced than a simple "falsifiable or not". String theory has produced AdS/CFT correspondence, microscopic explanations for black-hole entropy, new mathematical insights — that is theoretical traction, even though direct experimental confirmation is lacking. Loop quantum gravity has realised background-independent quantisation, suggested a possible solution to the Big Bang singularity — also theoretical traction. Whether these programmes ultimately prove experimentally correct is an open question, but dismissing them as "valid-but-not-useful" would be premature.

**What distinguishes this from Hume's scepticism?**

An important nuance: it is not the case that all non-empirical argumentation is empty. Mathematical proofs are not falsifiable in the Popperian sense, but they do deliver real knowledge — analytical knowledge about relations between concepts that has internal validity within a formal system. [[Hume and the limit of empiricism - scepticism habit and the disappearing self|Hume]] made here the famous distinction between "relations of ideas" (mathematics, logic — necessarily true, but says nothing about the world) and "matters of fact" (empirical statements — contingent, but does say something about the world). Falsifiability is a criterion for statements in the second category, not for mathematical or pure-logical statements.

The problem arises when someone makes a statement that **looks empirical** — a claim about how the world is actually constituted — but that is formulated such that it evades the testability of empirical statements. Leibniz' "we live in the best possible world" looks like an empirical statement about the factual world, but behaves as an analytical truth that is by definition true given God's perfection. The unfair part is that the best of two worlds is asked: the appearance of a factual statement with the invulnerability of an analytical one.

**The practical application.**

Whoever keeps this distinction sharp obtains a diagnostic tool that reaches surprisingly far — provided it is applied carefully. When someone presents an argument, ask yourself first: what specific observation would refute this argument? If the pretension is empirical — if the claim says something about how the world is actually constituted — there must be an answer. No answer means the claim is either analytical (in which case it says nothing about the world) or it evades the testability structure of empirical claims without giving up that status.

Then ask: **what type of untestability?** Is it structural (no conceivable observation can touch it), temporal (technology not yet available), or empirical indistinguishability (competing claims predict identically)? The type determines the right response. Structural unfalsifiability is reason to reject the claim as empirical statement. Temporal untestability is reason for justified uncertainty plus serious attention — future technology could decide the matter. Empirical indistinguishability signals that we are dealing with a metaphysical rather than scientific issue, which must be weighed on non-empirical grounds.

Ask finally: **what does the broader programme do?** A hypothesis that stands isolated without connections to the rest of successful science deserves more scepticism than a hypothesis that elegantly fits into existing theoretical structures and explains various phenomena simultaneously. This is the Lakatosian perspective — look at progressive versus degenerative dynamics of the programme the hypothesis is part of. Theoretical traction cannot replace empirical confirmation, but it can compensate for its absence for a time.

The tool works on classical philosophical positions as well as on modern pseudo-science, on political ideologies that use "every counter-argument is proof of false consciousness", on conspiracy theories where the absence of evidence itself becomes evidence, and on management theories that can explain every outcome with enough ad hoc adjustments. It is a knife that can cut through many refined but empty thought constructions — without wrongly cutting through legitimate frontier science or metaphysical positions along with them.

## Why this matters
This distinction is perhaps the most important philosophical tool one can develop, precisely because it works so unobtrusively. No formal logic course systematically teaches you to look at the livability and testability of arguments — only at their validity. The consequence is that many intelligent people are convinced by arguments that seem formally untouchable but on closer inspection are empty. The difference between a strong and an invulnerable theory is not always visible to whoever only attends to logical consistency. A strong theory runs risk — it makes specific predictions that could have failed, and its strength comes from the fact that it has survived that confrontation. An invulnerable theory runs no risk — it can accommodate everything and therefore says nothing specific. This is why modern science works fundamentally differently from many pre-modern philosophical systems: not because they deployed less thinking power, but because they constructed their premises so that the outcome was guaranteed. What we consider as scientific progress rests partly on the willingness to formulate hypotheses that can really turn out to be wrong. At the same time, **proportionality** is essential when applying the criterion. It would be a gross mistake to dismiss every theory not currently experimentally confirmed as epistemically empty. General relativity, antimatter, the Higgs boson, gravitational waves — all spent periods of temporal untestability for years to decades, and in each period were legitimate scientific positions later confirmed. The right response to such theories is justified uncertainty plus serious attention, not rejection. The criterion is a diagnostic tool, not an exclusion tool: it identifies structural emptiness where it really exists, while leaving room for legitimate frontier science waiting for empirical access. For whoever reads philosophical arguments — old or new — this distinction is a key. It makes visible when a thinker really risks something and when he only performs formal dance within a pre-established framework. Russell formulated this somewhere with his typical sharpness: most philosophical systems say more about the assumptions of their makers than about the reality they claim to describe. Whoever wants to know whether an argument says something about the world must ask what it excludes. What excludes nothing describes nothing — but what could in principle exclude something, even though we currently lack the technology to test it, deserves a different judgement.

## Open questions
- How do we distinguish in practice between structural unfalsifiability and temporal untestability when a theory is entirely new? For general relativity, hindsight made clear that the untestability was temporal, but in 1916 someone could have argued the theory was structurally untestable. The distinction is not always sharply available in advance.
- How far can theoretical traction be allowed as surrogate for empirical confirmation? String theory has produced AdS/CFT and black-hole entropy results, but no experimental test — when does theoretical richness become a substitute for empiricism instead of a productive complement to it?
- Which modern discourses — academic, political, popular — are structurally dependent on non-falsifiable argumentation? And what would a more epistemically responsible version of them entail? This is not a question that philosophy of science alone can answer; it touches rhetoric, sociology, and political theory as well.
- Is falsifiability the right criterion for all empirical statements, or are there areas where this is too strict? Quine's holistic criticism of Popper holds that individual hypotheses can rarely be tested in isolation, and that the real criterion is much more complex than Popper assumed — a line Lakatos further elaborated with his research-programme approach.
