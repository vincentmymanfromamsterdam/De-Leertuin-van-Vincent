# Teleological versus post-teleological political thought - the modern break and what it cost

## Core
Western political philosophy contains a fundamental break between two ways of thinking about the state. In the **teleological tradition**, whose most important representative is Aristotle and which Thomas Aquinas develops in Christian form, the political community is a natural phenomenon with an intrinsic purpose — the realisation of human flourishing (eudaimonia). Normative statements follow directly from teleological analysis: a good state is one that realises its natural purpose, a bad state is one that frustrates it. In the **post-teleological tradition**, which begins in the seventeenth century with Thomas Hobbes and extends through Locke, Rousseau and the whole of modern liberalism, natural purposes and human flourishing are abandoned as political categories. The state is reconceived as an instrument that individuals create via contract or agreement to solve specific problems (violence, freedom, equality). This break is not coincidentally contemporaneous with the scientific revolution in physics, which likewise removed teleology from natural description. What the modern tradition won — methodological purity, individual rights, secular justification of political power — was substantial. What it lost — a normative language for common good, a non-instrumental conception of political community, an anchoring of rights in human nature — is however also substantial and has been problematised anew since Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981). The question whether political thought can remain meaningful without teleological categories is one of the most important open questions of contemporary political philosophy.

## Explanation
When we in the West think about political philosophy, we often automatically stand in the modern tradition without realising it. We speak of rights, of contracts, of legitimacy-through-consent, of freedom and equality as fundamental values. This vocabulary seems self-evident, as if these were the natural categories in which political questions are posed. But on closer analysis this vocabulary proves specifically modern — it was invented or reformulated in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and it differs fundamentally from how classical and medieval thinkers thought about politics.

The break between these two ways of thinking is one of the most influential events in intellectual history. It determines how we today think about justice, state and community. And it is less inevitable than it appears.

**The Aristotelian model.**

Aristotle's political philosophy is explicitly and systematically teleological. It flows directly from his general metaphysics of the [[The four causes of Aristotle|four causes]] — material, formal, efficient, and final. For Aristotle everything that exists has an intrinsic purpose that determines its nature, and political communities are no exception to this.

The *Politics* opens with a statement that carries the whole edifice: "every polis is a form of community, and every community is established with a view to some good." Directly thereafter comes his most famous political statement: "man is by nature a political animal" (zoon politikon). These two statements form the teleological core of his approach. The polis is no contingent social construction but a natural phenomenon. Man only realises his full nature within the polis. To live outside the polis is either sub-human (an animal) or super-human (a god).

The purpose of the polis is **eudaimonia** — human flourishing. Not wealth, not power, not stability alone, but the possibility for citizens to lead a good life in which their rational and moral capacities are fully realised. Everything else in the polis is instrumental to this purpose.

What makes this model so powerful is that **normativity follows directly from teleology**. When Aristotle says that tyranny is a corrupt political form, this is not a political opinion but an analytical statement: tyranny is corrupt because it does not realise what a polis ought to realise. It serves the purpose of the ruler instead of the common good. Just politics is politics that serves its purpose; unjust politics is politics that frustrates it.

This model is developed in Christian form by [[Thomas Aquinas and the synthesis of Aristotle and faith|Thomas Aquinas]] in the thirteenth century. For Thomas the polis remains a natural phenomenon with an intrinsic purpose, but this purpose is itself directed at a higher purpose — beatitude in God. This development preserves the teleological structure and adapts it to Christian anthropology.

For nearly two millennia this teleological framework was the dominant Western political thought. Questions about the best form of politics, about the duty to obey, about legitimate power — all these questions were posed and answered within teleological categories.

**The modern break.**

In the seventeenth century something radical happens. Political philosophy is consciously and systematically decoupled from teleology. This break is not coincidentally contemporaneous with a parallel break in natural science. When Galileo and later Newton remove final causation from physics — when a ball rolling down a slope is no longer understood as something seeking its natural place but as something obeying mechanical laws — teleology also becomes suspect in politics.

The first to consistently implement this turn is Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679). His *Leviathan* (1651) designs political philosophy on explicitly non-teleological foundations. For Hobbes there are no natural purposes for humans or communities. What there is are individual passions — desire for self-preservation, fear of death, striving for power — and the rational calculation to satisfy them. In the "state of nature", before the existence of political authority, humans would continuously fight each other for resources; life would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short" (Hobbes's famous formulation).

The state arises, according to Hobbes, not because it realises human nature (Aristotle) but because rational individuals see that they are better off when they transfer their natural right to violence to a sovereign authority. The state is a **social contract** — an agreement between self-interested actors to avoid mutual violence. Not realisation of human nature but practical agreement; not intrinsic purpose but instrumental arrangement.

This turn is philosophically radical. Where Aristotle asked "what is the nature of political community?" (with a teleological answer), Hobbes asks "why would individuals consent to political authority?" (with an instrumental answer). The starting point shifts from community-as-natural to individual-as-primary. Justification of the state shifts from teleological (realisation of flourishing) to contractual (rational consent). The measure of good politics shifts from flourishing-realisation to problem-solving (particularly the problem of violence).

John Locke (1632-1704) follows Hobbes in the contract approach but modifies it substantially. For Locke the state of nature is no chaos but a condition of equality under natural law, in which individuals have natural rights to life, liberty and property. The state arises when individuals consent to a common authority to better protect these rights. Legitimacy rests on consent; when the state systematically violates the rights it ought to protect, resistance is legitimised.

Locke's model is post-teleological in the same structural sense as Hobbes: the state is instrument, not realisation; individuals are primary, not community; legitimacy comes from consent, not from purpose-realisation. But Locke's specific development — rights as naturally given, consent as conditional, resistance as legitimised — yields a liberalism more oriented to human equality than Hobbes's absolutism.

Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712-1778) takes the contract tradition in a third direction. For Rousseau the individual state of nature is neither war (Hobbes) nor lawful equality (Locke) but a condition of pre-social simplicity, in which humans are "good" without knowing it. Civilisation and society corrupt this original nature; they produce inequality, greed and power relations that distort man. The political problem is how the state can be arranged so that it restores the freedom and equality of the state of nature within social conditions.

Rousseau's solution is the "general will" — a collective expression of will that is not the sum of individual preferences but articulates the common good. Whoever follows the general will is truly free, even when this goes against their immediate individual preferences. This construction is still post-teleological — there is no appeal to natural purposes of community — but it produces a communitarian element that is absent in Hobbes and Locke.

These three thinkers, however much they differ among themselves, share the fundamental post-teleological structure. All three begin with the individual; all three treat the state as construction; all three avoid appeals to natural purposes as justification for political arrangements. They are variations on one theme.

**Why the scientific revolution mattered.**

The timing is not coincidental. Hobbes writes in the time of Galileo (whom he met and respected); Locke in the time of Newton (whom he personally knew); Rousseau in the time of Enlightenment physics that established Newtonian mechanics as paradigm. The removal of teleology from natural description and the removal of teleology from political thought are parallel movements of the same intellectual revolution.

The logic runs roughly as follows. In the Aristotelian worldview natural purposes are everywhere — a stone falls because it seeks its natural place, an oak grows because it aims at maturity, a human lives in community because community fulfils his nature. All movements and processes are explained by reference to final causes.

When the new physics of Galileo and Newton shows that falling stones can be explained without reference to natural places — that mechanical laws suffice — the question is sharpened whether teleology still deserves any place in scientific explanation. Bacon, Descartes and other early modern thinkers argue that it does not. Science becomes more effective when it seeks mechanical and efficient causes and drops final causes.

This methodological turn in natural science is subsequently applied to politics. If teleology is no longer a scientific tool for nature, why should it be one for politics? If individuals can be understood without reference to their natural purposes, why would communities need such reference? The way is cleared for a political philosophy on mechanistic, individualistic, contractual foundations.

This does not mean that Hobbes or Locke consciously applied natural philosophy to politics (although Hobbes did this for himself to a large extent). It means that a broader intellectual climate was created in which post-teleological thought became natural and attractive both in physics and in politics.

**What the modern tradition won.**

The post-teleological turn in political thought was no intellectual loss without gain. Substantial advantages were realised.

First, **methodological purity**. When you build a political theory on controversial teleological assumptions (what is human flourishing? what is the purpose of community?), your theory usually only works for people who share these assumptions. When you build it on less controversial foundations (people don't want to be killed; people want to own property), you get a broader basis of consent. This is precisely what early modern thinkers sought in a time of religious wars — a political justification that did not depend on which god you worshipped or which flourishing you pursued.

Second, **individual rights**. The post-teleological turn goes hand in hand with the rise of rights-language in political philosophy. When individuals are primary and the state instrument, then individuals have specific claims that the state ought to respect. Locke's natural rights, Kant's autonomy, modern human rights — these are all unthinkable in a strictly Aristotelian structure in which the common good always takes precedence. The teleological tradition has difficulty with individual rights against the community; the modern tradition precisely makes such rights possible.

Third, **secular legitimation**. In the teleological tradition political justification often runs through religious categories (Thomas's reference to God as ultimate final cause is exemplary). Post-teleological politics justifies itself without theological assumptions — the contract suffices, and consent suffices, and rights suffice. In a religiously pluralistic society this is a substantial advantage.

Fourth, **empirical modesty**. Strong teleological claims about what human flourishing is, or what the natural form of community is, are difficult to test empirically. The modern tradition is more modest about what it knows and more willing to revise political arrangements on the basis of experience. This fits with the broader empiricist turn of modern science.

**What was lost.**

But something substantial was also lost, and this has been increasingly acknowledged since the 1980s.

**Normative richness.** The teleological tradition has a rich language for speaking about political goods — flourishing, virtue, common good, nature of politics. The modern tradition often reduces this richness to rights and preferences. Discussions about whether a specific law is "good" easily degenerate into discussions about whether someone's rights are being violated or someone's preferences respected — an impoverished language that does not adequately express why some things are important.

**Instrumentalism of community.** When the state is an instrument that individuals create to solve their problems, the idea that political community has intrinsic value disappears. Citizenship becomes transaction instead of identity; participation in politics becomes costly instead of self-realising. This fits with a cultural pattern in which more and more citizens withdraw from public life — if politics is merely instrumental, why engage in it?

**Flattening of human nature.** To be able to take individuals as primary starting point, post-teleological thought must construct an image of individuals separate from community. Hobbes's fighting individuals, Locke's rights-bearing individuals, Rousseau's uprooted-good individuals — all are abstractions that neglect the actual social embeddedness of humans. Aristotle's insight that man **becomes who he is in community** is structurally marginalised.

**Difficulty with common goods.** Some political goods — a shared culture, healthy democracy, ecological sustainability, intergenerational responsibility — are structurally difficult to capture in rights-language or contract-language. They require a vocabulary about common good that the teleological tradition has but the modern tradition develops with difficulty.

**Rights without anchoring.** The modern tradition anchors rights in various grounds — natural rights (Locke), autonomy (Kant), utility (Bentham), consent (contract theory). Each of these foundations has however its own vulnerabilities, and post-teleological philosophy has difficulty giving a truly binding justification why rights must be respected. They say rights are fundamental, but the question "why?" ultimately often receives a circular answer.

**The contemporary revival.**

Since the 1980s there has been a substantial revival of teleological political thought, often gathered under the labels **communitarianism** or **virtue ethics**. The key work is Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* of 1981, in which he explicitly argues that modern moral and political philosophy is in crisis because it has lost the teleological structure that makes moral language meaningful. Without telos, MacIntyre says, moral judgements become arbitrary — we have the fragments of an old moral vocabulary without the structure that held them together.

MacIntyre and others (Charles Taylor, Michael Sandel, MacIntyre himself, later Martha Nussbaum with her capability approach) attempt to reactivate elements of the teleological tradition. They argue that we cannot do without — that a political philosophy without categories of common good, virtue and human flourishing ultimately becomes hollow. They sometimes build these categories explicitly on Aristotelian or Thomistic foundations, sometimes via more eclectic routes.

This is no unbroken return to Aristotle. The modern gains — individual rights, secular justification, empirical modesty — are usually preserved. But the radical post-teleological turn is tempered, and teleological categories again receive a legitimate place in political philosophy.

Whether this revival will be successful is uncertain. It works against a strong modern consensus, and its arguments are not easily translated into liberal-democratic institutions. But that the question is open again — whether we can do without teleology, whether we must reinvent it — is itself a substantial intellectual development.

## Why this matters
The question which political thought we adopt is no academic matter. It determines how we reason about concrete political problems — about immigration, about economic inequality, about environmental policy, about the nature of democratic participation, about what a good society is. Whoever works without teleological categories can only speak about rights being violated or preferences colliding; whoever works with teleological categories can also speak about whether societies realise what societies ought to realise. These differences matter. The break between teleological and post-teleological thought is also one of the most interesting examples of how **scientific revolutions shape intellectual climate far beyond their own domain**. When Galileo and Newton removed final causes from physics, this was not merely a technical natural-scientific change — it cleared the way for political philosophy on mechanistic-individualistic foundations. We keep seeing this pattern recur in our physics cluster: fundamental changes in how we look at reality have consequences reaching far beyond the immediate scientific domain. For your own reading programme the broader question here is interesting. In the essay [[What is a force - a meditation on relational fields|What is a force]] we saw that modern physics at its deepest level moves away from substance thinking towards relational thinking. This may be a second great intellectual turn whose consequences for political thought have not yet crystallised. If the first scientific revolution cleared the way for mechanistic-individualistic politics, what would a second turn — towards relational monism — mean for politics? This question lies open. It is not coincidental that the contemporary revival of teleological and communitarian approaches coincides with a period in which relational and emergent perspectives also gain influence in physics and biology. Whether these parallels lead to a new synthesis — a political thought that preserves the modern gains but reinvents the teleological and relational categories — is one of the open questions of contemporary political philosophy.

## Open questions
- Can the modern rights tradition truly do without teleological anchoring? Some rights theorists (Rawls, Nozick) argue that it can; others (MacIntyre, Sandel) argue that rights without telos are ultimately justified circularly. The discussion is not yet settled.
- What would a political philosophy be that integrates both the modern gains (rights, secular justification) and teleological categories (flourishing, common good)? Various proposals exist — Nussbaum's capability approach, Sandel's civic republicanism, various communitarian liberalisms — but no definitive synthesis.
- How does the break in political thought relate to the break in modern physics? Both movements remove teleology; both have substantial gains and losses. If modern physics is now moving towards relational and emergent categories, could political thought make a parallel movement? And what would that mean concretely?

## Further reading
- Aristotle, *Politics* (ca. 330 BCE) — the foundational work for teleological politics. Book I for basic arguments, Book III for the politeia classification
- Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan* (1651) — the most radical post-teleological break. Part I for his anthropology, Part II for the contract argument
- John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1689) — the liberal post-teleological alternative with natural rights
- Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *Du contrat social* (1762) — the communitarian variant of contract thinking
- Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981) — the key work of the contemporary teleological revival
- Charles Taylor, *Sources of the Self* (1989) — related work on how modern subjectivity is built up
- Michael Sandel, *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) — communitarian critique of Rawlsian liberalism
- Martha Nussbaum, *Creating Capabilities* (2011) — modern uptake of Aristotelian categories in human-rights thinking
