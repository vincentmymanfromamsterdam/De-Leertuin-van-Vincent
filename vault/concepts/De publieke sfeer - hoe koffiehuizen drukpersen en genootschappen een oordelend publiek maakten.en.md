# The public sphere - how coffeehouses, printing presses and societies made a judging public

## Core
The **public sphere** is the space between the state and private life in which citizens come together to read, talk and judge about common affairs. The concept comes from the German philosopher Jürgen Habermas (1962). On his account that space arose in the late seventeenth and the eighteenth century, in coffeehouses, salons, journals, reading societies and learned societies. What was new was that here, in principle, the better argument counted and not the rank of the speaker. From this came **public opinion**: a judgement that belonged to nobody in particular and that monarchs and churches nevertheless had to reckon with. The [[The Enlightenment as a movement - three ways of reading an era|Enlightenment]] cannot be understood without this infrastructure. The ideas had partly existed for longer; now they acquired channels, readers and protection. The Dutch Republic played a special part as Europe's printing house and safe haven. Habermas's picture is idealised, though: in practice access was limited to those with time, money and schooling.

## Explanation

**From court and pulpit to public.**

In Europe before 1650, "public" was what the monarch or the church displayed: a royal entry, a sermon, a proclamation. Knowledge circulated among scholars, in Latin, through universities and monasteries. Three developments changed that.

- **More readers.** In north-western Europe a large part of the urban population could read by 1750, most of all in the Dutch Republic, England and Scotland.
- **More and cheaper print.** Besides books there were now newspapers, pamphlets and journals.
- **An urban middle class** of merchants, lawyers, physicians and officials with leisure and an interest in what was happening in the world.

**The places.**

- **Coffeehouses.** The first English coffeehouse opened around 1650 in Oxford. Half a century later London had hundreds. For the price of a cup of coffee anyone could read the papers and join in. In 1675 Charles II tried to close them as hotbeds of sedition and had to withdraw the order within two weeks. Institutions grew out of coffeehouses too: the insurance market Lloyd's began as one.
- **Salons.** In Paris women such as Madame Geoffrin and Julie de Lespinasse received writers, scholars and nobles every week. The hostess decided who came and steered the conversation. Here the *philosophes* met their patrons and one another.
- **Societies and academies.** The Royal Society (1660) and the French Académie des sciences (1666) were the models. In the eighteenth century dozens of provincial academies followed, which set prize questions. Rousseau became famous by winning one, that of Dijon in 1750. In the Dutch Republic the Hollandsche Maatschappij der Wetenschappen (1752), Felix Meritis (1777), Teylers (1778) and the Maatschappij tot Nut van 't Algemeen (1784) were founded.
- **Masonic lodges and reading societies.** In the lodge members counted as equals regardless of rank. Reading societies shared the cost of books and journals.

**The channels.**

- **Journals.** From 1665 the first learned journals appeared in Paris and London. In 1684 Bayle started the *Nouvelles de la République des Lettres* in the Dutch Republic, with book reviews for a broad public. *The Spectator* of Addison and Steele (1711) brought civilised discussion of manners and taste to the breakfast table. Justus van Effen followed in 1731 with the *Hollandsche Spectator*.
- **The Republic of Letters.** This is what scholars called their own community: a network of letters, book shipments and visits that crossed national borders and denominations. In this period its working language shifted from Latin to French.
- **The book trade.** Publishers were entrepreneurs. They printed what sold, and banned books sold well.

**The Dutch Republic as safe haven.**

That so much of this ran through the Republic had institutional causes.

- **Divided authority.** There was no monarch and no central censorship. Power lay with provinces and cities, which competed with one another. A book banned in one city appeared in the next.
- **Commercial interest.** The book trade was a major export sector. Amsterdam, Leiden and The Hague printed for all of Europe, in Latin, French and English. Regents were reluctant to intervene in a business that made money.
- **Refugees.** Descartes worked there for twenty years. [[Locke and the tabula rasa - the beginning of empiricism|Locke]] lived there in exile from 1683 to 1689 and wrote his letter on toleration there. After 1685 tens of thousands of French Protestants arrived, among them printers, ministers and journalists. Bayle taught in Rotterdam.

The freedom was relative. The works of [[Spinoza and the one substance - God or nature|Spinoza]] were banned in 1674 and 1678, and his kindred spirit Adriaan Koerbagh died in the Amsterdam house of correction in 1669. But enforcement was weak and uneven, and the books remained available. In the eighteenth century the Amsterdam publisher Marc-Michel Rey brought out work by Rousseau and d'Holbach that could not appear in France. See also the note on the [[Philosophy and its institutional context - why secular thinking needed a safe haven|institutional context of philosophy]].

**Censorship and its leaks.**

In France every book needed a royal privilege. Voltaire was twice in the Bastille, Diderot spent some months at Vincennes in 1749. Yet the system leaked on all sides.

- Books were printed in Amsterdam, Geneva or Neuchâtel and smuggled into the country.
- Officials gave "tacit permissions" for works they could not officially approve.
- Malesherbes, who directed the supervision of the book trade from 1750 to 1763, protected the *Encyclopédie* against the ban he himself had to carry out.

A ban often worked as a recommendation. The historian Robert Darnton has shown that the underground trade sold philosophy, scandal chronicles and pornography side by side, under the common name "philosophical books".

**Public opinion as judge.**

Around 1760 the public became an authority people appealed to. Voltaire used this in the Calas affair. A Protestant merchant from Toulouse had been broken on the wheel in 1762 for the murder of his son, on poor evidence. Voltaire wrote pamphlets and letters to half of Europe, and in 1765 the verdict was annulled. A writer had corrected a court by mobilising the public. In 1781 the minister Necker published the state finances for the first time, as though the king owed the public an account.

**Criticism of Habermas.**

- **Exclusion.** Women ran salons but were rarely members of academies. Workers and peasants took no part. The philosopher Nancy Fraser points out that alongside the bourgeois public there were other publics, with their own places and papers.
- **No pure reason.** Coffeehouses were also places of gossip, party strife and trade. Patrons, money and reputation helped decide who was heard.
- **Older roots.** The pamphlet wars of the Reformation and of the Dutch Revolt already knew a public that took sides.

## Why this matters
The public sphere explains why the Enlightenment became a movement and not a collection of books. It also shows that ideas need institutions: printers who take risks, cities that look away, places where people meet. That is the same insight as in [[Paradigm and scientific revolution - Thomas Kuhn and the social structure of knowledge|Kuhn]] and [[Religion as plausibility structure - Peter Berger and the social construction of sacredness|Berger]], applied to a whole era. For the question of authority over knowledge this is the moment a new kind of authority appears. Alongside office and revelation comes the judgement of a public that weighs arguments. That authority had its own gatekeepers from the start: publishers, editors and hostesses decided what the public got to see. Anyone thinking today about social media and the loss of trust in institutions finds here the first chapter of the same story.

## Open questions
- Was the eighteenth-century public really a place where the better argument won, or a new elite with its own rules about who could join in?
- The Dutch Republic was a safe haven through divided authority and commercial interest, not through principle. Does that say something about how freedom of expression arises in general?
- Did salons and societies make certain convictions self-evident for their members, as a church community does? Then the public sphere was itself a plausibility structure.
