# Spinoza and the one - monism as radical break with dualism

## Core
Baruch Spinoza (1632-1677) is perhaps the most underestimated philosopher of the seventeenth century. While Descartes dominated the intellectual canon with his **dualism** — mind and body as two really distinct substances — Spinoza developed in Amsterdam and later The Hague a radically different system: **monism**. According to Spinoza there exists only **one substance**, which he identifies with God or Nature (the famous *Deus sive Natura*). Everything we experience as separate things — stones, humans, thoughts, emotions — are **modes** of that one substance, appearing to us under two of her infinite attributes (thought and extension). Mind and body are not two separate entities that interact (the problem Descartes could never satisfactorily solve), but **two aspects of the same** — his famed **parallelism doctrine**. This position brought Spinoza into conflict with virtually every religious authority of his time. In 1656 at age 23 he was **excommunicated** by the Jewish community of Amsterdam with one of the heaviest *cherem* declarations ever pronounced. His works were condemned as blasphemous by Catholic, Calvinist and Jewish authorities. His main work, the *Ethics*, appeared only posthumously in 1677. For more than a century Spinoza study was actively suppressed — those who openly embraced him lost professional and theological credibility. Only with the German Pantheism controversy (1785) did his work return to the centre of philosophy. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries Spinoza has undergone a substantial revival, partly because his monism fits strikingly well with what modern neuroscience and physics discover about the nature of reality.

## Explanation
To understand Spinoza you must first see the problem he solved. Descartes had published his *Meditations* in 1641 with his famous argument: there are two really distinct substances, *res cogitans* (thinking thing, mind) and *res extensa* (extended thing, body). The mind is not in space, has no dimensions, thinks. The body is in space, has dimensions, does not think. Both are real substances that can exist independently of each other.

This created a problem that already during Descartes' life seemed unsolvable: **how do they interact?** If mind and body are really distinct substances, how can an un-extended thought cause an extended movement? Descartes' answer — that interaction takes place in the pineal gland — was ridiculed by contemporaries. How can something without extension mechanically move something extended?

**The radical turn.**

Spinoza's answer was fundamental: there is no interaction problem because there are not two substances. There is only **one substance**. This substance has infinitely many **attributes** (fundamental ways in which it exists and can be known), of which we know only two: **thought** and **extension**. Everything that exists is a **mode** — a specific way in which this one substance presents itself.

What we call "mind" is the one substance under the attribute of thought. What we call "body" is the same substance under the attribute of extension. They are not two things that interact; they are the same thing viewed from two different attributive perspectives. This is Spinoza's famous **parallelism doctrine**: "The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things" (*Ethics* II, proposition 7).

When you blush from shame, there is not first a mental event (shame) that then causes a physical event (vasodilation). There is **one event** that you can describe under the attribute of thought as "the idea of shame" and under the attribute of extension as "vasodilation in facial capillaries". No causal interaction — one reality with two descriptions.

**God or Nature.**

Spinoza identifies this one substance with God — but not with the personal God of Judeo-Christian faith. His God is **not** a person who exists apart from the world and intervenes in it through miracles or revelation. God and Nature are the same (*Deus sive Natura* — "God or Nature"). The laws of nature are God's essence; they exist necessarily and cannot be otherwise. There are no miracles, no supernatural interventions, no personal providence, no heaven or hell in the traditional sense.

This was theologically an earthquake. For Catholic, Protestant and Jewish authorities this was **pantheism**, and pantheism in the seventeenth century was practically synonymous with atheism. If God equals nature, then there is no creator apart from creation, no transcendent lawgiver, no personal God to whom you can pray. Spinoza's God is no Father but a mathematical necessity.

**The excommunication.**

In 1656, when Spinoza was 23, the Portuguese-Jewish community of Amsterdam pronounced the heaviest cherem (ban) it had ever issued. Nadler reconstructs in his biography the precise circumstances — what Spinoza had precisely said or written is unclear, but it must have been substantial, because the formulation of the ban is exceptionally severe: "Cursed be he by day and cursed by night, cursed when he lies down and cursed when he rises up." He could not be approached, no one could speak to him or write letters to him, no one could read his books.

Spinoza left Amsterdam, immersed himself in Latin and the new philosophy, became a lens grinder (a trade that kept him financially independent), and devoted his life to philosophy. He wrote in Latin, the international language of the learned world. In 1670 he published anonymously the *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* — a plea for religious freedom and for historical-critical biblical reading that made him one of the founders of modern biblical scholarship. The *Ethics*, his main work, he withheld during his life for fear of persecution; it appeared only after his death in 1677.

He died at age 44, probably from silicosis caused by inhaling glass dust from his work as a lens grinder. He spent his last years in The Hague in modest circumstances. In 1673 he was offered a chair in Heidelberg, which he refused because the conditions did not give him complete freedom of thought.

**The theory of affects.**

A second great contribution of Spinoza is his **theory of affects**, developed in books III and IV of the *Ethics*. Here he applies his parallelism to emotions. Every emotion is simultaneously mental and bodily — not separate things, not a causal relation, but two aspects of one modification of our being.

He distinguishes three primary affects: **desire** (conatus, the drive to self-preservation), **joy** (increase in our power of acting) and **sadness** (decrease in that power). All other emotions are compositions or specifications of these. Love, for example, is joy associated with the idea of an external cause. Hatred is sadness associated with the idea of an external cause. Hope is unstable joy about a future outcome. Fear is unstable sadness about the same outcome.

This analysis is remarkably modern. Antonio Damasio, the contemporary neuroscientist, wrote in 2003 the book *Looking for Spinoza* in which he argues that Spinoza's parallelism and his theory of affects fit strikingly well with what brain research discovers about emotions. Emotions turn out indeed not to be purely mental phenomena that influence the body, but simultaneously mental and physical — exactly as Spinoza claimed, centuries before we had brain scans.

**Freedom through understanding.**

Spinoza's ethics — the aim of the *Ethics* as the title indicates — follows from his metaphysics. Because there is no separate soul standing above the body and able to steer it, freedom cannot exist in the classical sense of free will. You cannot through a pure act of will overcome your passions, as the Stoics or Christian tradition taught. What you can do is **adequately understand** your passions.

A passion you understand inadequately — whose causes you do not see through — happens to you passively. You feel powerless and controlled by something outside you. When you understand that same passion adequately — when you see what causes produce it in you — your relation to it changes. Not because thoughts influence the body (that would go against parallelism), but because adequate understanding itself is a change of your whole being, mental and bodily at once.

This is Spinoza's **freedom through understanding**. The highest form of understanding he calls the **amor intellectualis Dei** — the intellectual love of God — the seeing of everything sub specie aeternitatis, under the aspect of eternity, as necessary expression of the one substance. This is not religious mysticism in the traditional sense; it is the philosophical insight that everything that is, necessarily is, and the experiencing of joy in that insight.

**The reception history.**

Spinoza remained for more than a century a **suppressed philosopher**. His books were banned in the Netherlands, France, Italy, and most German states. Those who openly embraced him — like Spinoza students in the Netherlands and elsewhere — risked excommunication, dismissal, sometimes imprisonment. Official philosophy curricula treated him as cautionary example of atheism, not as serious interlocutor.

Interesting is the role of **Leibniz**. Nadler describes extensively how Leibniz visited Spinoza in The Hague in November 1676, a few months before Spinoza's death. They spoke for hours; Leibniz read the still unpublished *Ethics*. He was clearly impressed — and clearly influenced — but in his own publications he carefully distanced himself from Spinoza. His doctrine of monads can be read as an explicit alternative to Spinoza's monism, an attempt to combine monism-like coherence with Christian orthodoxy. His later letters show more sympathy than his publications.

The revival came with the **Pantheism controversy** in Germany (1785), when Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi revealed that Lessing had told him on his deathbed to be a Spinozist. The discussion that followed — with Mendelssohn, Kant, Herder, later Goethe, Schelling, Hegel — brought Spinoza for the first time into the centre of German philosophy. Goethe famously said he was "Spinozist" in the sense of someone who experiences the divine in nature. Schelling and Hegel built their own systems partly on Spinozist foundations.

In the twentieth century Spinoza underwent revival from unexpected corners. Gilles Deleuze read Spinoza as a radical immanence thinker relevant to postmodern criticism of substance metaphysics. Antonio Damasio advocated Spinoza as precursor of neuroscientific theories of emotion. And philosophers of mind such as David Chalmers and Thomas Nagel have renewed interest in **neutral monism** — a position clearly Spinozist in its structure.

## Why this matters
Spinoza's monism is not just a historically curious alternative to Descartes. It is structurally better able to withstand what we now know about the world. Modern neuroscience finds no "point of interaction" between mind and body; it finds consistently that mental and neural descriptions of the same processes are connected without one causally producing the other. Modern physics, as we discussed in the essay [[What is a force - a meditation on relational fields|What is a force]], is structurally moving away from substance thinking towards relational monism. Spinoza's intuition that there is only **one reality** of which various aspects present themselves to us seems to fit better with what modern science discovers than Descartes' separation ever could. Spinoza's case also illustrates a broader pattern in intellectual history: that good ideas can be suppressed by institutional forces for centuries, and that the canon we inherit does not simply contain the best ideas but the ideas that were politically and theologically digestible for dominant institutions. Descartes won not because he was "better" than Spinoza, but because he was more digestible. This is an early example of what we would later call "plausibility structures" in religious sociology — social infrastructures that determine which ideas count as credible. For the Leertuin, Spinoza is a central node. He connects with the political philosophy cluster (via his criticism of revealed religion in the *Tractatus* and his influence on later republican thought), with the physics cluster (via the parallel with relational monism in modern physics), with the methodological cluster (via his early historical-critical biblical scholarship that ran centuries ahead of academic consensus), and with the broader question of how knowledge authority works. A philosopher who in his time was heretical and whose work was only seriously read three centuries later compels us to ask which ideas of our own time might undergo the same suppression.

## Open questions
- Is Spinoza's parallelism a definitive solution to the mind-body problem, or only a shifting of the problem to the metaphysical structure of the one substance? Why do the parallel series of thought and extension run so perfectly in sync? Spinoza's answer — that they are aspects of the same underlying event — is elegant but does not explain the correlation; it postulates it as fundamental property. Whether this is satisfactory remains a philosophical point of contention.
- How does Spinoza's conatus concept (the drive to self-preservation) relate to modern biological and evolutionary concepts? His intuition that every being "strives for self-preservation" seems an early formulation of something that would later be rediscovered under other names (homeostasis, autopoiesis, inclusive fitness).
- Is Spinoza's radical determinism — everything that happens, happens necessarily — compatible with a meaningful concept of ethical responsibility? Spinoza himself thought so, but this question remains current in modern discussions about free will, especially now that neuroscience puts classical notions of autonomous choice under pressure.
