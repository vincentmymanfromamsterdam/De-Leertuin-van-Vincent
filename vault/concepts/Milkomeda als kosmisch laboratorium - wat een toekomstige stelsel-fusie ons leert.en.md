# Milkomeda as a cosmic laboratory - what a future galaxy merger teaches us

## Core
In about 4.3 billion years, our Milky Way will have a close encounter with the Andromeda galaxy (M31), and possibly merge into a single elliptical galaxy called "Milkomeda". Recent simulations based on Gaia and Hubble data have qualified the earlier certainty about this merger — the current estimate is roughly a 50% probability that the merger will actually occur within 10 billion years, strongly dependent on Andromeda's tangential velocity and the influence of the Large Magellanic Cloud. If the merger does proceed, virtually every theme of modern galactic physics is touched: dynamical friction via dark matter drives the merger itself, disc instability transforms both spirals into an elliptical galaxy, supermassive black holes in both cores merge with possible gravitational-wave recoil, and the dark-matter halos of both galaxies — which already overlap today — virialise into a new distribution. This makes Milkomeda a future cosmic laboratory in which we can test our theories about gravity, dark matter, and galactic evolution.

## Explanation
Edwin Hubble showed in the 1920s that Andromeda is not a "spiral nebula" in our Milky Way but an independent galaxy at about 780 kiloparsec (2.5 million light years) distance. It soon became clear that Andromeda shows a **blueshift** — one of the few galaxies in the observable universe moving toward us rather than away. The radial velocity is about 110 km/s toward the Milky Way. The only question was: is the galaxy heading straight at us, or passing by?

Until around 2012 the outcome seemed certain: a head-on collision within 4-5 billion years. But in 2025 a team from Helsinki, Durham, and Toulouse published a Monte Carlo analysis with 100,000 simulations, for the first time including uncertainties in all relevant observations. Their conclusion surprised the field: only about 50% of the simulations result in a merger within 10 billion years. The old certainty had been too optimistic about the precision of our measurements.

What changed? Two things.

First, the **tangential velocity of Andromeda**. The radial velocity (toward us) is well measured via spectral blueshift. But the sideways motion, perpendicular to the line of sight, is much harder to measure across cosmic distances. Gaia has brought improvements here, but the uncertainty remains large enough that we cannot definitively distinguish between "passes by" and "frontal collision".

Second, the **Large Magellanic Cloud** (LMC). This satellite galaxy is much heavier than previously thought — possibly up to 10% of the Milky Way mass. This means the LMC is pulling our centre of mass at a measurable rate. The Milky Way's orbit relative to Andromeda is slightly shifted as a result. Without including the LMC in the calculations, the merger came out more probable than with her.

An ironic consequence of these new analyses: while a merger with Andromeda has now become uncertain, a **merger with the LMC** is virtually guaranteed within about 2 billion years. The most dramatic event in our galaxy's future may not be the spectacular Andromeda confrontation, but the quieter absorption of our largest satellite.

**If the merger does proceed — the three phases.**

In the first phase, about 4.3 billion years from now, the **close approach** takes place. The galaxies fly past each other, not through each other. Their gravitational fields interact dramatically, but individual stars almost never collide — the distances between stars are enormous compared to their sizes. In a galaxy merger such as we now see in the Antennae Galaxies or NGC 4676 ("the Mice"), long tidal tails of stars and gas are drawn from both galaxies by mutual tidal forces. The spiral arms deform, but the galaxies initially remain recognisable as separate entities.

In the second phase, somewhere between 5 and 10 billion years from now, **dynamical friction** comes into play. After the first passage, in an ordinary non-gravitational situation the galaxies would fly past each other and flow apart again in the expanding space. What keeps them together is a subtle mechanism discovered by Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar in 1943: a massive object moving through a background of smaller masses experiences an effective friction force. The smaller masses are attracted by its gravity and form a density excess behind the moving object, which slows it down.

For the Milky Way and Andromeda, the relevant "background" is mainly their own **dark-matter halos**. These halos are enormous — each extends to 200-300 kiloparsec from the centre, roughly half the current mutual distance. They already overlap today. During the first passage and afterwards, the galaxies slow each other through this dark-matter friction, their orbits decay, and they return for repeated closer passages, each time with lower relative velocity, until they finally merge.

This is one of the most elegant confirmations of dark matter that you do not often hear about. Without dark-matter halos, galaxy mergers would proceed much less efficiently, and the **hierarchical structure formation** we have seen since the first galaxies — small galaxies merging into larger ones — would not have worked. The cosmic history of galactic evolution is in essence a history of dynamical friction through dark matter.

In the third phase, around 10 billion years from now, the merger is complete and **Milkomeda** has formed. The beautiful spiral structures of both original galaxies have then been completely erased. Stars move in random orbits around a common centre, not in ordered rotation. The result is a pressure-dominated elliptical galaxy — more a swarm of bees around a centre than a planetary rotation.

Here the story touches on the disc instability that Ostriker and Peebles discovered in 1973. A rotating disc is dynamically stable as long as there is an extended halo to damp small density perturbations. In a merger, the balance between rotation and pressure in both discs is disrupted; stars gain energy in random directions, and the rotation-dominant structure cannot recover. The difference between a spiral and an elliptical galaxy is essentially the difference between rotation-dominated and pressure-dominated, and galaxy mergers form the natural mechanism by which spiral galaxies are transformed into ellipticals.

**The galactic cores.**

Both galaxies have supermassive black holes in their cores. Sagittarius A* in the Milky Way weighs about 4 million solar masses; M31* in Andromeda is much heavier, estimated at 100 to 200 million solar masses. Their fate after the galaxy merger proceeds in three phases.

First, **dynamical friction** (the same mechanism that brought the galaxies themselves together) drives the two black holes to the inner region of Milkomeda. Then they form a **bound binary system** and exchange energy with passing stars in a gravitational slingshot mechanism — stars are flung away with higher velocity through three-body interactions, and the black-hole pair loses energy and spirals closer together. Finally, when they are within about one parsec of each other, **gravitational-wave emission** takes over and they inspiral within less than 17 million years. Just before their merger, they emit gravitational waves with the power of 10 quintillion suns — briefly one of the most energetic events in this part of the universe.

**Recoil kicks and runaway black holes.**

Here Milkomeda touches on a phenomenon we have already seen operate in RBH-1, the first confirmed runaway supermassive black hole. When two black holes with unequal masses or non-aligned spins merge, gravitational waves are not radiated symmetrically — more momentum goes one way than the other. By conservation of momentum, the resulting black hole receives a **recoil kick** in the opposite direction.

For the Milkomeda scenario, the mass ratio is about 1:30 to 1:50 (Sgr A* much smaller than M31*) — a rather asymmetric ratio. The magnitude of the kick depends mainly on the spin configuration. With low or non-aligned spins, the kick can be tens to hundreds of km/s. In specific configurations with high, anti-parallel spins in the orbital plane — the so-called "superkick" configuration — the kick can rise to 5000 km/s.

The escape velocity from the centre of an elliptical galaxy of Milkomeda's size is about 800-1500 km/s. With an average kick, the merged black hole would therefore not fully escape, but would move for a long time in an eccentric orbit around the centre before dynamical friction brings it back — a process that can take from hundred million to billions of years. In an unlucky spin configuration, Milkomeda could produce a runaway black hole comparable to RBH-1 — a future example of precisely the phenomenon we are only just learning to recognise.

**The dark-matter distribution.**

The most fascinating aspect of a Milkomeda merger is what happens to the dark-matter halos. Here the scenario differs fundamentally from the Bullet Cluster.

In the Bullet Cluster we saw a **high-velocity collision** (~4700 km/s), in which dark matter and gas separated because they have different interaction properties: gas is slowed by electromagnetic interaction between colliding particles, dark matter passes through almost unhindered. The result is the famous offset between X-ray emission (gas) and the lensing peak (dark matter).

Milkomeda involves a **low-velocity merger**. The relative velocity is now 110 km/s and will rise to several hundred km/s at the moment of close approach. At such low velocities, gas and dark matter have time enough to remain gravitationally coupled. The gas slows through collisions — triggering large-scale star formation, a temporary "starburst" phase in which Milkomeda briefly lights up enormously — but the gas then falls together with the dark matter into the same gravitational wells.

The result is that the two original halos initially superimpose into an asymmetric, two-peaked dark-matter distribution that swirls around a common centre of mass. Then this new halo **virialises** over a time scale of a few billion years into a roughly spherical distribution in equilibrium according to the virial theorem. The virial theorem (2T + W = 0) is a fundamental relation from classical mechanics that for self-gravitational systems in equilibrium states that twice the kinetic energy equals the potential energy. It is the gravitational equivalent of thermodynamic equilibrium equations.

The dark-matter reserve of Milkomeda will roughly be the sum of those of both original galaxies — approximately doubling the stellar and dark-matter mass of the Milky Way. The halo will extend over hundreds of kiloparsec, and in the far future of cosmic expansion will slowly become the only visible entity in this part of space — all other galaxies will be carried beyond our observable universe by accelerated expansion.

**A test for our theories.**

What makes Milkomeda interesting from a cosmological perspective is not only the spectacular character but the specific predictions each theoretical framework makes. The standard model with cold dark matter (Λ-CDM) predicts a gradual decay phase via dynamical friction, followed by spiral-to-elliptical transformation and halo virialisation. Modified-gravity theories such as MOND predict subtly different outcomes — without an extended dark-matter halo, dynamical friction would work much less effectively, and the time scale for the final merger should be substantially longer than in Λ-CDM simulations.

Unfortunately (or fortunately, depending on your perspective), no one will be alive in 10 billion years to observe the merger directly. But we can study similar mergers from the past by looking at galaxies in different phases of merger — from first interactions (NGC 4676) to late-stage fusions (NGC 1316). Each of these provides a snapshot of what Milkomeda will go through, thus offering touchstones for our theories.

## Why this matters
Milkomeda illustrates how all themes of modern galactic physics come together in one future event. Dynamical friction via dark matter — the mechanism that made hierarchical structure formation possible — will bring the Milky Way and Andromeda together. Disc instability will transform both spiral galaxies into an elliptical structure. Supermassive black holes will merge with intense gravitational-wave emission and possible recoil kicks. Dark-matter halos will virialise into a new equilibrium distribution. Virtually every note in our physics cluster finds application here. At the same time, the history of Milkomeda research shows something deeper about how science works. Ten years ago the merger was considered certain; today it is a coin flip. This is not a regression of knowledge but progress in precision — we can now specify which measurements determine the outcome (Andromeda's tangential velocity, the LMC mass) and how they shift the probability. That a future prediction becomes more uncertain as we learn more is no paradox but the hallmark of maturing science. Furthermore, Milkomeda puts something in perspective in our cosmic outlook: what appears to us as a dramatic downfall — two galaxies crashing into each other — is on a cosmic scale an ordinary event, one of billions of comparable mergers that have shaped the local universe. Stars will not collide. The sun, if it still existed then, would probably circle in an extended halo around Milkomeda's centre. The universe changes form, not substance. And even when all galaxies in the observable universe ultimately recede due to accelerated expansion and Milkomeda has lost its only visible neighbour, the galaxy itself remains intact — an island formation in an increasingly empty cosmos.

## Open questions
- How large is Andromeda's tangential velocity really? Future Gaia data releases and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will substantially improve this measurement and may definitively settle within a decade whether the merger takes place or not.
- What spin configuration do Sagittarius A* and M31* have? This determines whether the final merger of the two black holes yields a moderate recoil or a real ejection into a future runaway black hole. Methods to measure black-hole spins remotely are slowly improving (via accretion-disc spectra, event-horizon imaging with the Event Horizon Telescope), but certainty is still far away.
- How accurately can we reconstruct similar galaxy mergers in the distant past? Recent work with JWST shows old galaxy mergers with unprecedented detail, but the relationship between observed morphological phases and simulations remains a subject of active research.
