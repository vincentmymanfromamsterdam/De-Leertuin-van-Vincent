# Thomas Aquinas and the synthesis of Aristotle and faith

## Core
Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274) undertook what seemed impossible: unifying the philosophy of Aristotle — a pagan Greek who knew nothing of Christianity — with Christian revelation into one coherent system. His *Summa Theologica* became the masterpiece of scholasticism and gave Catholic thought a philosophical foundation that is influential to this day. Russell values the intellectual achievement but criticises the method: for him Aquinas is no true philosopher because he knows which conclusions he must reach before he begins to think. Still, Russell acknowledges the sharpness and historical significance of Aquinas' work.

## Explanation
In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Aristotle's work returned to Western Europe via Arabic translations. Until then, western philosophy had leaned mainly on Augustine and neo-Platonism. Suddenly, a systematically pagan philosophy was available that had something to say about everything — nature, ethics, politics, epistemology — and that on many points was in tension with Christian doctrine.

The reaction of the church was initially defensive. Aristotle's work was banned at the University of Paris in 1210. But the ban could not be maintained — Aristotle was too influential, too systematic, too convincing. The problem thus became urgent: how do you deal with a philosophy that says so much of value but on critical points seems to conflict with faith?

Thomas Aquinas was the man who took on this task. He was an Italian aristocrat who became a Dominican against his family's will, studied under Albertus Magnus, and eventually became a professor in Paris. Russell describes him as a calm, methodical thinker — no literary genius like Augustine, but a systematic architect of philosophical thoughts.

**The programme.** Aquinas' fundamental thesis was that **reason and faith cannot conflict**. Both ultimately come from God: reason is a natural gift, faith a supernatural revelation. If they appear contradictory, then we have not understood them correctly — not because they truly clash. The philosophical task is thus to show how they work together: philosophy can demonstrate much through pure reason; theology supplements what is only accessible via revelation.

Russell points out that this position is itself already philosophically problematic. What do you do when reason and faith truly cannot be reconciled? Aquinas' answer is implicit: then the reasoning must have gone wrong somewhere, because faith is fixed. Russell finds this "philosophically dishonest" — a thinker who knows which conclusions he must reach before he begins is doing no real philosophy but advocacy for a predetermined position.

**The five ways.** Aquinas' most famous philosophical contribution are the **five ways** to argue for God's existence. They appear in *Summa Theologica* I, question 2, and are the standard Catholic proofs of God to this day.

The first way: everything that moves is moved by something else. This leads to infinite regression, so there must be a first unmoved mover. The second way: everything has a cause. There must be a first cause. The third way: everything in the world is contingent (might also not have existed). But if everything were contingent, at some point nothing would have existed, and then nothing would exist now either. There must therefore be something necessary. The fourth way: things show degrees of perfection. There must be a highest perfection as standard. The fifth way: things in nature seem purposeful. This requires an ordering intelligence.

Russell treats these arguments critically. He points out that the first three ways all depend on the assumption that infinite regression is impossible — an assumption that is not self-evident. The fourth way appears circular. The fifth way is undermined by Darwin's evolutionary theory: purposefulness in nature can arise without an ordering intelligence, via natural selection. But Russell acknowledges that in the thirteenth century these arguments were serious and careful, and that they have played an enduring role in philosophical thinking about religion.

**Substance and accidents.** Another crucial element in Aquinas' system is his adoption of Aristotle's distinction between **substance** (what a thing fundamentally is) and **accidents** (properties that can change without the thing ceasing to be what it is). This distinction becomes central in his explanation of the Eucharist: the substance of bread changes into the substance of the body of Christ, while the accidents (taste, appearance, texture) remain unchanged. Russell finds this application of Aristotelian metaphysics to a specific theological doctrine problematic — a philosophy stretched to support a predetermined conclusion. But he acknowledges that scholastic metaphysics enabled centuries of deep thinking, even though its starting points were not his own.

**Natural law and ethics.** Aquinas developed an ethics based on **natural law** — morality can be derived from the natural ends of things. What a human ought to do can be determined by looking at what human nature is and what it strives towards. Russell appreciates this rational approach more than Augustine's dependence on revelation, but points out that the derivation from "what is" to "what ought" is philosophically problematic — a tension that Hume would make explicit five centuries later.

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell has a mixed portrait. He values Aquinas' systematic method, his clarity, his willingness to formulate every question explicitly and answer it. At the same time he holds that Aquinas was no true philosopher in the strict sense — a true philosopher investigates questions without predetermined answers. Aquinas always already knew what he had to conclude; his work was to show *how* he got there. For Russell this is a fundamentally different kind of intellectual activity. He compares it to a lawyer defending his client: admirably reasoning, but no truth-seeker.

At the same time, Russell acknowledges that the scholastic method produced something lasting: the habit of formulating questions sharply, taking counter-arguments seriously, and reasoning step by step. This method positively influenced the scientific development of the middle ages, even though the specific conclusions were often outdated.

## Why this matters
Aquinas marks a peak of medieval philosophy and gave Catholic thought a rational basis that it has maintained to this day. For someone who sees philosophy as a search for truth, like Russell, this is an ambivalent achievement — a brilliant thinker who kept his knife sharp but knew which decisions he had to make. But what Aquinas does show is that a philosophical system can be built up from specific starting points without giving up intellectual rigour. Whether that is enough to qualify him as a philosopher depends on what you expect of philosophy. The tension between reason and faith that Aquinas thought he resolved has not actually been resolved in retrospect — modern philosophers have largely abandoned him — but the attempt itself, with its systematic sharpness, is one of the peaks of what the human mind could reach under medieval conditions. And his influence continues: Catholic moral theology, the natural law tradition in jurisprudence, and aspects of bioethical debates are still permeated with Thomistic frameworks.

## Open questions
- Can a thinker really be a philosopher if he knows which conclusions he must reach? Russell says no; defenders of Aquinas point out that all thinkers work with assumptions and that the difference is more a matter of degree than of principle.
- Do the five ways hold up against modern criticism? Most philosophers think not, but in Thomistic circles they are still defended and elaborated.
- How does Aquinas' natural-law ethics relate to modern evolutionary insights? If human nature is the result of blind selection and has no fixed end, does Thomistic ethics still hold?

## Further reading
- Bertrand Russell, *History of Western Philosophy* (1945), Book 2 Part 2 chapter 13 — Russell's critical but respectful treatment
- Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* — especially I, question 2 (five ways) and I-II (ethics) for the philosophical core, [English translation online](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/)
- G.K. Chesterton, *Saint Thomas Aquinas* (1933) — sympathetic but witty biography by a Catholic author, a useful counterweight to Russell
- Anthony Kenny, *Aquinas* (1980) — modern scholarly introduction by an analytic philosopher
- Frederick Copleston, *A History of Philosophy*, Volume 2 — more extensive and sympathetic treatment than Russell's, written by a Jesuit
