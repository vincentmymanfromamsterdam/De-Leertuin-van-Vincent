# Popper and falsification - why science advances by trying to refute its own theories

## Core
Karl Popper (1902-1994) turned the usual view of science around. A theory, he held, is scientific not because observations confirm it but because observations could refute it. This is **falsifiability**. No number of white swans proves that all swans are white, but one black swan refutes it. Science therefore does not work by deriving laws from observations. It works by **conjectures and refutations**: inventing bold hypotheses and trying as hard as possible to bring them down. What survives the attempts is provisionally accepted and never proven. With this Popper opposed [[Logical positivism - the Vienna Circle and the ideal of a science without metaphysics|logical positivism]], with two differences. He sought a boundary between science and non-science, not between sense and nonsense, and he rejected the idea that observations make a theory more probable. Among scientists his criterion is still the best-known standard. Philosophers find it too simple as a description of practice, because a theory rarely falls to a single measurement.

## Explanation

**The year 1919.**

Popper grew up in Vienna and later said that his central idea arose in a single year. He was impressed by four theories: Marxism, Freud's psychoanalysis, Alfred Adler's psychology and Einstein's theory of relativity. The first three seemed able to explain everything. Whatever a person did, Freud and Adler had an account of it, and every outcome counted as confirmation. Einstein did something else. His theory predicted that starlight passing the sun would be bent by a definite angle, and during the solar eclipse of 1919 this was measured. Had the bending failed to appear, the theory would have been refuted.

Popper concluded that explanatory power without risk is no virtue. A theory compatible with every outcome says nothing about the world. A good theory forbids a great deal.

**The problem of induction.**

[[Hume and the limit of empiricism - scepticism habit and the disappearing self|Hume]] had shown that no logical path leads from observed cases to a general law. That the sun has risen every day so far does not prove it will rise tomorrow. The positivists tried to absorb this with probability: each confirmation makes the law a little more credible.

Popper chose another way out. On his view science does not use induction at all. There is a logical **asymmetry**: a general statement can never be proven but can be refuted, and refuting is ordinary deduction. So the order is not observing and then generalising. First comes a problem, then a guess, then the test. Where the guess comes from does not matter.

**Demarcation instead of meaning.**

In *Logik der Forschung* (1934) Popper proposed falsifiability as the boundary between empirical science and the rest. The difference from the Vienna Circle is larger than it looks:

- It is not a criterion of meaning. Metaphysics, for Popper, is not senseless and can be fruitful. Atomism was an untestable idea for two thousand years before it became physics.
- Confirmation counts only when it results from a serious attempt at refutation. Popper speaks of **corroboration**: a theory has held its ground. That says something about its past and does not make it probably true.
- Bold theories are better than cautious ones. A theory with much content is improbable in advance, because it excludes much. Whoever seeks certainty says as little as possible. Whoever seeks knowledge must do the opposite.

Popper did not belong to the Circle, though he knew its members well. Neurath called him "the official opposition". Later, in his autobiography, Popper claimed responsibility for the end of logical positivism. That is too much credit. The movement succumbed mainly to criticism from within.

**Where it chafes.**

- **A test never touches one hypothesis.** A prediction follows from a theory plus auxiliary assumptions about instruments and circumstances. If it fails, you do not know which part is wrong. When the orbit of Uranus deviated from Newton's prediction, nobody rejected Newton. An unknown planet was assumed, and Neptune was found. This is the Duhem-Quine problem. Popper knew it and answered with a rule: rescues after the fact are allowed only if they are themselves testable. Neptune was. That turns falsification into an agreement about decent conduct and not a logical fact.
- **Observations are fallible too.** The statements with which you refute a theory are themselves not certain. Popper acknowledged this. Science, he said, is a building on piles driven into a swamp: they go deep enough to carry the structure for the time being and never reach solid ground.
- **Scientists behave differently.** [[Paradigm and scientific revolution - Thomas Kuhn and the social structure of knowledge|Kuhn]] showed that in normal times researchers treat anomalies not as refutations but as puzzles. Popper thought that a danger and called the normal scientist a person one ought to be sorry for. In 1965 the two clashed at a conference in London. Imre Lakatos then tried to rescue Popper's norm by judging not single theories but research programmes over a longer time.
- **What do you achieve, then?** Popper wanted to say that successive theories come ever closer to the truth. His formal definition of this proved untenable in 1974. Critics also note that anyone who relies on a well-tested theory to build a bridge is doing something inductive after all.
- **Probability and existence claims.** "There exists a particle with these properties" cannot be falsified but can be confirmed. Statistical predictions strictly exclude no outcome at all. In both cases additional conventions are needed.

A well-known misstep illustrates the difficulty. Popper once called the theory of evolution a metaphysical research programme and not a testable theory. In 1978 he took that back.

**Knowledge and society.**

Popper extended his theory of knowledge to politics. If nobody owns the truth and progress comes from finding errors, then the best society is one that permits criticism and can replace its leaders without violence. In *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), written in exile in New Zealand, he attacked Plato, Hegel and Marx as thinkers who ascribed a fixed course to history. Against this he set step-by-step reform whose consequences you can test and reverse. He called his attitude **critical rationalism**: I may be wrong, you may be right, and with effort we may together get nearer to the truth.

## Why this matters
Popper's criterion is the philosophy of science that scientists themselves use. In the debate over string theory, the multiverse and the [[Interpretations of quantum mechanics - what they share where they differ and what is testable|interpretations of quantum mechanics]], "which measurement would refute this?" is still the first question, and judges have used the criterion to separate science from pseudoscience. For the note [[Valid versus useful - why an irrefutable argument need not explain anything|Valid versus useful]] Popper supplies the core: irrefutability is not a strength but a defect. At the same time his work shows where a logical standard ends. Whether a theory has been refuted is not decided by a formula. It is decided by a community that agrees when a rescue is still honest. In that way Popper, against his will, points forward to Kuhn and to the question of who has the authority to judge knowledge. Together the two form a tension that has not been resolved: Popper says how science ought to work, Kuhn how it does work, and a good philosophy of science has to do justice to both.

## Open questions
- If falsification rests on agreements about what counts as an honest rescue, is it still a criterion or a description of good manners?
- Conspiracy thinking is often irrefutable because every piece of counter-evidence counts as part of the conspiracy. Is that exactly what Popper meant, or is more going on here than a logical defect?
- May a theory that is not testable now but could become so, as atomism long was, be called science? And who decides how long "one day" may last?

- Karl Popper, *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1959, German original 1934) — the main work, more technical
- Karl Popper, *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945)

- Peter Godfrey-Smith, *Theory and Reality* (2003) — balanced survey of positivism, Popper, Kuhn and after
