# What gravitational waves reveal: black hole mergers and the wandering giants

## Core
Since 2015, LIGO has detected vibrations in spacetime itself — gravitational waves from merging black holes billions of light-years away. In ten years, hundreds of mergers have been registered, and these observations reveal not only that mergers occur regularly, but also something unexpected: the gravitational waves themselves are asymmetric enough to give the resulting black hole a kick. Some mergers produce so-called superkicks that fling the post-merger hole up to 5,000 km/s away — enough to escape its own galaxy and travel as a wandering giant through the intergalactic medium.

## Explanation
Until 2015, the detection of gravitational waves was a century-old ambition without result. Einstein predicted them in 1916, but the signals are so weak that they remained beyond technical reach until that point. On 14 September 2015, the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) detected for the first time a vibration in spacetime that could be attributed to a merger of two black holes of 36 and 29 solar masses, 1.3 billion light-years away. Since then, LIGO and the European Virgo detector together have confirmed more than 100 mergers, and the detection rate rises with each observation run.

**Why we detect so many despite cosmic rarity.** Per individual galaxy, a black hole merger is fairly rare — estimated at about one per hundred thousand years. But gravitational waves decline with distance (1/r) rather than with distance squared like light. As a result LIGO can detect mergers up to billions of light-years away, and in that volume sit about 100 billion galaxies. A merger per 100,000 years per galaxy, multiplied by that volume, yields about a million mergers per year within LIGO's range. We pick up only a fraction because many signals fade below the noise, but the volume explains why we get dozens of detections per year despite the local rarity.

**Where mergers happen.** Three mechanisms are likely responsible. In isolated binary evolution, two massive stars form a binary pair, evolve together, and end after their supernovae as a black hole pair that merges after hundreds of millions of years. This can happen anywhere in a galaxy — in spiral arms, in the disk, in the halo. In dynamical formation in dense stellar environments, chaotic interactions take place in globular clusters where black holes can capture each other via three-body encounters. And in active galactic nuclei, accretion disks around supermassive holes may act as accelerated matchmaking media for smaller holes. Which route dominates has not yet been decided — population statistics from future observation runs will help establish this.

**What we mostly see so far.** Nearly all LIGO detections concern stellar-mass black hole mergers — typically 10 to 50 solar masses per object. An important surprise was GW190521 in 2019, a merger of two holes of 85 and 66 solar masses. Those masses fall in a forbidden range — too heavy for ordinary stellar formation, too light for formation from supermassive holes — suggesting we are dealing with intermediate-mass holes themselves formed from earlier mergers.

**Supermassive-hole mergers and pulsar timing arrays.** LIGO is insensitive to mergers of million-solar-mass holes — their gravitational waves have frequencies of millihertz or lower, too slow for terrestrial detectors. For this category we need other instruments. In 2023, NANOGrav and other consortia published the first evidence for a background signal of nanohertz gravitational waves, detected via extremely precise timing of pulsars distributed across the sky. This signal is interpreted as the cumulative noise of thousands of supermassive-hole mergers across cosmic history — no individual detections, but evidence that such mergers occur frequently. The space-based detector LISA, planned for 2035, will be able to catch individual supermassive mergers.

**The recoil kick and its extremes.** Here is where it becomes physically staggering. When two black holes merge and the merger proceeds asymmetrically — through different spins, unequal masses, or non-aligned spin planes — the emitted gravitational waves are asymmetric. Just as with rockets, asymmetric emission produces recoil. Calculations show that under favourable conditions, these kicks can reach up to 5,000 km/s. For such a "superkick" specific conditions are needed: roughly equal mass ratio, both holes spinning rapidly, and spin planes precisely in the orbital plane. Statistically these conditions all come together in only a few percent of all mergers — but over cosmic timescales that generates a considerable population of displaced post-merger holes.

**Wandering giants in and outside galaxies.** Since the escape velocity of a typical galaxy is 500 to 1,000 km/s, a large recoil kick can shoot a supermassive black hole out of its host. Off-center supermassive holes have already been observed in various galaxies — NGC 3115, M84, and the well-known case 3C 186 with an offset of about 35,000 light-years and a speed of 2,000 km/s. In 2023, a team led by Pieter van Dokkum published a remarkable observation with Hubble: a long streak of star formation about 200,000 light-years long, possibly a trail left by a runaway supermassive hole moving at more than 1,500 km/s through intergalactic space. If this interpretation is correct, it is the most extreme runaway detection so far.

**The invisible population.** If these scenarios hold, the intergalactic medium is populated with invisible giants — supermassive black holes without a host galaxy, wandering through the emptiness between galaxies. Nothing feeding them, no stars to attract. Estimates vary widely, with numbers between 100 million and several billion such wandering holes in the observable universe. Most are now completely silent — no accretion, no emission, only gravity. They roam as survivors of galactic violence histories billions of years past. For dark matter they could contribute a small but real share, though they cannot solve the mystery.

**Why we still find so few.** Three reasons. A runaway supermassive hole usually no longer has an accretion disk and is therefore practically invisible, unless it happens to cross a gas environment or pulls stars with it that give it away. Statistically, extreme superkicks are rare — only a few percent of mergers. And our observations usually search in galactic nuclei, not in halos or intergalactic medium. We see what we look for, and this category fell outside the usual search strategies. Surveys such as JWST and the Vera Rubin Observatory (operating since 2025) will change that.

## Why this matters
Before 2015, every black hole merger was literally inaudible to humanity. We could know *that* black holes existed via their gravitational effects on nearby stars, but we could not "see" them interact. Gravitational waves have opened a totally new window onto physics that would otherwise remain hidden. Every detection tests general relativity in its most extreme regime — at velocities approaching the speed of light, at spacetime curvatures that are astronomically strong. And the bonus is an unexpected discovery: the birth burst of gravitational waves has strong physical consequences for the post-merger objects themselves. A merger is not just a coming together; it is a violent birth moment in which the new object can be flung away at enormous speed. This connects two scales that would otherwise seem separate — microscopic gravitational-wave emission and macroscopic galactic dynamics. What begins as quantum-relativistic asymmetry can end as a supermassive hole forever wandering through cosmic emptiness.

## Open questions
- What fraction of supermassive-hole mergers produces large recoil kicks, and how does that match population statistics of off-center and runaway black holes?
- Is the Van Dokkum streak really a runaway supermassive hole, or is an alternative explanation (edge-on dwarf galaxy, tidal-arm structure) more correct?
- How many wandering supermassive holes populate the intergalactic medium, and can we detect them via microlensing or via disturbances in galactic dynamics?
