# Kant and the Copernican revolution: how the mind shapes the world

## Core
Immanuel Kant (1724-1804) undertook the most ambitious synthesis attempt of modern philosophy. Faced with the impasse between rationalism (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz) and empiricism (Locke, Berkeley, Hume), he claimed that both parties were partially right. Knowledge requires both sensory input and thought structures that organise this input. Our mind is no passive receiver of impressions, but an active shaper: space, time and causality are not properties of the world in itself but **categories of the mind** that we impose on experience. This is Kant's "Copernican revolution": the mind does not arrange itself to the world, but the world as we know it arranges itself to the mind. Russell finds Kant's project grandiose and partly successful, but criticises central elements as untenable. At the same time he acknowledges Kant as probably the most influential philosopher since Aristotle.

## Explanation
Kant lived his entire life in Königsberg in East Prussia, where he studied, taught and conquered the continent intellectually without ever travelling. Russell describes him as the prototypical academic philosopher: a life of strict routine (the famous walks by which neighbours would set their clocks), without marriage, without dramatic events, but with enormous intellectual productivity. He published his masterpiece, *Critique of Pure Reason*, only at age fifty-seven in 1781, after decades of preparatory work.

What brought Kant to his project was the shock of Hume. Kant famously wrote that reading Hume had awakened him from his "dogmatic slumber". Hume had shown that empiricism leads to scepticism — that causality, the self and induction cannot be rationally justified from experience alone. Kant could not accept this, because it would make Newtonian physics — for him the pinnacle of human knowledge — fundamentally uncertain. But he could also not fall back on rationalism, because Hume had shown that innate ideas and pure reason also form no sufficient basis. A third way was needed.

**The Copernican revolution.** Kant's central insight is a radical inversion. Until then, philosophy had assumed that our knowledge must orient itself to objects — if our theories do not fit how the world is, we must adjust the theories. Kant proposes to view it the other way around: perhaps the objects must orient themselves to our cognitive faculties. We cannot perceive everything that is; we can only perceive what our cognitive equipment allows us to perceive. The structure of our experience is partly determined by the structure of our mind.

Russell notes that this was a startlingly new thought. It does not say that reality does not exist — Kant is no idealist in Berkeley's sense — but that reality as we know it is inseparably interwoven with how we know it. We cannot know the world as it is "in itself"; we can only know it as it appears to us.

**Space and time as forms of sensibility.** Kant's first major step is to reinterpret space and time. For Newton, space and time were absolute, objective quantities — a kind of container within which all events take place. For Leibniz they were relations between things. Kant offers a third option: space and time are **forms of sensibility**, ways our mind organises sensory impressions. Outside our consciousness there is no space or time as we experience it; within our consciousness we cannot but order all experience spatially and temporally.

This explains why mathematics — geometry about space, arithmetic about time — can offer *a priori* certainty. We do not know space and time from experience; experience is already structured in space and time by our mind. Therefore we can prove mathematical truths before we observe, and all future observations will have to satisfy those truths.

Russell values the elegance of this thought but points to a fundamental problem: scientific developments since Kant (especially non-Euclidean geometry and Einstein's theory of relativity) have shown that our intuitive space-time is no necessary structure of experience. We can formulate consistent theories in which space is curved and time is not absolute. Kant's claim that Euclidean space is the only conceivable space has been scientifically refuted.

**The twelve categories.** Besides space and time, Kant identifies twelve **categories of the understanding** that structure all experience: unity and plurality, cause and effect, possibility and necessity, and so on. When we perceive, we unconsciously impose these categories on the stream of sensory impressions, and thus a structured experience of objects in causal connections arises.

This is Kant's answer to Hume's scepticism about causality. Hume was right that we cannot derive causality from experience — but Hume was wrong in his conclusion. Causality is no perceivable fact in the world, but also no mere habit of thinking. Causality is an **a priori category** that we necessarily impose on every experience. Therefore causality is objective — not objective in the world in itself, but objectively valid for all possible experience.

Russell finds this ingenious but problematic. Why precisely these twelve categories? Kant derives them from the logic of his time, but modern logic has largely abandoned that syllogistic structure. Moreover, the derivation seems rather arbitrary — if Kant's categories are necessary, they should not be so dependent on a specific (and outdated) logic.

**The thing-in-itself.** A central and contested component of Kant's system is the distinction between the **phenomenal** (the world as it appears to us, structured by our space, time and categories) and the **noumenal** (the thing-in-itself, *Ding an sich*, reality as it is outside our experience). We can only know the phenomenal world; the thing-in-itself remains in principle unknowable.

Kant says the thing-in-itself exists because there must be something that causes our sensory impressions — there can be no appearance without something that appears. But what this something is, we cannot say. It is a limit-boundary of our knowing.

Russell finds this one of the weakest points in Kant's system. How can Kant claim that the thing-in-itself causes our impressions, when causality according to him itself only applies within the phenomenal world? Russell points out that Kant breaks his own rules here — he uses a category (cause) outside the domain where it is applicable according to him. This is a logical inconsistency that later idealists (Fichte, Hegel) would use to eliminate the thing-in-itself altogether — which led to speculative philosophy Russell strongly disapproves.

**Practical reason and the categorical imperative.** Kant's second major work, *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), is devoted to ethics. He argues that moral truth cannot be derived from empirical observation or consequences, but from the **categorical imperative**: act only according to that maxim which you can at the same time will to become a universal law. Another formulation: never treat people merely as means but always also as ends.

Russell respects the strictness of this ethics but finds its abstraction problematic. The categorical imperative offers too little grip in concrete situations — almost any action can be made universalisable with the right formulation. And the separation between duty and consequences sometimes leads to conclusions that clash with our moral intuitions (Kant's famous claim that even for a murderer asking where your friend is, one may not lie).

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell treats Kant with respect but little sympathy. He acknowledges that Kant undertook the greatest philosophical project of modern times and that his influence is enormous — both direct (the German idealist tradition of Fichte, Schelling, Hegel) and indirect (virtually all modern philosophy reacts in some way to Kant). At the same time Russell holds that many of Kant's specific conclusions are untenable: the necessary Euclidean structure of space, the twelve categories, the coherence of the thing-in-itself, the categorical imperative as ethical foundation. What remains is the Kantian insight that our cognitive faculties actively contribute to what we know — an insight that is fundamental and still works in modern philosophy of science.

Russell prefers in general the British empiricist tradition over the German idealist. He finds Kant too abstract, too systematic, too far removed from scientific practice. But he also acknowledges that Kant raised problems the empirical tradition has not been able to solve, and that the history of philosophy since Kant cannot be understood without him as anchor.

## Why this matters
Kant tries to achieve what no philosopher before him had attempted: a scientific description of the conditions of possible experience. Not what we actually know, but what we can know at all — and how. Whether his specific answers are tenable is another question. What remains is the method: do not only ask "is this true?" but "under what conditions could this be known?" That is a question not only philosophers but also modern scientists ask when they think about the limits of their own disciplines. Moreover, Kant's project poses a deep philosophical question that is still productive: can we really make a distinction between the world as it is and the world as it appears to us? Or does this distinction fall away as soon as we understand that we can never know the world other than through our own capacities? Russell would lean toward the latter, but acknowledges that the Kantian question is not easy to answer — and that is precisely why Kant is still read after 240 years.

## Open questions
- Does Kant's thesis that space and time are forms of sensibility hold, given that modern physics works with non-intuitive space-time structures? Some philosophers hold that the thesis can be referred to the structure of phenomenal experience without pretensions about physics; others see here a fundamental refutation.
- Is the thing-in-itself a coherent concept, or a self-defeating idea as Russell suggests? The question determines whether Kant's system as a whole is tenable or in fact must lead to a form of idealism.
- Does the categorical imperative work as ethical foundation, or does it lead to conclusions even Kant would not have wanted? Modern deontology is in important measure inspired by Kant but deviates on crucial points from his original formulation.
