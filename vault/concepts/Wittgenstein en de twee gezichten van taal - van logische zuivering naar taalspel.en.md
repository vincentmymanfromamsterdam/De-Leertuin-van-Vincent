# Wittgenstein and the two faces of language - from logical purification to language game

## Core
Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889-1951) is perhaps the only great philosopher to have developed within one lifetime two fundamentally different philosophies, both influential enough to initiate separate traditions. The early Wittgenstein of *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921) wanted to purify language into a logical mirror of reality and excise all metaphysics as meaningless babble — a stance that fed the logical positivism of the Vienna Circle. The late Wittgenstein of *Philosophical Investigations* (posthumous 1953) largely rejected this position and saw language as a collection of **language games** embedded in forms of life, where meaning arises from use rather than from logical depiction. Between both periods lies a self-criticism rare in philosophy — and which ironically illustrates exactly what he wanted to argue in both periods: that clarity arises only by daring to radically reconsider what you thought you knew. Russell, who had discovered Wittgenstein as a young man and saw him as his successor, became gradually alienated from his former protégé after the first period.

## Explanation
Wittgenstein was born in Vienna into one of the richest families of the Habsburg empire — his father Karl was a steel magnate who collected art and welcomed composers such as Brahms into his home. Four of Wittgenstein's five brothers committed suicide; he himself contemplated it repeatedly. He studied aeronautical engineering in Manchester and there became fascinated by the mathematical foundations of his subject. That brought him to Cambridge where he appeared at Bertrand Russell's door in 1911 — an unknown young man without philosophical training who within a year would become one of Russell's most important pupils. Russell later wrote that meeting Wittgenstein was one of the most exciting intellectual experiences of his life.

**The Tractatus.**

During the First World War, Wittgenstein served in the Austrian army — first on the eastern front, later in Italy, where he was taken prisoner. In his rucksack he carried the manuscript of what would later become *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*. The book was published in German in 1921, in English in 1922 with a foreword by Russell. It is a strange, hermetic work — only about 75 pages long, built up from numbered propositions (1, 1.1, 1.11, and so on) that explain or nuance each other in cascade.

The central thesis is that language works as **depiction** of facts in the world. A meaningful proposition is a kind of logical picture of a possible state of affairs. "The cat sits on the mat" is meaningful because it describes a specific configuration of objects that may or may not be the case. The value of a proposition lies in its **testability** — in which possible reality it would be true.

From this follows something far-reaching. Much of what philosophers have said through the centuries is **not even false**. It is literally **meaningless** — language behaving as if it were propositions but describing no possible state of affairs. "The Absolute Spirit realises itself through history", "Nothing nothings", "the Will to Power is the essence of existence" — for Wittgenstein these are no false statements but non-statements, grammatically correct but logically empty.

The [[Valid versus useful - why an irrefutable argument need not explain anything|kinship with Popper's falsifiability]] is direct, although Popper's *Logik der Forschung* (1934) appeared thirteen years later. Wittgenstein's criterion is in a sense stricter: non-testable statements are for Popper no science, for Wittgenstein no language.

**The famous closing line.**

At the end of the *Tractatus* comes proposition 7, famous in its terseness:

> *Wovon man nicht sprechen kann, darüber muss man schweigen.*
> Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.

This sounds like a cutting off of metaphysics and theology, and so it was also read by the Vienna Circle — as justification to ban all this "chatter" from philosophy in favour of strict scientific analysis. But Wittgenstein meant something subtler. For him, what lay at the boundaries of the sayable — ethics, aesthetics, religious experience, the mystical — was not smaller or less important than what we can say. On the contrary: it was precisely the **most important**. But one cannot speak meaningfully about it. One must show it through how one lives, how one works, how one dies.

Proposition 6.522 makes this explicit: "*Es gibt allerdings Unaussprechliches. Dies zeigt sich, es ist das Mystische.*" There is indeed the unutterable; this shows itself; it is the mystical. This Schopenhauer-like element — Schopenhauer was Wittgenstein's favourite philosopher in his youth — gave the *Tractatus* a layer that was often ignored by its positivist admirers.

**The ladder paradox.**

The most startling moment of the *Tractatus* comes in proposition 6.54, just before the closing line. Wittgenstein acknowledges that his own book is, according to his own criterion, **nonsense**. The propositions of the *Tractatus* make statements about the limits of the sayable, but such meta-statements cannot, according to the theory of the book itself, be meaningful propositions — they describe no possible state of affairs in the world.

Wittgenstein's solution is the **ladder metaphor**. His reader must use the propositions of the book as a ladder to climb higher, and then **throw away the ladder**. Whoever has truly understood the *Tractatus* sees that the text itself has been useful nonsense — nonsense because it violates its own rules, useful because it has brought the reader to a point where these rules become clear.

Russell found this aspect of the *Tractatus* always uncomfortable. In his foreword he expressed cautious admiration but also indicated that he did not believe a text can meaningfully communicate that communication beyond certain boundaries is impossible. Wittgenstein read the foreword and was so irritated that he accused Russell of not having understood his book. Their relationship, once warm, gradually cooled.

**The self-imposed silence.**

Wittgenstein believed after writing the *Tractatus* that he had solved all philosophical problems — or more precisely, that he had shown why they were no real problems. He gave up philosophy. He became a village schoolteacher in a remote Austrian mountain area, then worked as a gardener in a monastery, designed a house for his sister in Vienna (a minimalist architectural work that still stands), and stayed away from Cambridge and philosophy for nearly a decade.

In this period, however, he gradually came to the insight that the *Tractatus* had not solved everything. The occasion was partly a conversation in 1928 with Frank Ramsey and Piero Sraffa — Sraffa made an Italian gesture of contempt and asked Wittgenstein: "What is the logical form of this?" The question was rhetorical but for Wittgenstein paralysing. Not all meaningful-human communication could be captured in the structure "depiction of a possible state of affairs". There was something more fundamental to human language use that he had missed.

**The late Wittgenstein.**

In 1929 Wittgenstein returned to Cambridge and began the work that would posthumously appear as *Philosophical Investigations* (1953). It is a radically different book from the *Tractatus* — no systematic structure but numbered fragments, dialogues with himself, thinking out loud instead of presenting conclusions. And the content is largely a **rejection** of his earlier work.

The central thought: meaning arises not through logical depiction of facts, but through **use in a language game** embedded in a **form of life** (*Lebensform*). A word does not mean what it abstractly designates, but what it does in concrete contexts. "Pain", "red", "game", "understanding" — all these words receive their meaning not from definitions but from how they function in human practices: care for the sick, description of colours, child play, instruction.

Wittgenstein illustrates this with countless examples, sometimes endlessly elaborated. What is a "game"? Try to define it. Football has rules, chess has rules, but what are the rules of playing with a dollhouse? Football has winners, chess has winners, but who wins at hide-and-seek? With every characteristic you propose as essential for "game", you can find examples where the characteristic is absent but we still speak of a game. Wittgenstein concludes that "game" has no sharp definition but is a **family resemblance** — overlapping characteristics without common core, like the resemblances between family members without one feature applying to all.

This is a deep reversal compared to the *Tractatus*. There, language was logically precise and related to a fixed reality. Here, language is socially pluriform, contextual, and behaviourally anchored. There, the philosopher was someone who had to expose the logical structure of meaningful speech. Here, the philosopher is someone who must **describe ordinary language without wanting to rectify it** — because the illusion that language can be more ideal than it actually is, is according to the late Wittgenstein itself the source of most philosophical confusion.

**Broom and broomstick.**

An illustration of his method: a famous passage from the *Investigations* asks what it is to understand a word. Someone points at a broom and says "broom". How does the learner know that the word refers to the whole object, and not to the broomstick, or the broom head, or the colour of the wood, or the direction in which the broom points? For the *Tractatus*-Wittgenstein there should be somewhere a logical analysis that fixes the correct depiction. For the late Wittgenstein the answer is simpler and more radical: the learner understands the word because he has grown up in a community where brooms are used for sweeping. Understanding follows from participation in a form of life, not from logical depiction.

**Private language argument.**

Another famous argument from the *Investigations* tries to show that no truly **private language** can exist — a language in which only I know what the words mean, based on my inner experiences. Wittgenstein asks: how would I know that I use the same word today for the same inner experience as yesterday? Without external control — without a community that can correct my use — there is no difference between "using the right word" and "thinking I am using the right word". Language is intrinsically social; meaning requires correction-possibility; and correction requires others.

This argument is fundamental to the modern philosophy of mind. It undermines the Cartesian picture in which the thinking self can look into its own experiences and label them with private names. According to the late Wittgenstein, my access to my own pain, my own colour experience, my own understanding is not private but mediated by publicly available language.

**Russell's reaction.**

Russell has been strikingly critical of the late Wittgenstein. In the 1950s he wrote that the *Philosophical Investigations* disappointed him — he found the turn away from logic and scientific method a regression compared to the clear ambitions of the *Tractatus*. For Russell, who had invested his entire life in logical analysis, the turn to ordinary-language philosophy felt like a flight from the strict methodology of modern philosophy to something that looked suspiciously like anthropology.

The irony is that many philosophers today think Wittgenstein saw important truths in both periods — and that his development is precisely a sign of intellectual strength, not of inconsistency. The *Tractatus* was a brilliant elaboration of a specific position that ultimately proved untenable; the *Investigations* are an equally brilliant reaction that is partly right but partly overshoots.

**Wittgenstein's own judgement.**

Wittgenstein himself was strict about his early work. In the preface of the *Investigations* he wrote that he wanted to publish his two works alongside each other because his new thoughts were only understandable as reaction to his old. He did not see the *Tractatus* as wrong in the sense of false — the mistakes were deeper, in the assumptions about what language and meaning are. He had demanded too much that language be more precise than it is, and thereby made the ordinary-language philosophy of his later years necessary as correction.

He died in 1951 of cancer, loved by a small circle of pupils, infinitely influential but also deeply lonely. His last words were: "Tell them I have had a wonderful life." For those who know his biography — the suicidal brothers, the self-imposed poverty despite his fortune, the years of self-criticism, the strained loves — that is a remarkable statement. He meant it.

## Why this matters
Wittgenstein is interesting not only for what he thought but for **how** he thought. He is one of the rare philosophers who publicly revised his own work, not by correcting details but by rejecting fundamental assumptions. Most philosophers defend their youth works their entire lives; Wittgenstein used his youth work as a stepping stone and then stepped off. That is a form of intellectual integrity that calls for admiration, even from those who do not share his specific conclusions. Furthermore, his work offers two valuable tools for those who want to do philosophy. The early Wittgenstein sharpens our sensitivity to pseudo-statements — language that presents itself as description of reality but makes no testable claim. The late Wittgenstein sharpens our sensitivity to the **seduction of precision** — to our tendency to make language more precise than it needs to be, and to create philosophical problems by imposing this fantasised precision on ordinary language. Both tools also work against each other. Whoever goes too far with the early Wittgenstein declares almost all human speech meaningless. Whoever goes too far with the late Wittgenstein becomes uncritical about how ordinary language can actually be confused. The right philosophical posture is perhaps to keep both tools at hand and to choose per situation which fits. Russell would not have approved of this — he wanted one strict method — but Wittgenstein's example shows that methodological pluralism perhaps stands closer to the reality of philosophical work than strict unification.

## Open questions
- Which of the two Wittgensteins is ultimately more right? Analytic philosophy of the second half of the twentieth century has inherited elements from both, and the debate is not definitively settled. The early Wittgenstein lives on in logical-positivist and philosophy-of-science traditions; the late in ordinary-language philosophy, phenomenology, and pragmatism.
- Was Wittgenstein's turn really a break, or is there a deeper continuity between both periods — for example the persistent refusal to let philosophy become speculative metaphysics? Some interpreters see more unity than Wittgenstein himself acknowledged.
- How does the private-language argument relate to modern developments in cognitive science, in which inner representations are treated as real? Is a reconciliation possible, or is this an area where Wittgenstein remains sharply challenging?

## Further reading
- Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921) — short but hermetic, starting with Russell's introduction helps
- Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* (posthumous 1953) — more accessible in tone than the *Tractatus* but fragmentary in structure
- Ray Monk, *Ludwig Wittgenstein: The Duty of Genius* (1990) — extensive biography linking life and thinking, one of the best philosophical biographies ever written
- A.C. Grayling, *Wittgenstein: A Very Short Introduction* (2001) — concise clear introduction to both periods
- Bertrand Russell, *My Philosophical Development* (1959) — Russell's own retrospect on his relationship with Wittgenstein and what he thought of his work
