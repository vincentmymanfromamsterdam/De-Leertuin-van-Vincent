# White holes - mathematical possibility and quantum gravity relevance

## Core
A **white hole** is a region of spacetime whose event horizon allows **outgoing** matter but no **incoming** matter — precisely the time reversal of an ordinary black hole. They are not speculative modifications of general relativity but **direct consequences** of Einstein's field equations. When Martin Kruskal and George Szekeres independently worked out the maximally extended Schwarzschild spacetime in 1960, a **four-region structure** automatically emerged: our ordinary universe, the black hole interior, a second universe connected via an Einstein-Rosen bridge, and the interior of a white hole. Mathematically white holes are thereby as legitimate as black holes. Physically they are more problematic: their initial conditions are unnatural, they conflict with the second law of thermodynamics, they are unstable under classical perturbations, and we have never observed one astronomically. For decades they were therefore treated as mathematical curiosities without plausible physical realisation. Since about 2014 **Carlo Rovelli** with collaborators (Haggard, Vidotto, Bianchi, Christodoulou, D'Ambrosio and others) has developed a programme in which white holes acquire a central role — not as classical spacetime objects but as end-phases of quantum mechanical black hole evaporation. In this scenario an evaporating black hole tunnels via a **Planck star** to a white hole, which resolves the notorious information paradox. The remaining **Planck-scale white hole remnants** are stable on cosmological timescales and have recently (Rovelli and Vidotto 2018, 2024) been proposed as serious candidate contribution to dark matter. Recent spinfoam calculations (2024-2025) provide explicit quantitative support for this scenario. Whether it is correct for our actual universe remains empirically open, but white holes have been transformed from mathematical curiosity into serious scientific hypothesis in one of the most fundamental open questions of modern physics.

## Explanation
White holes have a strange history. They appeared unannounced in the mathematics of general relativity, were for decades seen as mathematical curiosities without physical meaning, and are now — via an unexpected detour through quantum gravity — returning as serious candidates for fundamental physical phenomena. This history illustrates how science sometimes works: what was initially considered valid-but-not-useful can later gain empirical traction when new theoretical frameworks place it in another context.

**The mathematical foundation: Kruskal-Szekeres.**

Karl Schwarzschild published in 1916, in the middle of the First World War, the first exact solution of Einstein's field equations. His solution describes the spacetime around a spherically symmetric mass in vacuum. For nearly fifty years this solution was mainly considered for the region **outside** what we now call the event horizon — the domain where stars and planets are found and where ordinary gravitational physics applies.

In 1960 Martin Kruskal and George Szekeres independently worked out the **maximally extended** Schwarzschild solution. This means: the complete mathematical domain in which the solution is defined, including the regions beyond the event horizon and behind the singularity. What emerged was stunning.

The maximal extension of Schwarzschild contains **four distinct regions**, not one as had been tacitly assumed for decades:

**Region I** is our ordinary universe — the outer region where stars, planets and observers are found.

**Region II** is the interior of an ordinary black hole — the region within the event horizon from which nothing can escape. Everything that ends up here is inevitably pulled to the central singularity.

**Region III** is a **second universe** mirror-image of Region I. It is connected to Region I via an Einstein-Rosen bridge — a so-called wormhole connecting the two universes through the common horizon structure.

**Region IV** is the interior of a **white hole** — the time reversal of Region II. Where Region II swallows everything, Region IV spews everything outward. Matter in Region IV can (and must) move to Region I or Region III, but cannot return to it.

This structure is mathematically compelling. If you accept the Schwarzschild metric and extend it to its full domain — which you would naturally want in any complete description — then you necessarily get these four regions. No extra assumption is needed, no adjustment of Einstein's equations, no exotic matter. White holes are as valid solutions of general relativity as black holes.

**What a white hole precisely is.**

Think of the time reversal of a black hole, literally. A black hole has an event horizon that allows **incoming** matter but no **outgoing** matter — what falls in does not come out. A white hole has an event horizon that does precisely the opposite: allows **outgoing** matter but no **incoming** matter. What is in it spews itself outward; what tries to enter from outside cannot.

In the spacetime diagrams physicists use (Kruskal diagrams or Penrose diagrams), black and white hole are perfectly symmetric. The black hole horizon tilts one way in the diagram; the white hole horizon tilts the other way. They are mirror images of each other in the time direction.

Important to understand: a white hole is **not** the same as a big-bang-like explosion or an ordinary source of matter. It is a specific kind of spacetime structure with a horizon that only lets matter out and not back in. For an external observer a white hole would appear as an object spontaneously ejecting matter from a region where previously nothing was visible — as a reverse film of gravitational collapse in which matter escapes from a compact object instead of falling into it.

**The physical objections.**

While white holes are mathematically perfectly legitimate, they stand in tension with various physical principles. For decades these objections were seen as sufficient reason to consider white holes unrealistic.

**First objection: unnatural initial conditions.** For an ordinary black hole we can imagine how it arises: a massive star runs out of fuel, collapses under its own gravity, and forms an event horizon. This is a natural astrophysical process. For a white hole there is no analogous origin mechanism in classical general relativity. The maximally extended Schwarzschild solution contains the white hole as "eternally present" — it always was. To have a white hole you must simply **postulate** that it is there with specific initial conditions — a hugely specialised configuration that does not just follow from ordinary star formation.

**Second objection: conflict with thermodynamics.** Black holes have entropy via the Bekenstein-Hawking formula ($S = A/4\ell_P^2$), as we discussed in the [[The holographic principle - information as building block of spacetime|holography note]]. When matter falls into a black hole, the entropy of the black hole increases, which is consistent with the second law of thermodynamics. A white hole as time reversal would do the opposite — entropy would decrease when it spews out matter. This conflicts with the universal tendency of entropy growth that defines the arrow of time. Classical white holes seem to conflict with one of the most robust principles of physics.

**Third objection: instability.** Even if a white hole somehow arose, calculations show it is unstable under classical perturbations. Matter or radiation trying to approach it would undergo infinite blueshift — the emitted material meets incoming radiation with exponentially increasing energy. In classical calculations a white hole would almost immediately be destroyed or transform itself into something else (for example back into a black hole).

**Fourth objection: astronomical absence.** If white holes existed with astrophysical masses (solar mass or greater), their explosive ejections should in principle be observable. Astronomers have never found anything matching this description. This virtually rules out large white holes in our observable universe.

These four objections together made white holes in classical general relativity be treated for decades as a curious mathematical possibility without plausible physical realisation — a valid but unlikely scenario that was not seriously considered as part of our actual physics.

**The quantum gravity turn.**

From about 2014 the picture changes radically. What was previously a mathematical curiosity becomes a central component of a specific research programme within loop quantum gravity. The architects of this programme are mainly Carlo Rovelli and his collaborators.

It began with a paper by Hal Haggard and Rovelli in 2015: "Quantum-gravity effects outside the horizon spark black to white hole tunneling", published in *Physical Review D*. Their proposal is elegant. When a black hole via Hawking radiation shrinks sufficiently, quantum gravity effects become dominant at its centre. Classically the black hole would keep shrinking until it collapses at its singularity. But quantum gravity — in the form of loop quantum gravity — prevents the singularity through discrete geometric structure at the Planck scale. What remains is not a singularity but a compact object with density of order the Planck density, supported by quantum gravity effects. This object is called a **Planck star** by Rovelli and Vidotto (proposed in 2014).

The Planck star can subsequently transform into a white hole via quantum tunneling. This is analogous to how an alpha particle tunnels through a classically forbidden potential barrier in atomic physics — a process classically impossible but quantum mechanically real. In the case of a Planck star the tunneling would carry over to the other side of what would otherwise be the singularity, where it re-emerges as a white hole. The time-symmetric spacetime is reached again, but now with a white hole at the end of evaporation.

**White holes as remnants.**

In 2018 Bianchi, Christodoulou, D'Ambrosio, Haggard and Rovelli published an influential paper in *Classical and Quantum Gravity* titled "White holes as remnants: a surprising scenario for the end of a black hole". The idea: at the end of Hawking evaporation of a black hole no singularity remains, and also no total destruction, but a long-lived **white hole remnant** with Planck-scale mass (about $10^{-8}$ kg, the Planck mass).

These remnants would live extraordinarily long — timescales comparable to or much longer than the age of the universe — because their escape rate of mass is astronomically small at such small size. In fact they are almost stable on cosmological timescales. More importantly: they would contain all information that fell into the original black hole, thereby resolving the notorious [[The end of a black hole - why evaporation need not be a mystery|information paradox]] without information having to escape via Hawking radiation.

This is philosophically elegant. Where the traditional information paradox has plagued theoretical physics for nearly half a century (Hawking's original paper is from 1976), the white hole remnant scenario offers a specific way out. Information does not disappear, is not smuggled out via subtle correlations in Hawking radiation (as in the holographic replica wormhole solution), but simply remains locked in the long-lived remnant.

**White holes as dark matter.**

This brings us to a surprising recent development. In 2018 Rovelli and Vidotto published "Small black/white hole stability and dark matter" in *Universe*, in which they speculated that these Planck-scale white hole remnants could make a significant contribution to dark matter. This was reworked in 2024 by Rovelli and Vidotto in an extensive review article ("Planck stars, White Holes, Remnants and Planck-mass quasi-particles"), where they present the scenario as serious candidate.

The scenario works roughly as follows. In the early universe, during inflation or just after, **primordial black holes** of various masses arose. Small primordial black holes — with masses below about $10^{12}$ kg — should by now have completely evaporated via Hawking radiation. But according to the black-to-white tunneling scenario a white hole remnant with Planck-scale mass remains at the end. If enough such primordial black holes existed in the early universe, then billions of Planck-scale white hole remnants roam through the universe now. They would virtually not interact with ordinary matter (too small for significant gravitational interaction at short distances, no electromagnetic charge), but their collective mass would be detectable as [[Hot warm or cold - the search for what dark matter actually is|dark matter]].

Important: this is a **speculative** proposal, not a confirmed model. Other dark matter candidates — WIMPs, axions, sterile neutrinos — each have their own empirical support and limitations. The white hole remnant scenario is one of several hypotheses in an active experimental research domain. But it is striking that an idea long seen as exotic mathematical possibility now plays a serious role in one of the most fundamental open questions of modern cosmology.

**Recent technical work.**

What makes the Rovelli programme stronger than pure speculation is that explicit calculations in spinfoam models of loop quantum gravity show that the black-to-white tunneling can indeed be formulated quantitatively consistent. Multiple recent papers work this out. Han, Qu and Zhang published in 2024 a calculation of the spinfoam amplitude of the black-to-white hole transition. Christodoulou, D'Ambrosio and colleagues have quantified the characteristic timescales of the geometry transition. Donà, Haggard, Rovelli, Sreeram and Taddei published in July 2025 "Spinfoam tunneling of quantum geometries in angle variables" — further technical refinement.

There are also alternative approaches within quantum gravity. Belfaqih, Bojowald, Brahma and Duque published in October 2025 in *Physical Review Letters* a paper on "Hawking evaporation and the fate of black holes in loop quantum gravity", which works out the scenario in a slightly different formulation. Various approaches converge on similar qualitative conclusions — white holes or their remnants could play a role in the end-phase of black hole evaporation.

This does not mean the scenario is confirmed. Loop quantum gravity itself is not yet an empirically verified theory; it competes with string theory, causal sets, asymptotic safety and other programmes discussed in the [[Quantum gravity and its various programmes - the search for unified physics|quantum gravity note]]. But it does show that the white hole scenario can be worked out mathematically precisely and produces concrete predictions, which distinguishes it from pure speculation.

## Why this matters
White holes illustrate a fascinating pattern in the history of physics: how apparently purely mathematical possibilities sometimes acquire empirical traction decades later when new theoretical frameworks place them in other context. When Kruskal and Szekeres worked out the maximally extended Schwarzschild solution in 1960, there was no reason to consider white holes serious physical phenomena. They were mathematically valid but physically unnatural. Six decades later they stand central in one of the most active research programmes in quantum gravity and are serious candidates for dark matter. This development strengthens an observation we made in the [[Valid versus useful - why an irrefutable argument need not explain anything|valid-versus-useful]] note: the status of "valid but not useful" is not permanent. New theoretical developments can transform ideas that initially seemed unusable into productive research hypotheses. White holes are a textbook example. Philosophically there is another point. White holes explicitly confront us with the question what it means for a physical phenomenon to be "possible". The classical objections — thermodynamics, initial conditions, instability — were not trivial; they were serious physical objections against white holes as realistic phenomena. The quantum gravity turn has not refuted these objections but transformed them: in the context of quantum mechanical tunneling "unnatural initial conditions" are less problematic, and Planck-scale remnants evade the classical thermodynamics and instability objections because they operate in a regime where classical physics no longer applies anyway. This shows that what is "possible" in physics depends not only on the mathematics but also on which physical framework we adopt — and that extending that framework can open new possibilities. For the broader Leertuin context this is a beautiful example of how quantum gravity research works in practice. It is not only abstract-theoretical work on the unification of general relativity and quantum field theory; it produces concrete predictions about observable phenomena (dark matter, black hole evaporation end-states, possibly gravitational wave signatures of tunneling processes). Whether these predictions will be empirically confirmed will have to be shown in the coming decades. But that we can make them at all is a sign of maturation of the field — from valid mathematical possibilities to testable physical hypotheses.

## Open questions
- Are Planck-scale white hole remnants really a significant part of dark matter? The scenario is theoretically attractive but empirical testing requires detection or exclusion of their collective gravitational effects, which is technically extraordinarily challenging given their extremely small individual size. Proposals for detection via cosmological signatures, gravitational lensing effects, and subtle deviations in structure formation are in development.
- If the black-to-white hole tunneling scenario is correct, what precisely happens during the tunneling phase? The quantum gravity calculations yield consistent amplitudes but the intuitive interpretation of what happens in the Planck star remains difficult. Classical spacetime language breaks down at the Planck scale, and it is unclear which natural language replaces it.
- What is the relation between the white hole solution of the information paradox (information in remnants) and the holographic solution (information via replica wormholes in Hawking radiation)? Both claim to solve the problem, but via structurally different mechanisms. Whether they are compatible, complementary, or competing is an active theoretical question.
