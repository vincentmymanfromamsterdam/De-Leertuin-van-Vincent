# Spinoza and the one substance: God or nature

## Core
Baruch Spinoza (1632-1677) built a philosophical system so radical that his contemporaries rejected him as atheist and dangerous. For him there existed only one substance — God or nature, *Deus sive Natura* — of which everything we perceive are modifications. No separation between mind and matter, between God and world, between human and universe. His *Ethics* tried via geometric method to prove how a human can live under this awareness and so achieve real freedom and blessedness. Russell has an exceptional admiration for Spinoza, both for the philosophical depth and for the personal character — he describes him as "the noblest and most lovable of the great philosophers". At the same time he points out that the system as a whole is not tenable and that the geometric method suggests certainty where interpretation is needed.

## Explanation
Spinoza was born in Amsterdam as son of Portuguese Sephardic Jews who had fled the Iberian peninsula from the inquisition. At twenty-three he was excommunicated by the Amsterdam Jewish community — a dramatic *cherem* with extraordinary curses — presumably because of his unorthodox philosophical views. He earned his bread by grinding lenses for microscopes and telescopes, refused university appointments to preserve his intellectual freedom, and lived modestly in The Hague until his early death at age 44, probably from lung disease from his glass-grinding work.

Russell describes Spinoza's character with remarkable warmth. Spinoza refused a professorship in Heidelberg out of fear of compromise. He refused a substantial pension offered by the French king because he did not want to dedicate his books. He was generous where he could, polite in correspondence even with hostile critics, and kept inner peace under persecution. For Russell, who himself had little sympathy for religion in general, Spinoza was the rare example of a thinker whose philosophy and life were completely one.

**The one substance.** Spinoza's central thesis, developed in his *Ethics* (posthumously published 1677), turns against Descartes' dualism. Descartes had argued that there exist two fundamentally different substances: mind and matter. Spinoza found this incoherent. A substance is by definition something that exists independently of anything else. If mind and matter were truly independent, how then could they interact with each other? The mind-body problem Descartes never solved was for Spinoza not a problem but a sign that the dualistic premise was wrong.

His alternative was monism. There exists only one substance — infinite, eternal, self-existing. This substance he called **God or nature** (*Deus sive Natura*), with the "or" not as alternative but as equation. Everything that exists is a mode or modification of this one substance. Stones, humans, stars, thoughts — all are appearances of one underlying reality.

This sounded like atheism to his contemporaries. A God identical to nature is no God who stands above or outside the world, no person we can worship or who knows us individually. He does not punish and does not reward. He has no goals or intentions. He simply is — the total reality, considered as one whole.

But for Spinoza this was anything but atheism. It was rather a more radical theism: nothing is truly separated from God, because everything is God. If you touch a stone, you touch a mode of the divine substance. If you think, you think a thought that is not only your thought but part of the thinking of the universe. Russell calls this "pantheism of the most consistent kind" and notes that Einstein and other scientists in later centuries still drew inspiration from it, precisely because it offers a god-concept reconcilable with scientific knowledge.

**Mind and body as attributes.** Spinoza solved the mind-body problem by understanding mind and body as **two attributes of the same substance**. What we see from outside as a body, we experience from inside as a mind. They are not two separate things that mysteriously interact, but two perspectives on one reality. Just as a coin has a front and a back that do not "interact" but are simply two aspects of the same object, humans have a mental and a physical aspect that proceed in parallel without causal connection between them.

Russell points out how elegant this solution is — it bypasses the entire mind-body problem without simply denying one side. But he also notes that the system is hard to test. How do we know that mind and body run in parallel? It seems rather a metaphysical postulate than an empirical finding.

**The geometric method.** Spinoza wrote the *Ethics* in the style of Euclid's *Elements*: definitions, axioms, propositions, demonstrations. He thought philosophical truth could be proved as certainly as mathematical truth. Russell finds this a mistake. The method suggests a certainty that the content cannot deliver. Philosophical premises are never as clear as mathematical axioms, and when they are presented as such, they hide the places where interpretation and assumption enter. Russell argues that many of Spinoza's claims are not really proved, only explained through the geometric form — as if presentation solves the problem of justification.

**Freedom as understanding of necessity.** Spinoza's ethics flows from his metaphysics. If everything is mode of one substance, everything is **necessary** — it could not be otherwise than it is. Free will in the traditional sense (being able to choose between alternatives) does not exist. But this is no reason for fatalism. Real freedom is for Spinoza the **understanding of necessity**. Not being free from causes, but free from ignorance about causes. Not liberated from emotions, but able to understand them and thereby transcend them.

Russell sees here similarities with the Stoicism that may have influenced his early philosophy, but also something original. Spinoza's *amor Dei intellectualis* — the intellectual love of God — is no passive surrender but an active understanding through which you recognise yourself as part of the eternal order. Whoever achieves this is liberated from unnecessary fear, anger and sorrow, not by suppressing them but by seeing them in a larger perspective.

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell concludes his treatment with exceptional praise: "Spinoza's philosophy as a whole cannot be accepted, but contains so many deep insights that no philosopher can ignore him. His moral authority is greater than his intellectual authority." For Russell, Spinoza is not so much someone with the right answers, as someone whose life and thinking together demonstrate something rarely achieved: how philosophy can lead to a form of rest and serene acceptance without self-deception. This makes Spinoza not only historically interesting but personally inspiring, even for those who cannot endorse his metaphysics.

## Why this matters
Spinoza offers an alternative to the most common ways of thinking about God, human and world. Not a personal God above nature, not a godless universe, but nature itself as the most fundamental reality we can know. Not free will against determinism, but freedom *through* understanding of determinism. Not mind against matter, but two perspectives on one reality. These alternatives have remarkably regained influence in modern times — Einstein called himself "Spinozistic" with regard to religion, and ecological thinkers have found in Spinoza an early philosopher of nature as a whole. But Spinoza's lasting significance perhaps lies more in what he lived than in what he argued. He showed that philosophy can be more than intellectual exercise — that it can become a way of life that provides steadfastness in difficult circumstances. Russell admired this aspect particularly; he saw in Spinoza someone whose inner life proved that the philosophical rest that ancient thinkers strove for was still achievable in modern times.

## Open questions
- Is Spinoza's monism — one substance, two attributes — really a solution to the mind-body problem, or does it merely shift the difficulty to the relationship between attributes?
- How does Spinoza's identification of God with nature relate to traditional theism? Is this a form of faith, or a refined atheism that uses religious language to express philosophical ideas?
- Does Spinoza's idea of freedom as "understanding of necessity" really work psychologically? Or is it a consoling abstraction that does not change much about how we feel in difficult situations?

## Further reading
- Bertrand Russell, *History of Western Philosophy* (1945), Book 3 Part 1 chapter 10 — Russell's admiring treatment with explicit criticism of the geometric method
- Baruch Spinoza, *Ethica ordine geometrico demonstrata* (1677) — direct acquaintance with the text, especially Part 1 (on God) and Part 5 (on freedom and blessedness)
- Spinoza, *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* (1670) — Spinoza's political and religious writings, more accessible than the *Ethics*
- Stuart Hampshire, *Spinoza* (1951) — classic modern introduction, balanced interpretation
- Steven Nadler, *Spinoza: A Life* (1999) — scholarly biography, valuable context for his philosophical development
