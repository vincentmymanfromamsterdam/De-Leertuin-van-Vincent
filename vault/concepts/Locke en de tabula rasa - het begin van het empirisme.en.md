# Locke and the tabula rasa: the beginning of empiricism

## Core
John Locke (1632-1704) turned against the rationalist tradition of Descartes and Leibniz with one simple claim: all knowledge comes from experience. At birth the human mind is a **tabula rasa** — a blank slate — on which experience writes. There are no innate ideas, no truths we know before we acquire experience. This empiricism became the foundation of a tradition that via Berkeley and Hume led to modern science. At the same time Locke laid with his political philosophy the basis for modern liberalism — natural rights, property rights, government with the consent of the governed. Russell values Locke for his influence and calm reasonableness, but criticises him as philosophically not very original or consistent.

## Explanation
Locke lived during a turbulent period in English history — the civil war, the execution of Charles I, the protectorate of Cromwell, the Restoration of the monarchy, and the Glorious Revolution of 1688. He studied medicine and political philosophy at Oxford, became personal physician and political advisor to the earl of Shaftesbury, went into exile in the Netherlands during political unrest, and returned after the Glorious Revolution to publish his influential works. Russell points out that Locke's philosophy was deeply intertwined with the political events of his time — he was no academic thinker but an engaged intellectual who practised philosophy in service of his idea of a reasonable society.

**The Essay Concerning Human Understanding.** Locke's main philosophical work, published in 1689 after twenty years of working, opens with an attack on innate ideas. Descartes and the rationalists had claimed the mind is equipped with certain basic truths — mathematical axioms, the idea of God, moral principles — that we do not learn from experience but possess as innate knowledge. Locke contests this on multiple fronts.

Empirically: if certain ideas were innate, they would be universal. But travellers see that different cultures have radically different moral views, that some peoples have no idea of God in our sense, that even mathematical insights are not automatically present in children. Logically: the concept "innate" is confusing. If an idea is already "in" the mind without being conscious, what does that mean? Methodologically: the appeal to innate ideas is an easy escape. When someone says "we do not know this from experience, it is innate", the inquiry stops. Locke wants to start precisely where the rationalists stop — with the question how we actually acquire our ideas.

Russell values Locke's approach here. He notes that Locke usually does not try to build all-encompassing systems like Spinoza or Leibniz, but treats problems one by one, often with considerable hesitation. This can be seen as lack of philosophical systematicity, or as intellectual honesty — for Russell it is more the latter.

**How ideas then arise.** According to Locke the mind has two sources of ideas. **Sensation** provides simple ideas of sensory qualities — colour, sound, taste, feeling. **Reflection** provides ideas of mental operations — thinking, willing, remembering. Both yield "simple ideas" that the mind itself cannot make or destroy, but can only receive.

From these simple ideas the mind constructs more complex ideas through combination, comparison and abstraction. The idea "apple" is formed from sensory ideas of shape, colour, taste and texture. The idea "justice" is formed by abstraction from specific experiences of what people call just or unjust. Even the idea of "infinity" is constructed from finite experiences through iteration — we begin with a number and imagine that we keep adding to it.

Russell sees here the programme of empiricism: all ideas, however abstract, must be reducible to sensory experience. What is not reducible is no real idea but confusing language use. This programme would later be radically worked out in the logical-positivist tradition, with sometimes problematic consequences for metaphysics and theology.

**Primary and secondary qualities.** A crucial distinction in Locke is that between **primary** and **secondary** qualities of objects. Primary qualities — extension, shape, motion, number — are really present in objects themselves and our ideas of them resemble objective reality. Secondary qualities — colour, sound, taste — are merely powers of objects to evoke certain experiences in us. An apple is really round, but the red colour we see is no objective property of the apple itself, only an experience the apple evokes in us via light and sense.

Russell points out that this distinction is philosophically untenable. Berkeley would later argue that the same argument that makes secondary qualities subjective also applies to primary qualities — we know shape and size only through our senses, so why would they be "more objective" than colour or sound? Locke's distinction was an attempt to unite empiricism with a form of realism about the external world, but the attempt is logically unstable.

**Political philosophy.** Locke's other major work, *Two Treatises of Government* (1689), laid a foundation for modern liberalism. Against Robert Filmer's defence of the divine right of kings, Locke argued that all political authority arises from the consent of the governed. People have certain rights in a "state of nature" — life, liberty, property — and government exists to protect these rights. When a government violates these rights, citizens have the right to replace it.

Russell values this position, but points to a serious tension. Locke's argument for property rights — that property arises through labour on natural resources — was later used to justify colonial expropriation of indigenous peoples (they had not "cultivated" the land in the European sense). Locke himself was involved in colonial trade and held investments in the slave trade. Russell points out that even a philosopher who formulates enlightenment ideals can apply them in ways his worst enemies had not imagined.

**Religious tolerance.** Locke also wrote influential writings on religious tolerance. He argued that governments should not exercise conscience-coercion, because faith cannot be forced — only outward conformity, no inner conviction. Russell values this position, but points out that Locke's tolerance had its limits: atheists and Catholics were excluded, because they could not swear reliable oaths (atheists) or were obedient to a foreign power (Catholics, given their loyalty to the pope). Tolerance was more practical than principled.

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell has an ambivalent judgement about Locke. He values his calm reasonableness, his dislike of extremes, his engagement with practical problems. Locke was, for Russell, the philosopher of the English temperament — pragmatic, moderate, willing to compromise. At the same time Russell finds him philosophically not very original or consistent. Much of what Locke claims is derived from Descartes and scholasticism, and his starts are often not thought through. His historical influence was enormous — English and American political developments were shaped by his work — but as a systematic philosopher he was less brilliant than Spinoza or Leibniz.

But Russell also sees why this can precisely be a merit. Locke's relative modesty made his ideas accessible and usable. A philosophical genius like Spinoza is rarely directly translated into policy; a moderate thinker like Locke can lay a foundation for institutions that last for centuries.

## Why this matters
Locke stands at a pivot point in western philosophy. Before him, the rationalist tradition was dominant — knowledge came mainly from reasoning. With him a serious empirical counterforce begins that via Berkeley and Hume would lead to Kant and ultimately to modern philosophy of science. At the same time he marks a political shift: the thought that government is legitimate by consent and that individuals have rights that stand above politics, came via Locke into the American Declaration of Independence and the French Rights of Man. This is perhaps the first philosopher whose philosophy directly had political consequences we still experience now. Locke is therefore not only interesting as thinker but as source of our institutional world. Whether his philosophical problems themselves are solved is a different question — Russell suggests they are largely not solved, only shifted, and that Locke's calm reasonableness has not answered the most difficult questions but has shifted the discussion to a more productive terrain.

## Open questions
- Is the tabula rasa thesis scientifically tenable? Modern developmental psychology and evolutionary biology suggest that babies are indeed born with certain cognitive structures — capacity for language acquisition, number sense, social recognition. How does this relate to Locke's empiricism?
- Does the distinction between primary and secondary qualities hold up, or was Berkeley right that it is philosophically unstable?
- How can Locke's enlightenment ideals be reconciled with his involvement in colonial and slavery practices? Was that a personal inconsistency, or a fundamental problem in his theory?
