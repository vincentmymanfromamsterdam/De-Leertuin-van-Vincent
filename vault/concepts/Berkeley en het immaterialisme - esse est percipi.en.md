# Berkeley and immaterialism: 'esse est percipi'

## Core
George Berkeley (1685-1753) drew empiricism to its most startling conclusion: matter does not exist. What we call "material objects" are nothing but **bundles of perceptions** — colour, shape, sound, taste — and when no one perceives, they do not exist. *Esse est percipi*, to be is to be perceived. This sounded absurd to his contemporaries, and Samuel Johnson supposedly kicked a stone to "refute" Berkeley. But Berkeley's argumentation is logically watertight given his empiricist premises, and precisely for that reason interesting. Russell treats him with a mixture of respect for the logical sharpness and criticism of the specific conclusions — he sees in Berkeley a brilliant thinker who showed where Locke's half-empiricism inevitably leads.

## Explanation
Berkeley was an Irish Anglican bishop whose philosophical work largely arose in his young years. At twenty-five he published his masterwork *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710), and at twenty-eight the more accessible *Three Dialogues Between Hylas and Philonous* (1713). After that he focused mainly on religious, educational and political writings, plus a remarkable plan to found a college for young people in Bermuda — a plan that failed after years of waiting for promised government support.

Russell notes that Berkeley as a person was remarkably warm and pleasant — no one who met him believed that such an eccentric philosophy could come from such a charming man. David Hume later wrote that Berkeley's arguments "produce no conviction — only bewilderment". That is in a sense the fate of many philosophers whose conclusions stand too far from common sense.

**The attack on matter.** Berkeley's central thesis is that the concept "matter" — an independently existing, unperceived substance that supposedly causes our perceptions — is incoherent. He argues this in stages.

First he repeats Locke's argument against secondary qualities. We know that colour, sound and taste are not really in objects — they are experiences objects evoke in us. Locke had made this distinction to save primary qualities (shape, size, motion) as objective properties. But Berkeley shows that the same argument also applies to primary qualities. How do we know an apple is really round? Only by seeing or feeling its shape — which is again perception. Shape and size are not less dependent on the senses than colour or taste. If secondary qualities are subjective, so are primary ones.

Then Berkeley goes deeper. What would a "matter" be that stood entirely apart from perception? When I try to imagine an unperceived object, I still imagine an object — with its shape, colour, position in space. But that is perception in my imagination. A truly unperceived object, without any sensory quality, is simply not imaginable. And what we cannot imagine, we have no reason to postulate. Berkeley concludes that the concept of matter has no real meaning.

Russell values the logical sharpness of this argument. It is a direct corollary of Locke's empiricism: if all knowledge comes from experience, and experience from perception, then we know only perceptions — not an unknown substance behind the perceptions. Berkeley took Locke more seriously than Locke took himself.

**Esse est percipi.** Berkeley's positive claim he summarises in a Latin phrase: *esse est percipi* — to be is to be perceived. An object exists only in the sense that it is perceived or perceivable. When I close my eyes, does my cup of coffee cease to exist? Berkeley answers subtly: for me it ceases to exist, but it still exists in the perceptions of other people, and when everyone is gone — here his theology enters — it still exists in the perception of God.

God is for Berkeley not only creator but continuous perceiver. It is God's constant perceiving of the world that keeps it in being. The material world that seems stable and independent to us is in reality a shared perception generated by God and shared with us. Russell sees here the theological motivation of Berkeley's entire system: by reasoning matter away, he made room for a God who is not only creator but continuous ground of existence.

**The Anglican limerick.** A later anonymous limerick captures Berkeley's position:

> There was a young man who said "God
> Must find it exceedingly odd
> That this tree continues to be
> When there's no one about in the quad."

And the reply:

> "Dear sir, your astonishment's odd,
> I am always about in the quad,
> And that's why the tree
> Will continue to be,
> Since observed by
> Yours faithfully, God."

Russell finds these limericks charming but points out that they actually capture Berkeley's position accurately. The tree in the quad exists because God perceives it. For Berkeley this is no absurd consequence but a natural conclusion that strengthens divine worship — God is constantly, omnipresently present as perceiver of all things.

**How do you refute Berkeley?** Dr. Samuel Johnson according to James Boswell's story kicked a large stone and said "I refute him thus!" Russell notes that this is no refutation. Berkeley did not deny that we can kick stones or feel pain — he only denied that stones are anything *more* than our perceptions of them. The pain of kicking is also a perception, and fits perfectly in Berkeley's system. Johnson's "refutation" shows mainly the misunderstanding that empirical realism is an answer to idealist metaphysics. It is a different kind of claim, and is not refuted by empirical demonstration.

Russell suggests that the real criticism of Berkeley must be that he refutes **too much**. If matter has no independent existence, does the same not apply to other minds? How do I know that other people exist, when I only know my own perceptions? Berkeley answers that we "infer" other minds from behaviour — but the same inference could apply to material objects. Berkeley's selectivity (perceptions do exist, matter does not) seems less principled than practically necessary to arrive at a workable position.

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell has a paradoxical judgement of Berkeley. He finds his arguments technically stronger than most empiricists acknowledged, and his conclusions more logically consistent than many admit. At the same time he finds Berkeley's specific position untenable because it leads to solipsism if you carry it through consistently, and because the theological justification (God as continuous perceiver) introduces a philosophical blemish to solve practical problems.

But Russell sees in Berkeley something that modern philosophy must still acknowledge: that the relationship between perception and reality is not as simple as naive realism suggests. Whether we accept Berkeley's specific solution or not, the problem he raised — how do we know there is something outside our perceptions? — has not been fully solved by any later philosophy. Kant would build on it, and modern phenomenology and philosophy of science still struggle with it. Berkeley showed that consistent empiricism leads to uncomfortable conclusions, and that is a lasting lesson.

## Why this matters
Berkeley is in a sense the most important philosopher in this series precisely because his conclusions are so bewildering. He forces us to make explicit assumptions we take for granted. Do we believe there is an independent material world? Berkeley shows this is a belief, not self-evident. Do we believe our perceptions are caused by something outside us? Berkeley shows we have no direct evidence for that. Whether we accept Berkeley's answer or not, he does what philosophy should do: examine the most fundamental assumptions rather than presuppose them. That is intellectual work even Locke and Descartes had not done so radically. And it is precisely what Hume would continue, with even more serious consequences for our ordinary assumptions about knowledge and reality. Berkeley's specific conclusions have few modern adherents, but his questions are still productive.

## Open questions
- What is the right response to Berkeley's argument? Not "kicking stones", we have seen that, but a real philosophical refutation. Various modern positions (representational realism, externalism about content, naive realism) attempt it, but none gives a fully satisfying answer.
- Does Berkeley's appeal to God make the system more coherent or more problematic? A secular Berkeley would be more logically consistent but would lead to solipsism.
- Does modern physics have anything to say about Berkeley's thesis? Some interpretations of quantum mechanics suggest a role for observation in concretising reality — a remarkable echo of Berkeley's position. But such an interpretation remains controversial.
