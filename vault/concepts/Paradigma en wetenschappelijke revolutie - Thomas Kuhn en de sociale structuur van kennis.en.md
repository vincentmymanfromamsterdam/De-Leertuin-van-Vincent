# Paradigm and scientific revolution - Thomas Kuhn and the social structure of knowledge

## Core
**Thomas Kuhn** (1922-1996), trained as a physicist and later a historian of science, published *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* in 1962, one of the most influential books about science of the twentieth century. His claim is that science does not develop as a steady accumulation of facts but in a recurring pattern. Long periods of **normal science**, in which a scientific community solves puzzles within a shared **paradigm**, are interrupted by **revolutions** in which the paradigm itself is replaced. Such a paradigm determines which questions make sense, which methods count and what qualifies as a good answer. Deviating observations (**anomalies**) rarely lead directly to rejection; they are set aside or explained away until they pile up and cause a **crisis**. According to Kuhn the transition to a new paradigm cannot be fully compelled by logic and evidence, because the rivals partly use different standards and concepts; he calls this **incommensurability**. Kuhn thereby places the scientific community, with its training, textbooks and shared examples, at the heart of how knowledge is formed. The book was celebrated and fiercely contested: critics such as Popper and Lakatos saw in it an undermining of scientific rationality, which Kuhn himself always denied. The term "paradigm shift" has since come into use far beyond philosophy of science, often more loosely than Kuhn intended.

## Explanation
Kuhn arrived at his insight by a detour. In 1947, as a doctoral student in physics, he had to prepare a course on the history of mechanics and read Aristotle's *Physics*. At first it seemed to him simply bad physics. Until he saw that Aristotle was not a failed Newton but asked different questions within a different conceptual framework, in which "motion" covered every kind of change. Within that framework the text was coherent. That experience, of an older science making sense from within and being unintelligible from outside, is the seed of the whole book.

**The received picture Kuhn wrote against.**

Textbooks present science as cumulative: each generation adds facts and better theories to what was already there. Karl Popper had set a sharper model against this, in which scientists propose bold hypotheses and try to refute them. Kuhn argued that neither describes what scientists do in practice. Most of the time they do not try to refute their theory but take it as given.

**Normal science.**

Kuhn calls most scientific work normal science. A scientific community shares a paradigm and works it out: measuring more precisely, applying the theory to new cases, tying up loose ends. Kuhn compares it to puzzle-solving. The paradigm guarantees that a solution exists and supplies the rules; if it does not work out, the fault lies with the researcher, not with the puzzle.

This sounds conservative, and it is, but Kuhn does not mean it as a reproach. Precisely because the foundations are not constantly under discussion, a field can go into details that would otherwise never be reached. And precisely that precision makes deviations visible.

**What is a paradigm?**

The concept is notoriously elastic. Margaret Masterman counted twenty-one different senses in the book. In the postscript to the second edition (1970) Kuhn therefore distinguishes two:

- The **disciplinary matrix**: everything a scientific community shares, such as laws and formulas, models, values and views on what a good explanation is.
- The **exemplars**: the concrete model solutions through which students learn the field, such as the pendulum, the inclined plane or the planetary orbit.

Kuhn himself considered the second sense the most important. A physicist does not learn the trade by memorising rules but by working problems until new problems come to look like variants of familiar ones. Much of that knowledge is tacit.

**Anomaly and crisis.**

No theory ever fits all observations. Deviations are therefore not immediately treated as refutation. Two examples from astronomy show how open the outcome is:

- The orbit of Uranus deviated from prediction. It was assumed that an unknown planet was perturbing it, and in 1846 Neptune was found. The deviation became a triumph for Newton.
- The orbit of Mercury deviated too. Again an unknown planet was sought, Vulcan, but it turned out not to exist. The deviation remained unresolved for more than half a century, until Einstein's general relativity explained it in 1915.

The same strategy thus succeeded once and failed once, and this could not be seen in advance. Only when anomalies pile up, prove stubborn or touch a core point does a crisis arise. The rules loosen, competing variants appear and colleagues begin to debate foundations, which they hardly do in normal times.

**Revolution.**

A crisis sometimes ends because the old paradigm solves the problem after all, sometimes because the problem is shelved, and sometimes with a new paradigm. Kuhn stresses that a paradigm is only given up when an alternative is at hand; without a paradigm no science can be done.

His best-known example is the chemical revolution. Joseph Priestley isolated in 1774 the gas we call oxygen, but described it in terms of the phlogiston theory. Antoine Lavoisier saw something else in the same gas and built a new chemistry on it. Priestley never accepted the oxygen theory, up to his death in 1804. Who then discovered oxygen, and when? According to Kuhn the question itself shows that discovery is not a moment but a process in which observation and understanding shift together.

Kuhn compares the transition to a gestalt switch, like the drawing that is now a duck and now a rabbit. After a revolution, he writes, scientists work in a sense in a different world. It is his most quoted and most disputed formulation.

**Incommensurability.**

Why can the choice between paradigms not simply be settled by evidence? Kuhn gives three reasons:

- The rivals differ about which problems a theory must solve.
- The same words take on a different meaning. "Mass" is something different in Newton than in Einstein; "planet" meant something different to Copernicus than to Ptolemy.
- The rivals partly see different things when looking at the same thing.

As a result proponents partly talk past each other. Kuhn cites Max Planck, who remarked that a new scientific truth does not win because opponents are convinced, but because they die out and a new generation grows up with it.

What Kuhn does not claim here matters. Incommensurable does not mean incomparable, nor that the choice is arbitrary. In later years he narrowed the concept to a matter of partial untranslatability between specialist languages.

**Invisible revolutions.**

If revolutions are so far-reaching, why does the history of science look so gradual? Kuhn's answer: because textbooks are rewritten after every revolution. They present the past as preparation for the present and leave out what does not fit that line. Students learn the winning paradigm, not the struggle. This chapter bears directly on the question of how a community maintains its own authority.

**Progress.**

Kuhn does not deny that science progresses. Later theories solve more puzzles, and more precisely, than earlier ones. What he doubts is that this progress is a movement toward the truth about how the world really is. He compares it to evolution in Darwin: a development from a starting point, without a goal given in advance.

**The criticism.**

The book provoked immediate resistance, especially at a colloquium in London in 1965.

- **Karl Popper** considered normal science a danger: the normal scientist, in his view, has been badly taught, because he has not learned to think critically.
- **Imre Lakatos** accused Kuhn of turning theory choice into mob psychology, and set against it his own model of research programmes, which aimed to preserve rational standards.
- **Paul Feyerabend** drew the opposite conclusion and went further than Kuhn: there is no fixed method.

Kuhn defended himself in 1973 with a list of values all scientists share: accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness. These values guide the choice but do not compel it, because scientists weigh them differently and they can conflict with one another. Theory choice is thus a judgement and not an algorithm, but not arbitrary either.

Sociologists of the Edinburgh school, among them David Bloor and Barry Barnes, built on Kuhn and explained the content of scientific knowledge from social factors. Kuhn distanced himself from this; he felt nature played too small a part in those explanations.

**Reading guide.**

When reading, these chapters form the backbone:

- II to IV: normal science and puzzle-solving
- VI to VIII: anomaly, crisis and the response to it
- X: revolutions as changes of world view, the most disputed chapter
- XI: the invisibility of revolutions
- XIII: progress through revolutions
- The 1969 postscript, in which Kuhn answers his critics and sharpens "paradigm". Read it along with the rest; many misunderstandings about the book are already corrected there.

## Why this matters
Kuhn puts to science the question Berger put to religion: what keeps a worldview self-evident for a community? The parallels are large. Normal science rests on socialisation through textbooks and practice problems, on authoritative colleagues, on journals and on a shared language, and deviations are long absorbed within the existing framework. This makes it understandable why new ideas that later proved right initially met resistance, and why that resistance was not necessarily stupid or ill-intentioned. Equally important is the difference, and this is where it becomes interesting for the question of knowledge authority. In Kuhn the community generates its own crises: the very precision of normal science exposes the anomalies that later undermine the paradigm. A scientific paradigm is arranged so that nature can contradict it, even if it takes a long time before anyone listens. Whether that difference from other forms of authority is one of principle or of degree is an open question to which Kuhn himself gave no conclusive answer. The book also qualifies the criterion of falsifiability from the note [[Valid versus useful - why an irrefutable argument need not explain anything|Valid versus useful]]: in practice theories are not rejected because of a single counterexample, and the history of Uranus and Mercury shows that holding on is sometimes justified and sometimes not. Finally it is a warning against too easy a use of Kuhn. That established science is sometimes mistaken does not mean that every outsider with a deviant idea is an unrecognised Galileo. Most anomalies are resolved within the existing paradigm, and most challengers are wrong.

## Open questions
- Does Kuhn explain why a paradigm wins, or does he only describe that it happens? If the shared values do not compel the choice, what tips the balance: nature, the persuasiveness of proponents, or the turnover of generations?
- Does the model hold outside the physics and chemistry from which Kuhn derived it? In biology, medicine and the social sciences several approaches often coexist for long periods without a clear revolution.
- How does Kuhn's paradigm relate to Berger's plausibility structure? Both describe how a community maintains self-evidence, but only in Kuhn is the mechanism of self-undermining built in. Is that the distinction between scientific and other authority?

## Further reading
- Thomas Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962; second edition 1970 with postscript). The 2012 anniversary edition has a clear introduction by Ian Hacking
- Thomas Kuhn, "Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice" (1973), collected in *The Essential Tension* — his answer to the charge of irrationality
- Thomas Kuhn, *The Copernican Revolution* (1957) — the historical case from which much of the later book grew
- Imre Lakatos and Alan Musgrave (eds.), *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge* (1970) — the debate with Popper, Lakatos and Feyerabend
- Ludwik Fleck, *Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact* (1935) — precursor with the concept of the thought collective, mentioned by Kuhn in his preface
- Naomi Oreskes, *Why Trust Science?* (2019) — why science deserves trust precisely as a social practice
- Steven Shapin and Simon Schaffer, *Leviathan and the Air-Pump* (1985) — how the seventeenth century fought out what counts as a fact
