# Descartes and the cogito: the beginning of modern philosophy

## Core
René Descartes (1596-1650) broke with a thousand years of scholasticism through one simple question: what do I really know with certainty? His answer, "*cogito ergo sum*" — "I think, therefore I am" — became the most famous philosophical sentence of modern times. With this one certainty as anchor, he tried to rebuild all knowledge from scratch, without relying on authority, tradition or sensory experience. Russell sees in Descartes the beginning of modern philosophy — not because his conclusions proved tenable (Russell finds many of them untenable), but because his method shifted the focus of philosophy from "what is" to "how do we know". With that he opened the epistemological turn that continues to this day.

## Explanation
Descartes was a French aristocrat and soldier who found his fortune in quiet study. After a wandering life in Europe, he settled in the Netherlands, where for twenty years he worked in seclusion on his philosophical and scientific programme. Russell notes that Descartes stood out as the first great philosopher since Plato who was also original in mathematics — he developed analytic geometry, which unified algebra and geometry, and his influence on physics was considerable. For Descartes, philosophy and science were not separate subjects; they formed part of one investigation into certain knowledge.

**The problem Descartes wanted to solve.** In the seventeenth century, Europe was intellectually divided. The scholastic synthesis of Aquinas fell apart under pressure from the scientific revolution (Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo). Religious wars showed that even on the most fundamental questions there was no agreement. What did one still have that was indubitable? How could one free oneself from centuries of possibly false assumptions and start again on a solid basis?

Descartes' answer was radical: through **methodical doubt**. He proposed treating everything that was even slightly doubtable as provisionally false. Not from principled scepticism, but as method — a filter that would let only the indubitable through.

**The three steps of doubt.** In his *Meditations* (1641) Descartes worked systematically. First he doubted his sensory perceptions — they sometimes mislead us, so in principle they can always mislead us. Then he doubted the distinction between waking and dreaming — we dream convincingly, so how do we know we are not dreaming now? Finally he introduced the thought experiment of the **evil demon**: suppose there is an almighty deceiver who fabricates my entire experience, even my mathematical insights. What then remains?

Russell points to the power of this construction. Descartes allowed no room for compromise: everything that was even one percent doubtable was rejected. To modern readers it may sound like exaggerated scepticism, but for Descartes' time it was necessary — if you really wanted to rebuild all knowledge, you really had to be able to reach zero.

**The cogito.** And then Descartes discovered something remarkable. Even if everything is doubted, one thing remains: while I doubt, I think. And while I think, I must exist — otherwise there would be no one to think. "*Cogito ergo sum*" — "I think, therefore I am". This one certainty cannot be doubted away, because the fact that I doubt it proves it.

Russell appreciates the sharpness of this argument, but also points out what it does not prove. The cogito shows that thinking is taking place. It does not show that there is a persistent "I" that remains the same over time. It does not show that this thinking has a body, or is situated in a physical world. Strictly speaking, the cogito is a much weaker result than Descartes made of it — only that at this moment, in this thought-experience, something is taking place. No more.

But Descartes built an entire system on this one certainty. He identified himself as "a thinking thing" — *res cogitans* — essentially different from the extended, material world — *res extensa*. This is the notorious **mind-body dualism**: two fundamentally different substances (thinking and extension) that somehow mysteriously come together in a human.

**The proof of God.** To extend from his own thinking to knowledge of an external world, Descartes needed a proof of God. His reasoning: I have an idea of a perfect, infinite being. But I myself am finite and imperfect. How can I conceive such an idea? It must come from outside — from a being that is truly perfect and infinite. Therefore God must exist. And if God is perfect, he cannot be a deceiver — so I can trust my clear and distinct ideas. With that, Descartes restored trust in reason, in mathematics, and ultimately in the senses.

Russell finds this argument weak. It depends on the assumption that an idea of perfection cannot arise from an imperfect mind — an assumption Russell does not find plausible. He points out that we can form ideas of many things we are not ourselves (unicorns, infinity, perfect circles) without those things actually existing. Descartes' proof of God was no more convincing than earlier medieval attempts — only differently formulated.

**The Cartesian circle.** An even more serious problem Russell points out: Descartes uses clear and distinct ideas to prove God's existence, and then uses God's existence to justify trust in clear and distinct ideas. This is circular. How does Descartes know that his clarity criterion is reliable, before he has proved God? And how does he prove God, without already relying on that criterion? Contemporaries like Antoine Arnauld already pointed out this problem, and Russell treats it as a fundamental weakness of the Cartesian programme.

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell has mixed feelings. He values Descartes as a **methodological pioneer** — the idea to subject all knowledge to radical doubt and start again from the least doubtable was a breakthrough. It shifted the philosophical task from "describing what is" to "justifying how we know". This epistemological turn still determines what we understand by philosophy.

But Russell finds Descartes' specific conclusions untenable. Mind-body dualism leads to the notorious mind-body problem: how do two fundamentally different substances interact? Descartes' answer — through the pineal gland in the brain — was unsatisfying even to his contemporaries. The proof of God does not hold up against modern criticism. The Cartesian circle is a real logical weakness. And the starting point of a lonely thinking self, apart from body and world, has according to Russell led to deep distortions in how we have thought about knowledge and mind.

What remains is the method — not the conclusions but the willingness to question assumptions radically. In that sense a straight line runs from Descartes to Hume, Kant, and ultimately to the scientific method itself. Descartes' real legacy is not what he proved, but what he set in motion.

## Why this matters
Descartes opened modern philosophy through one simple shift: instead of starting with the world and then asking how we know it, he started with our knowledge and only then asked what we can know of the world. This epistemological turn proved productive and at the same time full of problems. However it may be, after Descartes philosophy was never the same. Locke, Berkeley, Hume, Kant and all later philosophy unfold within the framework Descartes opened: questions about the relationship between mind and world, about the nature of certainty, about how we can justify knowledge. Descartes' influence is therefore perhaps the best example in philosophy of someone whose specific answers are outdated but whose questions are lasting. Russell would probably add that this is how philosophy progresses — not by giving definitive answers, but by formulating better questions. In that, Descartes was undisputedly great.

## Open questions
- Is the cogito really a certain knowledge, or does it only show that there is a thought-event, without a persistent "I"? Hume would later make precisely this point sharply.
- How can mind-body dualism be reconciled with modern neuroscience? Most philosophers today accept some form of physicalism, but the mind-body problem remains stubborn.
- What remains of Descartes' method when his specific conclusions are rejected? Russell suggests: the willingness to doubt radically, provided it is connected to the willingness to recognise real knowledge when it presents itself.

## Further reading
- Bertrand Russell, *History of Western Philosophy* (1945), Book 3 Part 1 chapter 9 — Russell's treatment with explicit criticism of the Cartesian circle
- René Descartes, *Meditationes de Prima Philosophia* (1641) — direct acquaintance with the texts themselves, [English translation online](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/23306)
- Descartes, *Discours de la méthode* (1637) — more accessible and shorter than the *Meditations*, with the famous introduction of the cogito
- Bernard Williams, *Descartes: The Project of Pure Enquiry* (1978) — modern scholarly explanation and critique
- Anthony Kenny, *Descartes: A Study of his Philosophy* (1968) — analytic-philosophical approach that sharply works out the logical problems
