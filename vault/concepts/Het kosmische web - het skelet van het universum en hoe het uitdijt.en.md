# The cosmic web: the skeleton of the universe and how it expands

## Core
The universe is, on the largest scale, not a uniform sea of galaxies but a network-like structure — a cosmic web of filaments, knots and empty regions. Dark matter forms this skeleton and visible matter follows. The expansion of the universe does not work uniformly on this structure: dense clusters and knots remain gravitationally bound and intact, while the thin filaments are gradually pulled apart and voids become ever emptier. Dark energy is everywhere equally strong, but its net effect is inherently non-homogeneous, and this non-homogeneity determines how the universe will evolve over cosmic timescales.

## Explanation
When you make a 3D map of all observable galaxies in the universe, you do not get an even distribution. What appears is a stunning pattern: long thread-like structures — filaments — stretching across hundreds of millions of light-years, coming together in dense knots where galactic clusters sit, surrounding large empty regions — voids — where very little visible matter exists. This is the **cosmic web**, mapped in detail by surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and the Dark Energy Survey.

The structure is no artistic metaphor but a measurable reality. A typical filament extends across tens to hundreds of megaparsecs (one megaparsec is about 3.3 million light-years) and contains thousands of galaxies along its length. Galactic clusters such as the Virgo Cluster and Coma Cluster sit at knots where multiple filaments converge. Voids have diameters between 100 and 300 million light-years and contain only a handful of galaxies.

**Dark matter forms the skeleton.** The crucial observation is that the pattern is not determined by visible matter. In the early universe there were small density fluctuations in dark matter. Under gravity these fluctuations grew into the web we see today. Gas and later stars flowed along the dark matter filaments and formed galaxies at the knots. The visible galaxies are therefore not independent entities each with their own halo — they are embedded in a much larger dark matter structure that spans whole clusters and filaments. Estimates suggest that about a quarter to half of all dark matter sits in galactic halos, with the rest in the broader web.

**The largest scales.** On the very largest scales — above several hundred megaparsecs — the universe is statistically homogeneous. On smaller scales it certainly is not. The variation in density between the centres of galactic clusters and the centres of voids spans ten orders of magnitude. What standard cosmology calls homogeneity is an averaged behaviour on large scale, not the local reality everywhere.

**How expansion affects this web is not uniform.** Here lies a deep counterintuitive insight. Dark energy has a constant density in space — everywhere about 10⁻⁹ joules per cubic metre. But its net effect is strongly non-homogeneous, because it competes with local gravity of mass, and that mass is far from uniform.

For a specific volume, roughly: if the mass density lies above a critical threshold, gravity wins and the volume remains bound. Below it, dark energy wins and the volume expands. The threshold comes out around the present time at about 10⁻²⁹ kg/m³. Comparing this value to different structures:

| Location | Density (kg/m³) | Dynamics |
|---|---|---|
| Cluster centre | ~10⁻²⁴ | Tightly bound, no expansion |
| Galactic halo | ~10⁻²² | Tightly bound, no expansion |
| Filament centre | ~10⁻²⁷ | Mostly bound, locally stable |
| Filament edge | ~10⁻²⁸ | Precarious balance, can go either way |
| Void | ~10⁻³⁰ | Expands, faster than average |

This explains how the web evolves. Dense knots and clusters remain intact, bound by their own attraction. Thinner parts of filaments sit in a gradual phase transition where dark energy slowly gains the upper hand. Voids become even emptier over time because their matter is drawn to filament edges while the volume expands.

**The Bullet Cluster and separated dark matter.** The most spectacular confirmation that dark matter exists independently and forms the web comes from the Bullet Cluster — a pair of merging galactic clusters at 3.7 billion light-years. When they passed through each other in the past, their hot gas was slowed by its own pressure and stayed in the middle. But the dark matter, measured via gravitational lensing, flew with the galaxies — not with the gas. In the centre there is now ordinary matter without much dark matter, while on either side dark matter concentrations have flown away. This is direct evidence that dark matter is not inseparably coupled to ordinary matter, and that it plays its own dynamic role in the universe.

**Dark galaxies and intergalactic dark matter.** On top of the filament structure are smaller objects consisting fully or almost fully of dark matter. **Dark galaxies** such as the candidate VIRGOHI21 have the gravitational mass of a dwarf galaxy but virtually no stars. **Ultra-diffuse galaxies** such as Dragonfly 44 have the volume of a spiral galaxy with a thousand times fewer stars, possibly 99% dark matter. In addition, filaments themselves have dark matter densities up to hundreds of times higher than the average of the universe. And even voids are not entirely empty — they have about 10-20% of average density. The general lesson is that dark matter is much more widely distributed than visible matter suggests, and large parts of its mass sit in regions we would classify as "empty" if we only looked at light.

**The future of the web.** Dark energy accelerates the expansion of the universe. As this continues, the filaments of the web are pulled ever thinner and ultimately broken. The dense clusters remain intact — they are tightly bound. But between clusters the connections weaken, and eventually they drift apart at accelerating speeds. Over hundreds of billions of years, what is now a coherent web will fragment into isolated clusters that each retain their integrity but ultimately can no longer perceive each other — their mutual expansion becomes faster than light can travel between them. Each cluster will end in its own cosmic island existence.

## Why this matters
The cosmic web fundamentally revises our mental picture of the universe. We have grown up with the idea that galaxies stand apart from each other in a uniform space. But the reality is that they are highly clustered, embedded in a larger structure shaped by dark matter. Galaxies like the Milky Way are not "somewhere in space" — they sit at specific places in a cosmic architecture that traces back to the first moments after the Big Bang. The web is therefore both the silenced history of the universe and its present state. And it explains why cosmic expansion is such a deceptive concept: it does not proceed uniformly everywhere but exclusively in those regions where density is low enough for dark energy to gain the upper hand. That is a deeply counterintuitive fact, and it determines the fate of the universe on the longest timescales.

## Open questions
- How much of the dark matter sits in galactic halos versus in the broader web? Current estimates range from 25% to 50% in halos, and more precise measurements via future lensing surveys could clarify this.
- Do dark galaxies really exist as primordial structures, or are all candidates actually normal galaxies that have lost their stars through interactions?
- Does dark energy change over time, or is its density truly constant? A change would make the future of the web fundamentally different — a decreasing dark energy would make the web more stable, an increasing one would eventually pull even clusters apart.
