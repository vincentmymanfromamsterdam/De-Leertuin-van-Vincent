# Augustine and the inward turn: time, memory and the seeking soul

## Core
Augustine of Hippo (354-430) connected Greek philosophy — especially Plato and the neo-Platonists — with Christian revelation into a synthesis that would dominate medieval thought for twelve centuries. With his *Confessions* (around 400) he wrote the first autobiographical philosophy of the western tradition and shifted the focus of philosophical inquiry to the inner world. For him, the examination of his own memories, desires and doubts became a serious philosophical method. Russell has mixed feelings about Augustine: he admires his psychological depth and intellectual power, but criticises his theological conclusions — especially the doctrine of original sin and predestination — as psychologically and morally harmful.

## Explanation
Augustine was born in Roman North Africa at a time when the empire was falling apart. He was successively a Manichaean (a gnostic sect), a Platonist, and finally a Christian. Russell emphasises that these biographical wanderings deeply shaped his philosophy: he did not think abstractly about faith but from his own experience of seeking and conversion.

**The Confessions as a new form.** For Augustine, no earlier philosophical text was so personal. He described his youth, his sexual desires, his intellectual crises, his troubled relationship with his mother Monica, his theological struggles. Russell notes that this was a unique literary achievement — pre-Christian philosophers wrote treatises or dialogues, but no one wrote so intimately about his own soul. With the *Confessions*, the inner world became a legitimate subject of philosophical inquiry.

But it was not just novelty for novelty's sake. Augustine had a philosophical programme. He wanted to show that man can only understand himself by turning to God — that self-knowledge and knowledge of God are intertwined. "*Tu autem eras interior intimo meo*" — "You were more inward than my inmost self" — is perhaps his most famous formulation. God is not somewhere outside us in heaven, but deeper in us than our own consciousness reaches.

**The problem of time.** Russell points to Augustine's analysis of time in *Confessions* Book 11 as one of his deepest achievements. Augustine asks: what is time? When I am not asked, I know it. When I have to explain it, I do not know it any more. He discovers that the past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present has no duration — it is an infinitely thin borderline between what has been and what will come. How can time exist then?

Augustine's answer is psychological. Time does not exist in the world but **in the mind**. Past is memory, future is expectation, present is attention. When I sing a song, I do not only hear the current note, but I also remember earlier notes and expect the next — otherwise there would be no melody. The mind stretches over time, and that stretching *is* the time as we experience it. Russell sees here a remarkably modern phenomenological analysis that would precede later thinkers (Husserl, Heidegger, Bergson) by more than fifteen hundred years.

**Original sin and predestination.** Here Russell becomes critical. Augustine developed the doctrine that all humans are born in sin — not their own sin, but the common sinfulness they inherit from Adam. Redemption cannot be earned through good works; it is a gift of God's grace, and God gives that grace to whom he wills. The rest is predestined for damnation.

Russell finds this doctrine psychologically devastating. It disrupts moral psychology: if everything depends on God's arbitrary grace, there is no room for human responsibility or self-development. It alienates the average person from themselves — convinced that their natural desires are corrupt and their salvation is not in their own hands. And historically this doctrine would feed centuries of religious anxiety-thinking, especially in its Calvinist variant.

Russell acknowledges that Augustine himself did not experience this doctrine as psychological tyranny. For him it was precisely a liberation from Pelagianism (the doctrine that salvation can be reached through one's own effort), which he saw as proud and self-satisfied. Augustine saw in grace a gift, and in total dependence on that gift a form of rest. But Russell argues that the psychological consequences for weaker souls have been more serious than Augustine himself realised.

**The City of God.** In his other magnum opus, *De Civitate Dei* (around 426), Augustine responded to the sacking of Rome by the Visigoths in 410. Pagan critics said: this is happening because we have abandoned our old gods. Augustine answered by distinguishing between the earthly city (based on self-love) and the city of God (based on love of God). Earthly empires come and go; the city of God is eternal and is not injured by political collapse.

Russell sees here an early separation between church and state that would determine medieval thought for years. At the same time he warns that the same doctrine could also be used to evade worldly responsibility — if the earthly city is unimportant, why should we work for its improvement?

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell values Augustine for his psychological sharpness, his literary power, and his integration of Platonic philosophy with Christian revelation — an achievement that had historical influence, even though Russell himself does not share the faith. He sees in Augustine a first-rate thinker, comparable to Plato in influence, if not in originality. What Russell objects to is the specific theological conclusion: that man is a fallen being who cannot save himself. Russell thinks this view has influenced western self-image in problematic ways — recurring themes of sinfulness, guilt and need for external redemption are not only Augustinian inheritance, but strongly shaped by him.

## Why this matters
Augustine marks the transition from antique to medieval philosophy, and his influence is hard to overestimate. Twelve centuries of Christian thinking built on his synthesis, and even modern psychology and philosophy of time carry traces of his analyses. But his significance also lies in something subtler: he showed that philosophy could take the inner world seriously, that self-examination was a legitimate part of philosophical work, and that the seeker himself — with all his doubts, desires and weaknesses — could be the object of inquiry. This turn opened a space for reflection that was less central in classical philosophy. At the same time, his influence shows how a profound thinker can be both enlightening and harmful — harmful not through bad intentions, but through doctrines that worked out differently in other hands than he had foreseen. A warning for every thinker who formulates belief systems.

## Open questions
- Is Augustine's analysis of time — time as psychological experience — really a deep truth, or does it only show how we experience time, apart from what time objectively is? The question remains open: modern physics treats time differently from psychology, and how these two relationships connect is still unresolved.
- Has the doctrine of original sin harmed more than helped western self-experience, as Russell suggests, or does it also capture something real — the experience that we are not spontaneously in balance or free?
- How can we appreciate Augustine's deep psychological insights without embracing the theological conclusions Russell warns against?

## Further reading
- Bertrand Russell, *History of Western Philosophy* (1945), Book 2 Part 1 chapter 4 — Russell's treatment with explicit criticism of the predestination doctrine
- Augustine, *Confessions* — direct acquaintance with the text itself, especially Book 10 (on memory) and Book 11 (on time) are of philosophical richness
- Augustine, *De Civitate Dei* — Book 14 on the two cities provides a good introduction to his political and historical thinking
- Peter Brown, *Augustine of Hippo* (1967, revised 2000) — modern scholarly biography, valuable context for his work
- Charles Taylor, *Sources of the Self* (1989), chapter on Augustine — how Augustine's inward turn shaped modern self-understanding
