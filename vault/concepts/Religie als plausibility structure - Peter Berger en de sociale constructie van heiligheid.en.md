# Religion as plausibility structure - Peter Berger and the social construction of sacredness

## Core
The Austrian-American sociologist **Peter Berger** (1929-2017) developed in the 1960s one of the most influential theoretical frameworks for understanding religion as a social phenomenon. His core concept is the **plausibility structure** — the network of social relations, institutions, daily practices and shared meanings that ensures a particular worldview remains self-evident for its adherents. Religions, Berger argues, do not "survive" through the intrinsic truth of their doctrines but through the robustness of their plausibility structures. As long as a community performs the rituals, tells the stories, recognises the authorities and its members interact with other believers, the religious reality remains plausible. When this social infrastructure weakens — through migration, modernisation, pluralism, or secularisation — the religious reality begins to lose its self-evidence, even if the doctrines themselves have not been refuted. Berger's work, especially *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) and his earlier *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966, with Thomas Luckmann), builds on classical sociology of religion by **Émile Durkheim** (religion as social cohesion) and **Max Weber** (religion and economic rationalisation), but adds a phenomenological dimension that makes the understanding of religious experience from within possible. His approach is especially relevant for those who want to understand religion without being part of it — it offers a vocabulary to ask why certain worldviews were plausible at certain moments, without having to pronounce on their truth.

## Explanation
Those who grow up within a traditional religious community — an Orthodox Jewish enclave, a Catholic village in southern Italy, an evangelical church community in the American South — experience religious reality as **self-evident**. The idea that God exists, that specific rituals are meaningful, that certain behaviours are sinful — these are not hypotheses that need to be proven but aspects of reality itself, as evident as that the sun rises.

How is this possible? Peter Berger asked this question in a way that was neither religious apology nor atheist reckoning. He did not ask "are these beliefs true?", but "**which social mechanisms ensure that these beliefs remain self-evident to their adherents?**". The answer lies in what he called a plausibility structure.

**What is a plausibility structure?**

A plausibility structure is the whole of social supports that carries a worldview. For a religion it contains:

- **Daily rituals** that make the religious reality visible and tangible (prayer, meal blessings, sabbath observance, mass)
- **Community structures** where believers gather and affirm each other (church, synagogue, mosque, prayer group, home meeting)
- **Authoritative figures** who guard interpretation (priests, rabbis, imams, elders)
- **Socialisation** of children that impresses the religious reality on them from a young age
- **Shared language and symbols** that make the religious reality expressible
- **Social sanctions** that discourage deviation (from mild disapproval to excommunication)
- **Physical spaces** that embody the sacredness (church buildings, pilgrimage sites, holy places)
- **Cultural artefacts** that carry the worldview (prayer books, icons, religious music, art)

As long as this infrastructure is intact and the believer is primarily in contact with it, the religious reality remains plausible. When you sing every Sunday with hundreds of others about God's grace, when your children are taught at a Catholic school, when your neighbours venerate the same saints, when you marry in a cathedral and your parents are buried in the parish cemetery — then Catholic reality is no hypothesis but the air you breathe.

**What weakens a plausibility structure?**

Berger identified various processes that undermine plausibility structures. The most important is what he called **pluralism**: when the believer regularly comes into contact with people with other worldviews, and has to admit that those people are also reasonable and intelligent. In a homogeneous community the outsider is an abstraction; in a pluralistic society he is your colleague, your neighbour, your brother-in-law.

Pluralism forces what Berger called the **heretical imperative** (title of his 1979 book): modern man must **choose** what he believes, because he no longer simply grows up in one tradition that he accepts without question. This compulsion to choose — even when one chooses traditional religion — fundamentally changes the nature of faith. Religion is no longer the self-evident background but one option among other options.

Other factors that undermine plausibility structures:
- **Migration** that takes people away from their original community
- **Urbanisation** that increases anonymity and freedom of choice
- **Mass media** that make alternative worldviews visible
- **Scientific explanations** that displace supernatural explanations for certain phenomena
- **Bureaucratic rationalisation** (Weber) that replaces religious authority with technical expertise
- **Economic development** that reduces existential insecurity where religion traditionally offered comfort

**The unexpected turn in Berger's thinking.**

Berger is often associated with the **secularisation thesis**: the prediction that modernisation inevitably leads to decreasing religion. In his early work he largely embraced this thesis. But from the 1990s he did something few sociologists do: he **publicly revised his position**. In works such as *The Desecularization of the World* (1999) he acknowledged that empirical reality refuted his earlier predictions.

Religion turns out worldwide **not** to be declining with modernisation. Evangelicalism is growing explosively in Latin America and Africa. Pentecostalism is one of the fastest-growing religious movements ever. Islam is in many contexts actually strengthened by modernisation. Only Western Europe and parts of elite cultures in other regions are clearly secularising. This is a regional exception, not a worldwide rule.

Berger adjusted his theory: not secularisation is universal, but **pluralisation**. Everywhere in the world people must deal with multiple plausibility structures simultaneously. In some contexts this leads to secularisation (Western Europe, academic milieus); in others to hardening and fundamentalism (which can be seen as attempts to actively defend a plausibility structure against pluralisation). Both reactions are **modern phenomena**, not survivals of premodernity.

**Connection with sociology of knowledge.**

Berger's sociology of religion does not stand on its own. It is an application of his more general sociology of knowledge, developed with **Thomas Luckmann** in *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966). There they argue that **all** worldviews are socially constructed — not in the sense that they are arbitrary or untrue, but in the sense that they are maintained by social processes and that their self-evidence depends on institutional support.

This applies not only to religion. It applies also to scientific communities (which have their own rituals, authorities and socialisation processes), to political ideologies, to professional identities. The physician who experiences his reality in terms of disease entities and therapeutic interventions, functions within a plausibility structure maintained by medical education, collegial consensus, institutional practices and patient expectations. Change that structure (as happened with Semmelweis's hand-washing theory in the nineteenth century, initially rejected by the medical establishment) and what was first evident becomes controversial.

**Berger's limitations.**

The approach has critics. A principal one is that it deliberately circumvents the **epistemic question** — what is actually true. Berger describes how worldviews become and remain plausible, but does not judge which worldviews are correct. For some this is a virtue (sociological purity, no mixing of description and norm); for others a shortcoming (religions after all claim truth, and that claim deserves substantive assessment, not only sociological explanation).

A second criticism is that the approach under-illuminates **individual religious experience**. Mystics, converts, people who have deep spiritual experiences without communal support — these fit poorly in the plausibility structure framework. Berger acknowledged this later and paid attention to individual religious experience, but his core framework remains structurally sociological.

A third criticism comes from **rational choice theory of religion** (Rodney Stark, William Bainbridge, Laurence Iannaccone): they argue that Berger pays too little attention to the rational cost-benefit analyses of believers. People choose religious participation, they argue, partly on the basis of what they get from it — community, identity, existential comfort, sometimes specific services (education, care). This model better explains why some religions grow and others shrink in competitive religious markets.

**Applications.**

Berger's framework has proven extraordinarily productive for various questions. Some examples:

Why did some religious groups hold up better in modernity than others? Groups that made their plausibility structures strong enough — through residential concentration (Orthodox Jews in Bnei Brak), through their own educational systems (evangelical Christian schools), through strict regulation of contact with outsiders (Amish), through their own media ecosystems — maintained their religious reality much better than groups that let their members assimilate.

Why do converts and apostates sometimes become so intensely invested in the new worldview? Because they have made an active investment in a new plausibility structure and must confirm it to themselves. Berger describes how "alternation" — the shifting between worldviews — often goes together with biographical reinterpretation in which the whole life is re-read from the new framework.

Why are fundamentalist movements a modern phenomenon, not a premodern remnant? Because they are an active reaction to pluralisation — attempts to harden a threatened plausibility structure through sharper boundaries, stricter rules, and explicit rejection of alternatives.

## Why this matters
Berger's approach offers a vocabulary to look at religion without having to be part of it, without having to be hostile, and without having to pretend content is unimportant. You can analyse why a particular religion at a particular moment in a particular culture was plausible, and what carried its plausibility structure, without pronouncing on whether its doctrines are true. This is precisely the type of analysis someone who finds religion structurally-sociologically more interesting than substantively — someone who for instance finds the Grimm tales as wondrous as religious texts — can productively work with. It forces a subtle but important distinction: the question "why does this community believe this?" is a different question from "is this true?", and both questions deserve their own methods. More importantly: Berger's framework is generalisable beyond religion. It explains not only why Catholicism was self-evident in medieval Europe, but also why Newtonian physics was self-evident for nineteenth-century physicists (and why relativity was initially rejected), why certain political systems in certain times seemed "natural" (monarchy, nationalism), and why certain medical paradigms were so hard to replace. The broader lesson is that self-evidence of a worldview does not prove its truth but its institutional anchoring. This is in a sense a secularisation of what Thomas Kuhn described in philosophy of science — paradigm shifts that have more to do with institutional dynamics than with pure rational persuasion. For the Leertuin, Berger is a natural entry point to the broader thematic of knowledge authority that emerges across multiple ongoing threads. He connects with the Spinoza question (why was an intellectually superior philosophy suppressed for centuries?), with the biblical scholarship discussions (why do some believers hold to interpretations that virtually all scholars reject?), and with the methodological question of how worldviews are legitimised in general. The plausibility structure is one of the most powerful conceptual tools to approach these kinds of questions in a structured way.

## Open questions
- Is there in a globally pluralistic society still something possible as a stable plausibility structure, or is permanent uncertainty the new condition? Berger's late work tended toward the latter, with consequences for how we must think about meaning-making in more modern societies.
- How does Berger's analysis relate to digital "echo chambers" — online communities that build their own plausibility structures around conspiracy theories, political ideologies or lifestyles? The mechanism seems comparable but the speed of formation and dissolution is much higher than in traditional religious communities.
- Is it right that Berger's framework passes no judgment on truth? Critics (especially from religious quarters) argue that sociology that remains consistently methodologically agnostic becomes implicitly relativistic and thereby itself takes a position in the debate it claims to describe.

## Further reading
- Peter Berger, *The Sacred Canopy: Elements of a Sociological Theory of Religion* (1967) — the classic work, accessibly written, introduces the plausibility structure concept
- Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann, *The Social Construction of Reality: A Treatise in the Sociology of Knowledge* (1966) — the broader sociology of knowledge from which the sociology of religion emerged
- Peter Berger, *The Heretical Imperative: Contemporary Possibilities of Religious Affirmation* (1979) — the analysis of modern religious choice
- Peter Berger (ed.), *The Desecularization of the World* (1999) — his public revision of the secularisation thesis
- Grace Davie, *The Sociology of Religion* (2007) — modern introduction that places Berger in broader context
- Charles Taylor, *A Secular Age* (2007) — complementary work, more philosophical in design, on how modern secularity became possible
- Rodney Stark and Roger Finke, *Acts of Faith* (2000) — rational choice approach as counterweight
