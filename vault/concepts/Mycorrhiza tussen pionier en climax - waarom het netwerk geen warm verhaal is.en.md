# Mycorrhiza between pioneer and climax - why the network is no warm story

## Core
The popular picture of the underground mycorrhizal network — old trees that via their fungal partners actively feed younger generations — is empirically less well supported than often thought. When pioneer and climax species are connected through the same network (which is not always the case, because they often use different mycorrhiza types), the network usually works **competitively** rather than facilitatively. Adult trees that supply many carbohydrates dominate fungal allocation via the **reciprocal reward** mechanism; seedlings more often experience suppression than support. The succession from pioneer to climax is mainly driven by light competition and by a fundamental **fungal turnover** from arbuscular to ectomycorrhizal systems that restructures the entire soil regime. The network modifies competition but does not reverse its fundamental direction. This picture fits better with the Darwinian model of constant competition for resources — inter- and intra-species — than with the romantic narrative of underground care and mutuality.

## Explanation
The popular books about the "wood wide web" — Peter Wohlleben's *The Hidden Life of Trees* (2015), Suzanne Simard's *Finding the Mother Tree* (2021), large parts of Merlin Sheldrake's *Entangled Life* (2020) — have established a picture of forests as cooperative communities in which old trees share their nutrients with younger generations via mycorrhizal networks. The story spoke to people, was widely picked up by media, and became anchored in public imagination. But its scientific foundation was thoroughly revised in 2023, and the more refined picture now emerging is more interesting than the original story — not warmer, but sharper.

**First a fundamental distinction: two different networks.**

Not all mycorrhizal connections are the same, and this distinction determines which trees can even be in contact via the network. Arbuscular mycorrhiza (AM) is the oldest form, about 400 million years old, and is used by about 80% of all plant species. AM fungi penetrate the root cells and form small tree-shaped structures (the arbuscules) through which exchange takes place. Ectomycorrhiza (EcM) arose later, about 200 million years ago, and is used by a smaller group of plant species. EcM fungi grow around the root cells without entering them, and often form clearly visible mushroom fruiting bodies at the surface.

The ecological consequence of this distinction is large. Pioneer species — birches, willows, poplars, aspens, and most herbaceous pioneers — are virtually all AM. Many classic climax species of temperate regions — oaks, beeches, pines, spruces — are EcM. AM fungi and EcM fungi belong to different fungal kingdoms (Glomeromycota respectively Basidiomycota and Ascomycota) and cannot participate in each other's networks. An AM birch and an EcM beech live in **separate underground worlds**, even when they stand next to each other.

This is already in itself a mechanism that drives succession. When an AM-dominated pioneer forest transitions to an EcM-dominated climax forest, an underground fungal turnover takes place that fundamentally changes the soil regime. EcM forests have a slower carbon cycle, more humus accumulation and higher acidity. The soil becomes characteristic of the next ecological regime, and can gradually become inhospitable to the pioneer species — a kind of ecological lock-in in which the winner of phase A actively contributes to a soil more suitable for phase B. Daniel Janzen once called this type of succession "autosuicide by soil improvement".

**When they are connected.**

Not every pioneer-climax relationship runs via different mycorrhiza types. Some climax species are also AM, especially in tropical and subtropical forests — maples, elms, many tropical climax trees. And some pioneers are EcM, such as trembling aspen in North American boreal forests. In these cases pioneer and climax species can be in the same fungal network. Here the central question of this note becomes relevant: what does that connection do to their mutual competition?

Recent research gives a fairly unambiguous answer. A nice study from 2024 with Brazilian savanna grass (*Brachiaria brizantha*), pioneer tree (*Heliocarpus popayanensis*) and climax tree (*Cariniana estrellensis*) showed that when these three species are connected via a shared AM network, the adult individuals **suppress seedlings** via that network — not only of other species, but even of their own species. Seedling growth was most strongly inhibited by the network of the adult grass, moderately by the network of the pioneer aspen, and least by the network of the climax seedling. The pattern follows succession in reverse: earlier successional stages produce networks that counteract all later stages.

The mechanism behind this is called **reciprocal reward**, and it unmasks the network as a biochemical market rather than a cooperation. Fungi need carbohydrates they cannot produce themselves (they do not photosynthesise), plants need phosphorus and nitrogen which fungi are much better at extracting from the soil. The fungus-plant relationship is a symbiosis, but within a network with multiple plants an implicit negotiation arises. The fungus invests its phosphorus in the plants that return the most carbohydrates. A large, adult, well-illuminated tree can supply much sugar and thereby monopolises fungal attention. Seedlings in its shadow can supply much less and therefore receive relatively little in return. The network works for whoever produces most, not for whoever needs most.

This is a deeply Darwinian logic, and it is consistent with what we already know about evolutionary selection. A fungus that gives away its phosphorus to weak partners without receiving adequate sugars in return is selected against — other fungi that negotiate more strictly get relatively more carbohydrates and reproduce better. The network looks like a cooperation when you only look at the plant-fungus relationship, but becomes competitive as soon as multiple plants enter the network. This is no moral criticism of fungi; it is simply how natural selection acts on a mutually-dependent relationship when multiple parties participate.

**The Karst controversy of 2023.**

Here comes an important recent revision that sharpens the whole story. In 2023, Justine Karst, Melanie Jones and Jason Hoeksema published in *Nature Ecology & Evolution* a thorough re-analysis of the scientific literature on which the "mother tree" story is based. Their conclusions were sharp. Many of the original studies had methodological weaknesses — small sample sizes, no adequate controls, or results that were specific to one species but were overgeneralised. Small proven effects were inflated in later literature to general principles. The direction of carbon transfer via mycorrhizal networks was more often bidirectional or opposite than unambiguously "from old to young". And especially: light competition and root competition explained in most experiments more than network effects.

Karst and colleagues do not claim that mycorrhizal networks do not exist or are irrelevant. They exist absolutely, and substances move through them. What they contest is the specific claim that old trees actively send carbon or nutrients to their offspring or to other trees in a way that is ecologically meaningful for the recipients. The amount of carbon transferred is in many measured cases negligible — less than 1% of the recipient's needs.

The response from Suzanne Simard and her colleagues was sharp, and the debate is not yet settled. But what has happened in the meantime is that the original story can no longer be presented as scientific consensus. For those who really want to understand the ecological dynamics, the new picture is presumably closer to the truth, even though it is less attractive to tell.

**Plant-soil feedback and the direction of succession.**

A final layer of the story: AM and EcM systems have fundamentally different **plant-soil feedback** properties, and this influences which type of forest ultimately establishes itself.

AM systems often show **negative feedback**: seedlings of the same species do worse under their parents than under foreign species, presumably because species-specific pathogens accumulate in the soil around adult specimens. This promotes species diversity — no single species can locally monopolise because its own offspring is suppressed. AM-dominated tropical forests belong to the most species-rich ecosystems on earth, and this feedback is an important mechanism that maintains that diversity.

EcM systems often show **positive feedback**: seedlings do better under parents of the same species, because the existing EcM network can support them and the soil chemistry is already well-attuned. This promotes monodominance — beech woods, oak woods, pine forests — in which one species can dominate over large areas. EcM-dominated temperate and boreal forests are therefore characteristically poorer in species than AM-dominated tropical ecosystems.

For the pioneer-climax dynamic this means: pioneers live in AM systems with negative feedback (which reinforces their short lifespan and rapid turnover), while many classic climax species live in EcM systems with positive feedback (which explains their long dominance). The fungal turnover during succession is thereby not only a shift of fungal species, but of the entire **feedback regime** that structures the forest.

**The synthesis.**

What this whole story shows is that the underground network is no counterforce to Darwinian competition but an **arena** in which that competition continues, only via biochemical means instead of light- and water-competition. Pioneers and climax species do not only compete for sunlight and soil water, they also continuously negotiate with their fungal partners about nutrients, in a market that pulls toward the most productive player. The adult pioneer in full sun dominates the AM network and simultaneously inhibits its own replacements; when it weakens and dies, its dominance breaks and the climax seedling gets its chance — not through help from the network, but through the disappearance of a dominant competitor.

In this picture, the succession from pioneer to climax is no cooperation story but a sequence of competitive regimes, with mycorrhizal networks as one of the fronts on which that struggle takes place. That is less attractive than the romantic version, but it is more consistent with what we recognise elsewhere in the physics of living systems — selection works, market processes arise as soon as multiple actors with different interests participate, and cooperation is usually a marginal phenomenon within a larger context of competition.

## Why this matters
This revision of the wood wide web story touches on a deeper point about how scientific narratives become independent of the evidence supporting them. The picture of forests as caring communities came from somewhere — a few studies, a charismatic researcher, a writer with talent for metaphor — and subsequently grew into a generally accepted truth that was repeated in textbooks, documentaries and popular books. The Karst controversy shows that this kind of narrative-reinforcement was not halted by peer review or normal scientific criticism; it was only ten years later explicitly pushed back, and the debate is not yet over. This is a nice example of what came up in [[Between myth and method - how humans deal with intrinsic uncertainty|our earlier reflection on knowledge and uncertainty]]: also in science we slide easily from what we cautiously know to what we would like to believe. At the same time, the story illustrates that the sharper, Darwinian picture of constant competition between organisms — inter- and intra-species — is no outdated nineteenth-century romantic. It remains the most consistent framework with which we can understand biological dynamics, even when recent revision replaces simpler cooperation stories. Symbiosis exists, but within every symbiosis hides negotiation, and as soon as there are multiple partners, competitive markets arise rather than caring communities. That is no reason to think more cynically about nature, but to describe it more accurately.

## Open questions
- To what extent is the reciprocal reward mechanism universally applicable, and do ecosystems exist in which it really runs differently? Some researchers suspect that under extreme stress (drought, nutrient deficiencies) the network dynamics can temporarily become more facilitative, because fungi then benefit from keeping as many plant partners alive as possible. Hard evidence for this is still thin.
- How does underground mycorrhizal competition relate to above-ground light competition? Do they work in parallel, do they reinforce each other, or do they compensate each other in different circumstances? Integrated models that simulate both levels simultaneously are still in their infancy.
- What is the role of mycorrhizal networks in human-disturbed forests — production forests, urban greenery, restoration plantings? Much popular literature assumes that promoting mycorrhizal connections is always beneficial for forest recovery, but if networks mainly work suppressively, this can produce misdirected policy.
