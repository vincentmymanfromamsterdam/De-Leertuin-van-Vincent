# The end of a black hole: Hawking evaporation and the final explosion

## Core
Black holes are not eternal. Through quantum effects at their event horizon they slowly lose mass via Hawking radiation, and the smaller they become, the hotter they radiate — a self-reinforcing feedback loop that ends in the final phase with an explosive burst of gamma radiation. For stellar-mass black holes that end lies 10⁶⁷ years in the future; for primordial black holes of the right original mass it should happen right now. That a symbol of gravitational dominance is ultimately defeated by microscopic quantum effects is perhaps the most beautiful paradox of modern physics.

## Explanation
Until the 1970s, a black hole seemed an endless endpoint. What fell in never came out; mass could only grow through accretion or mergers with other black holes. Stephen Hawking changed this in 1974 with a staggering prediction: through quantum effects at the event horizon, black holes emit **radiation** and slowly lose their mass.

The mechanism works as follows. At the horizon, virtual particle pairs constantly arise from quantum fluctuations of the vacuum. Normally these pairs immediately annihilate and produce no net effect. But at an event horizon, one particle can fall in while the other escapes. The escaped radiation carries energy away, and because energy and mass are equivalent via E = mc², the black hole shrinks. The technical description is subtler — it is not literally "a negative particle falls in" — but the net effect is correct: the black hole loses mass, the outside universe receives radiation.

Here comes the staggering property. The Hawking temperature is **inversely proportional** to mass. The smaller the black hole, the hotter the radiation it emits:

T = ℏc³ / (8π GM kᵦ)

For a stellar-mass black hole (10 solar masses) this comes to a Hawking temperature of about 6 nano-Kelvin. For Sagittarius A* (4 million solar masses) it is 10⁻¹⁴ Kelvin. Both are unimaginably colder than the cosmic microwave background at 2.7 K — meaning such black holes currently absorb more radiation from the universe than they emit. They grow heavier on net, not lighter.

But for a primordial black hole with the mass of an asteroid (~10¹² kg), the Hawking temperature is hundreds of billions of Kelvin. Such holes lose mass much faster than they gain, and that loss accelerates itself.

**The runaway feedback loop.** Here lies what is physically staggering. As mass is lost, temperature rises. As temperature rises, radiation intensity grows. As radiation intensity grows, mass is lost faster. It is a self-reinforcing cycle that exponentially accelerates in its final phase. A black hole that can radiate stably for a billion years ejects its last mass in seconds — as an explosion of extremely energetic gamma radiation. Some physicists call this a "Hawking explosion" or "primordial black hole explosion". Not an explosion like a chemical bomb, but a burst of pure radiation that literally marks the end of a black hole.

**Why this is fundamentally different from a supernova.** In a supernova, radiation pressure wins over gravity at the moment fusion in the core ends — iron cannot fuse further without costing energy, so counter-pressure disappears, outer layers collapse, bounce off the neutron core, and explode outward. There is a counter-acting force that won, and then lost.

In a Hawking explosion, there is no counter-acting force. The black hole loses mass not because gravity weakens, but because quantum radiation grows stronger as the black hole shrinks. It is more like a cooking pot set to setting 1 that gradually heats itself up and eventually jumps to setting 1000 — not because something turns up the heat, but because the pot itself becomes ever better at heating up as less water remains.

**A rough mass classification by timescales.** Different mass black holes have qualitatively different lifespans:

| Type | Mass | Hawking lifetime | Status now |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supermassive | 10⁶ - 10¹⁰ solar masses | 10⁹⁰ - 10¹⁰⁰ years | Still growing through background absorption |
| Stellar-mass | 3 - 100 solar masses | ~10⁶⁷ years | Still growing through background absorption |
| Intermediate | 100 - 10⁶ solar masses | 10⁶⁷ - 10⁹⁰ years | Comparable |
| Primordial heavy | >10¹⁵ kg | >10¹⁰ years | Possibly still exists, dark matter candidate |
| Primordial medium | ~10¹² kg | ~10¹⁰ years | Should be in final phase *now* |
| Primordial light | <10¹² kg | <10¹⁰ years | Already evaporated; flash somewhere in cosmic history |
| Sub-Planck | <10⁻⁸ kg | n/a | Probably do not exist classically |

For stellar-mass and supermassive holes the end phase lies so far in the future that it remains pure theory. For primordial black holes of precisely the right original mass — around 10¹² kg — the end phase would occur now, which is why experiments like the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope actively search for gamma flashes that could mark these final moments. So far no definitive detections, but several unexplained short flashes cannot be ruled out as candidates.

**What exactly happens at the end is theoretically uncertain.** In the very last moments, when the black hole approaches the Planck mass (~10⁻⁸ kg), our current theories break down. There, a full theory of **quantum gravity** would be needed to describe what happens. Some scenarios predict a tiny "Planck remnant" remains — a stable object at the quantum-gravity scale. Other scenarios predict all mass is fully converted to radiation. The question is further complicated by the **information paradox**: is the information about what once formed the black hole lost in evaporation, or does it return encoded in the Hawking radiation? Hawking himself shifted his position on this several times; a definitive answer probably requires the same quantum gravity theory that describes the final phase itself.

**The experimental status of Hawking radiation is uncomfortable.** It is one of the most celebrated predictions of twentieth-century physics, but has never been directly observed astrophysically. For stellar-mass holes the temperature is far too low — trillions of times colder than the background — to ever be detected. For primordial holes an evaporation flash would in principle be observable, but no confirmed detection yet. Analog experiments in liquid helium, sound waves and optical systems have found signals mathematically identical to Hawking radiation, supporting the theory without confirming the astrophysical version. It is a remarkable situation: a theoretically well-grounded prediction that experimentally lies beyond our current reach, but where the effort of searching is serious enough to dedicate fundamental observatory time to.

## Why this matters
The most striking thing about all this is that a black hole — the ultimate symbol of gravitational dominance, the object from which even light cannot escape — is ultimately not defeated by gravity but by quantum effects at its own horizon. The macroscopic strength of a black hole turns out, on long timescales, to be subordinate to microscopic processes we only began to understand in the second half of the twentieth century. Hawking evaporation thereby connects two parts of physics that elsewhere remain unreconciled: general relativity (which predicts black holes) and quantum field theory (which describes their evaporation). It is thus one of the rare places where these two theories together predict something concrete — and where their tension, in the form of the information paradox, becomes most sharply visible. Whether we will ever observe a gamma flash from an evaporating primordial black hole is uncertain, but the theoretical reality this prediction evokes — a universe in which everything eventually evaporates, including the blackest holes — revises our picture of what "eternal" means in physical terms.

## Open questions
- Will gamma flashes from evaporating primordial black holes be detected in the coming decades, or must we accept they did not exist in the right mass ranges?
- What happens physically in the very last moments when a black hole approaches the Planck mass? Does a stable "Planck remnant" remain, or does everything fully evaporate?
- Is information about the original composition of a black hole lost in evaporation (as Hawking initially predicted), or does it return encoded in the emitted radiation (as later analyses suggest)?
