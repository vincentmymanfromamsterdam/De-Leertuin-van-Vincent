# Axions and the strong CP problem - two puzzles in one particle

## Core
The **axion** is a hypothetical particle proposed in 1977 to solve a specific problem in the strong nuclear force — the so-called strong CP problem — and which gradually developed into one of the most important candidates for dark matter. What makes axions theoretically attractive is that they were not ad hoc postulated to explain dark matter, but follow from an independently motivated mechanism: the **Peccei-Quinn symmetry**. When this symmetry spontaneously breaks in the early universe, a light scalar field arises that automatically produces a cold-dark-matter component via the **misalignment mechanism**. The range of predicted masses runs from micro-electronvolts to milli-electronvolts, and experiments such as ADMX (since 1996) and MADMAX (first results 2025) are actively scanning this range. With the stagnation of the WIMP paradigm in recent years, axions have moved from a marginal option to the most seriously considered alternative dark-matter candidate — a shift that is theoretically and sociologically interesting.

## Explanation
In 1977 Roberto Peccei and Helen Quinn faced a problem different from dark matter. Their concern was a peculiar feature of the strong nuclear force, described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD). The Lagrangian of QCD contains a term that can in principle violate **CP symmetry** — that is, it could make the fundamental laws of strong interaction different for matter and antimatter, with a strength characterised by a parameter θ.

The problem is that θ must be virtually zero. Measurements of the electric dipole moment of the neutron give an upper limit of about 10⁻¹⁰ — a startlingly small number. Without explanation that is worrying, because there is no fundamental reason why θ would not lie somewhere around the order of one, like other parameters in nature. A dimensionless parameter that is so extremely small without consequence of symmetry calls for explanation.

This kind of fine-tuning problem feels unsatisfying to physicists. It looks like an accidental calibration that nature has set to precisely the right value, without a mechanism that brings it there. Compare it with the [[Cosmic inflation - the exponential expansion that shaped the universe|flatness problem]] of the universe before inflation: technically true but exceptionally strange without explanation.

**The Peccei-Quinn elegance.**

Peccei and Quinn proposed an elegant solution. What if θ is no fixed parameter, but a **dynamical variable**? Add a new global symmetry — U(1)_PQ — that spontaneously breaks at a certain energy scale f_a. According to Nambu and Goldstone, such spontaneous symmetry breaking produces a new, light scalar field that behaves as a pseudo-Nambu-Goldstone boson.

Frank Wilczek and Steven Weinberg independently realised that this field is actually a physical particle, and Wilczek named it **axion** — a reference to a detergent brand that would "clean up" what was dirty in QCD. The name stuck.

The beauty of this mechanism is that θ is no longer a fixed constant. The effective θ is now related to the value of the axion field divided by f_a. The axion field rolls to the minimum of its potential, and at that minimum the effective θ is automatically zero. The strong CP problem is solved not through fine-tuning but through a dynamical self-correction mechanism. Nature has, as it were, a built-in thermostat that brings θ to the right value.

**How it accidentally became dark matter.**

Here comes the unexpected part. When this theory is worked out in a cosmological context, something emerges that neither Peccei nor Quinn had initially foreseen.

In the early universe, just after [[Cosmic inflation - the exponential expansion that shaped the universe|inflation]] and before the QCD phase transition, the axion field is effectively massless. It takes a random initial value — the "misalignment angle" — because spontaneous symmetry breaking happened earlier and the field does not yet "know" where its equilibrium lies. From the perspective of the fundamental theory, this initial value is a free parameter determined by initial conditions.

When the universe cools to the QCD phase transition — about 10⁻⁵ seconds after the Big Bang — something crucial happens. Non-perturbative QCD effects suddenly give the axion a mass. The field now sees that it is not at its minimum, and starts to **oscillate** around that minimum, like a pendulum released from its rest position.

Here lies the key. An oscillating scalar field behaves cosmologically **exactly like cold dark matter**. The energy density of the oscillations scales with 1/a³ (where a is the scale factor) — exactly the same as massive, non-relativistic particles. The field is pressureless, non-relativistic, and interacts only gravitationally with the rest of the universe. It looks like dark matter, behaves like dark matter, and can be produced in the right amount to explain the observed dark-matter density.

This mechanism is called **misalignment**, and it is startling in its naturalness. You do not need to postulate new particle production, choose no specific scenario — if the PQ symmetry spontaneously breaks and the axion field has a random initial value, then a quantity of axion dark matter **must** arise. The only question is how much, and that depends on the PQ scale f_a and the misalignment angle.

For f_a around 10¹¹ to 10¹² GeV — which gives the axion a mass of about 1 to 100 micro-electronvolts — production exactly matches the observed dark-matter density. No fine-tuning needed. It fits spontaneously.

**Two problems for the price of one.**

This is precisely what makes axions so theoretically attractive. They are **not ad hoc postulated** to explain dark matter, like many other candidates. They follow from an independently motivated mechanism that solves the strong CP problem, and upon continuation turn out to also be able to provide dark matter. One new hypothesis solves two different problems at different scale levels of physics.

Compare this with WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles) — the main candidate of recent decades. WIMPs were proposed from the idea that if heavy particles with weak interaction exist, their thermal production automatically yields the right dark-matter density (the "WIMP miracle"). But these particles have no independent motivation — supersymmetry predicts them, but supersymmetry itself is motivated by other problem solutions. Axions have a more direct theoretical basis: they solve a concrete, measurable problem in QCD.

**Why the current popularity.**

Several factors make axions more popular now than ever.

The WIMP paradigm is faltering. Two decades of direct-detection experiments — XENON, LUX, PandaX, and their successors — have found no WIMPs, and the exclusion limits are becoming ever tighter. The most natural parameter space has by now been excluded. WIMPs are not impossible, but the most "elegant" versions are gone, and what remains requires increasing fine-tuning. This forces physicists to take alternatives more seriously.

At the same time, the experimental technology for axion detection has matured. Pierre Sikivie proposed in 1983 detecting axions through their conversion to photons under a strong magnetic field — the **haloscope** principle. Under the influence of a static magnetic field, an axion field oscillation can faintly produce electromagnetic radiation. Place that conversion in a resonant cavity tuned to precisely the right frequency, and the signal amplifies to something potentially measurable.

Forty years later, this technique is finally advanced enough to test realistic axion couplings. No longer just exotic models, but the most natural versions of the theory.

**The current experiments.**

ADMX at the University of Washington is the longest-running haloscope experiment, since 1996. It scans low-mass axions (1-40 μeV) with a superconducting cavity in an 8 Tesla magnetic field. ADMX has by now reached the KSVZ coupling boundary for parts of the mass spectrum — sensitivity is now high enough to test standard theoretical predictions, not just extreme variants.

A newer player is MADMAX (MAgnetized Disk and Mirror Axion eXperiment), a German-French collaboration. In July 2025 MADMAX published its first results, based on prototype measurements at CERN with the Morpurgo magnet. Over a data collection period of 14.5 days, the mass range around 76.7 to 79.4 μeV was scanned — no axion signal was detected, but a 95% confidence limit was set that exceeds the previous constraints in this range.

MADMAX uses a fundamentally different technique than ADMX — no resonant cavity but a dielectric haloscope with stacked sapphire disks. For higher axion masses, the ADMX cavity approach becomes technically untenable (the cavity must become ever smaller, weakening the signal), so MADMAX offers complementary coverage for the 40-400 μeV range. The full MADMAX facility is expected to be operational in 2026-2029, during a scheduled LHC shutdown.

In addition, experiments like ABRACADABRA, HAYSTAC, ORGAN, and a large number of smaller searches run with different techniques for different mass ranges. The field is fragmented but lively, and the scan rate is increasing yearly as detector sensitivity improves.

**Open theoretical problems.**

Three issues deserve attention.

First the **coupling strength**. Two main families of axion models exist — KSVZ (Kim-Shifman-Vainshtein-Zakharov) and DFSZ (Dine-Fischler-Srednicki-Zhitnitsky) — that make different predictions about how strongly axions couple to photons. This difference determines whether current experiments are sensitive enough. Current limits are starting to penetrate the KSVZ region for some mass ranges, marking a serious stage: experiments now test realistic theoretical predictions, not just extreme variants.

Second, the question **pre- or post-inflationary PQ breaking?** Whether the Peccei-Quinn symmetry broke before or after inflation makes considerable difference. In the pre-inflationary scenario, the entire observable space has one single misalignment angle, and production is easy to calculate. In the post-inflationary scenario, topological defects (axion strings and domain walls) produce extra dark matter, and the total story becomes much more complex. Recent work from 2026 showed that in the post-inflationary scenario the amount of dark matter is determined not only by QCD dynamics but also by subtle PQ symmetry breaking effects that can disturb the "classical" prediction.

Third, the **axiverse**. String theory typically predicts not one axion but a whole family of axion-like particles (ALPs — Axion-Like Particles) with different masses and coupling strengths. This "axiverse" scenario means that dark matter may consist of multiple ALPs at once, with different cosmological signatures. It makes the search more complicated but also richer — some ALPs can be observed via entirely different mechanisms, such as cooling of stars or supernova physics.

**Connection with the broader physics cluster.**

Axions connect in several ways with themes treated elsewhere in this cluster. They are a product of **spontaneous symmetry breaking**, comparable to Higgs but then for U(1)_PQ instead of SU(2)×U(1). Their mass arises via a **phase transition** in the early universe (the QCD transition), directly connecting them to the broader history of symmetry breakings in cosmic evolution. Their role as dark matter via the misalignment mechanism illustrates how an oscillating scalar field behaves cosmologically — which also lies behind inflation, with the inflaton as another scalar field that temporarily plays a cosmological role.

Compared with [[Neutrinos - three light particles possibly a heavy counterpart and the seesaw elegance|neutrinos]], axions form an interesting contrast. Both are light particles that can in principle constitute dark matter, but via very different mechanisms: neutrinos via thermal production (warm dark matter), axions via misalignment oscillations (cold dark matter). The experimental challenge in both cases is comparable — detecting minuscule interaction signals against overwhelming background — but the techniques are totally different.

## Why this matters
Axions are a beautiful example of how theoretical physics works at its best. A specific problem in QCD calls for explanation; an elegant solution is proposed; this solution turns out unexpectedly to also be able to solve another, independent problem. That is no coincidence — it is the way deeper theoretical structures show themselves by touching multiple phenomena at once. When one mechanism solves two independently motivated problems, our justified belief in the correctness of that mechanism increases considerably, because the chance that the same arbitrary adjustment solves two problems at once is much smaller than that it does so for one problem. This is the logic of what philosophers call "inference to the best explanation", and it is a strong argument for axions despite the fact that they have not yet been experimentally confirmed. Moreover, the axion story shows something about how science moves through paradigm shifts. The WIMP hypothesis dominated thinking about dark matter for decades, partly because it fit into a broader framework (supersymmetry) that was pursued independently. When that framework began to encounter experimental setbacks, alternatives were taken more seriously — not because axion theory improved but because the competing theory weakened. This is normal science, not revolution: alternatives become serious when the prevailing position loses its lead. Whether axions will be experimentally confirmed within a decade we do not know. What we do know is that current experimental effort is large enough to give a verdict within foreseeable time on the most natural versions of the theory. For those who want to see how particle physics works at the limits of what is detectable, this is one of the most exciting stories to follow.

## Open questions
- What mass range do axions actually contain? The theoretically allowed range extends from micro- to milli-electronvolts, and scanning this entire range requires different experimental techniques. No current experiment can cover the whole range; we need a whole generation of experiments that complement each other.
- Do axions really solve the strong CP problem, or are there complications we do not yet understand? Recent theoretical work on explicit PQ symmetry breaking and the "companion axion" scenario suggests the problem may be subtler than originally thought.
- How does the axion hypothesis relate to other dark-matter candidates? If we find axions, that does not definitively rule out neutrinos or WIMPs — dark matter can be a mix of components. Disentangling such a mixed composition would require a new round of experiments.
