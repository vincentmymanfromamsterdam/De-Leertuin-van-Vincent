# Hot, warm or cold: the search for what dark matter actually is

## Core
Dark matter is not a homogeneous concept. Physicists distinguish three temperature categories — hot, warm and cold — based on how relativistic the particles were when they decoupled from the cosmic plasma in the early universe. That speed determines on what scale dark matter can clump, and thereby which structures could form in the universe. Observations point predominantly to cold dark matter, but the exact candidate — WIMPs, axions, sterile neutrinos, or something still unknown — remains open after fifty years of searching.

## Explanation
We know *that* dark matter exists from its gravitational effects on large scales (see [[The dark universe - why there are two mysteries|the dark universe]]). What we do not know is *what* it is. It is not ordinary matter made of protons and neutrons — for that the observations are too sharp. It must be something new: a particle or class of particles that experiences gravity but barely or not at all interacts via electromagnetism.

To distil a serious list from the infinite space of conceivable candidates, physicists use the **temperature classification**. It rests on one simple observation: how fast a dark matter particle moved in the early universe determines on what scale it can clump under gravity. Fast-moving particles escape from small gravitational wells before those wells can grow. Slow-moving particles fall into all wells, even the smallest.

A particle is **relativistic** when it moves at speeds that are a significant fraction of the speed of light. More precisely: relativistic when its kinetic energy is roughly equal to or greater than its rest mass energy (mc²). A light particle becomes relativistic more easily than a heavy particle, because its rest mass energy is smaller. That is why mass is so central to the classification.

**Hot dark matter.** Light particles (less than a few electron-volts) that were extremely relativistic at decoupling — moving at speeds very close to the speed of light. Ordinary neutrinos fall in this category. At their decoupling, about one second after the Big Bang, they had typical energies of a few megaelectron-volts and masses of less than an electron-volt.

**Warm dark matter.** Particles with masses in the keV range (1,000 to 100,000 electron-volts) that were somewhat relativistic at decoupling — speeds between 10% and 90% of the speed of light. Sterile neutrinos in the right mass range would fall here.

**Cold dark matter.** Heavy particles (GeV range or higher) that were non-relativistic at decoupling — moving at speeds much smaller than the speed of light. Hypothetical WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles) and axions usually fall in this category.

The names do not refer to the current state but to the speed of motion at the crucial phase in the early universe. Now all dark matter particles have cooled through cosmic expansion, but the fact that they once were relativistic (or not) determined which cosmic structures could form.

**How this plays out for structure formation.** With hot dark matter, small structures cannot begin to grow until the particles have sufficiently cooled through cosmic expansion. When they finally cool, they have already erased the small density fluctuations. Structure formation then runs "top-down" — first gigantic superclusters, then fragmentation into galaxies. With cold dark matter the reverse happens: small lumps form first, which clump together into larger structures. What we observe — galaxies that are older than the largest clusters — fits cold, not hot. Pure hot dark matter is therefore excluded as an explanation.

With warm dark matter you are in between. The particles suppress structure formation on a specific scale — not as radically as hot, but noticeably for the smallest galaxies. The effect is called the "free-streaming length" — the distance a particle travels before being slow enough to fall into gravitational wells. For a keV particle that distance would be about 10,000 light-years, corresponding to the scale of dwarf galaxies.

**The current candidates and their status.** The standard cold dark matter model works excellently on large scales, but on small scales there are persistent tensions between predictions and observations. Predicted dwarf galaxies are missing (the *missing satellites problem*), the largest subhalos seem not to form the visible galaxies they should (the *too big to fail* problem), and the central density profiles of small galaxies are flatter than predicted (the *core-cusp* problem). Warm dark matter of the right mass range could mitigate all three problems at once, which keeps the search for such particles interesting.

Four candidates are currently most serious:

- **WIMPs** were the favourite for decades — heavy particles (10 to 1000 GeV) predicted by supersymmetric theories. But the LHC has found no supersymmetric particles in the expected mass range, and direct detection experiments (XENON, LUX, PandaX) have drastically shrunk the allowed parameter space. WIMPs are not excluded but heavily constrained.
- **Axions** are ultralight particles (mass around a microelectron-volt) originally predicted to explain a separate asymmetry in the strong nuclear force. They are actively sought by experiments such as ADMX in the US. Currently probably the most popular alternative candidate.
- **Sterile neutrinos** in the keV range would provide warm dark matter. A possible hint — an unexplained 3.5 keV X-ray line — was found in 2014, but later analyses strongly question the neutrino interpretation. Future X-ray telescopes such as XRISM (2023) and Athena (2030s) should definitively clarify this.
- **Primordial black holes** from the early universe could provide dark matter without new particle physics — just massive black holes of specific size. Some mass ranges are already excluded, but a window remains open.

**What stands out is that we still have no positive detection after fifty years of searching.** Every "discovery" has so far been withdrawn after closer analysis. That can mean dark matter is harder to catch than we thought, that we are searching in the wrong places, or that the actual solution is something we have not yet conceived. Some physicists seriously investigate alternatives such as modified gravity (MOND, TeVeS), although these have problems on large scales that dark matter neatly solves. Others speculate about a whole "dark sector" with its own particles and forces — not one particle but shadow physics alongside ours.

## Why this matters
Dark matter is not an unimportant detail of cosmology. It makes up about 85% of all matter in the universe and has largely shaped the structure we see today. Understanding what it is touches the most fundamental question in modern physics: does physics beyond the Standard Model exist, and if so, what does it look like? The temperature classification is a fairly elegant filter — from the infinite space of conceivable particles we can rule out a large part through observation of cosmic structure. But every decade we search without definitive proof, doubt grows about whether we are asking the right question. The search for dark matter is therefore not just a search for a particle, but also a test of how good our theoretical lenses are — how well we can predict what nature can show us before she shows it.

## Open questions
- Is dark matter one particle species or an entire "dark sector" with its own particles and forces?
- What explains the 3.5 keV X-ray line if it is not a sterile neutrino — an instrumental error, an atomic transition, or something else?
- How much longer can we search without positive detection before we have to seriously reconsider the basic assumptions — does dark matter exist as a particle at all, or does the phenomenon require something fundamentally different?
