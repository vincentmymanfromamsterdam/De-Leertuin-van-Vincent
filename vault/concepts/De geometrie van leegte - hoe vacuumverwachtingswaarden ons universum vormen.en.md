# The geometry of emptiness: how vacuum expectation values shape our universe

## Core
What we experience as "natural laws" — mass, the distinction between forces, the structure of matter — are partly not fixed properties of nature but configurations of fields in their ground state. The Lagrangian of the Standard Model is more symmetric than our world appears; the apparent asymmetry arises because the Higgs field has "chosen" a specific direction in the space of possible vacuum states. At different temperatures, fields select different ground states, and thereby what is effectively possible in the universe changes. Our world is in a sense the geometry of emptiness.

## Explanation
In modern physics, the fundamental building blocks of the universe are not particles but **fields** — mathematical objects that have a value at every point in spacetime. The electromagnetic field, the electron field, the Higgs field: each has a value at every point, and particles are excitations of these fields. A photon is a ripple in the electromagnetic field, an electron is a ripple in the electron field. The quantum vacuum is what remains when all excitations are removed — but the fields themselves remain, with their ground state values.

For most fields that ground state value is zero. But for the Higgs field that value is **not zero**. In our universe the Higgs field has, everywhere and always, a vacuum expectation value of about 246 GeV. That is a constant background permeating all space, comparable to how water surrounds a fish without the fish noticing.

Every particle moving through space moves through this Higgs field. Some particles interact strongly with the field (top quark), others weakly (electron), others not at all (photon). The strength of that interaction *is* what we observe as mass. A particle with mass is literally a particle that has to swim against the current of the Higgs field to move. That is no metaphor; it is the literal physical description.

Above the electroweak temperature of 10¹⁵ K, the vacuum expectation value of the Higgs field was zero. Not because the field was not there, but because its ground state was symmetric. At that temperature all particles moved through a field that on average had no net influence — they were effectively massless. Below that temperature the symmetric state became energetically unfavourable. The Higgs field "rolled" into a new ground state with a non-zero vacuum expectation value. From that moment all particles interacting with the Higgs field felt a constant background current against which they must move.

Here lies the staggering insight. **The Lagrangian itself — the mathematical description of the natural laws — did not change at this transition.** What changed was which ground state the universe occupies within those laws. A physicist above the electroweak temperature would observe an SU(2)×U(1) gauge theory with four massless gauge bosons. A physicist below it observes electromagnetism (one massless photon) plus the weak nuclear force (three massive bosons). The same underlying physics, two completely different observations. The difference lies not in the laws but in which vacuum configuration the world occupies.

This pattern is not unique to the Higgs field. At the GUT transition the strong nuclear force separated from the electroweak force because another field acquired a non-zero vacuum expectation value. At the chiral symmetry breaking, a combination of quark fields acquired a non-zero vacuum value that confines quarks inside protons and neutrons rather than letting them roam free. Each time the same mechanism: a field that had a symmetric ground state above a critical temperature finds an asymmetric ground state energetically favourable below it, and chooses one. See [[Phase transitions of the early universe - a cascade of broken symmetries|the cascade of phase transitions]] for the full sequence.

Which configuration the universe chose is in a sense arbitrary. In the mathematics of the Higgs field there is a symmetric ring of possible ground states — the so-called Mexican hat potential — and the universe selected one. Different regions of the universe could in principle have made different choices. Some inflation models predict this also happened in regions far beyond the observable universe.

**What makes this so confusing is a subtle philosophical point.** For centuries we were used to thinking that the natural laws *themselves* were asymmetric — that the photon is fundamentally different from the W boson, that matter is fundamentally different from antimatter, that forces are fundamentally distinct. The realisation of the twentieth century is that the underlying laws are much more symmetric than they appear, and that the apparent asymmetry of the world is a product of which ground state the universe happens to occupy. Our entire rich, structured world is in a sense an accidental product of which configuration fields chose when the universe cooled.

This explains why cosmological transitions are not simply "different temperature" but actually create a different physical reality. In ordinary phase transitions such as water freezing, the underlying physics remains identical; only the macroscopic arrangement changes. In cosmic symmetry breaking, what effective physics is possible at all changes. In the symmetric phase, certain structures simply do not exist — no mass, no distinct forces, no stable atoms. Only in a broken-symmetry configuration does complex structure become possible.

**A sobering consequence:** if our current vacuum is not the true ground state, it could in principle still change. Precision measurements of the Higgs and top quark masses suggest our vacuum might be metastable — a local energy minimum with possibly a deeper one beneath. The calculated timescales for such a transition lie between 10¹⁰⁰ and 10⁶⁰⁰ years, unimaginably long but in principle finite. If it spontaneously happened anywhere in the universe, the "bubble" of new vacuum configuration would expand at the speed of light, and within that bubble the natural constants would be different from what we know. Our world bathes in a Higgs field whose definitive ground state we cannot be sure has been reached.

The deeper observation is that much of what we consider "fundamental" — mass, distinctions between forces, the structure of matter — is in fact the geometry of a background field, not a property of nature itself. Anaximander's Apeiron, Aristotle's prima materia, and modern physics' quantum vacuum are three attempts to point at the same thing (see [[Apeiron, prima materia, quantum vacuum - the search for the primal substrate]]). What modern physics adds to that old intuition is concrete and astonishing: the substrate is not formless, it is a configuration. And configurations can change.

## Why this matters
This insight revises what we understand by "natural laws". The laws themselves — the mathematical structure of the Lagrangian — are as far as we can tell stable and eternal. But what we experience as the physical world is the product of those laws *plus* the specific configuration the fields occupy. Our constants, our particles, our forces are not the natural laws themselves but consequences of a configuration the universe selected during cooling. That places our position as observers in a new perspective: we describe nature not as it necessarily is but as it has happened to become in our corner of reality. Other regions of the universe, or other phases of the same universe in the distant future, could exhibit fundamentally different physics without the underlying laws having changed. That is perhaps the most striking insight of twentieth-century physics: that eternity and contingency can coexist in the same reality, and that our entire world is in a sense an accidental occupant of one configuration of emptiness.

## Open questions
- Is our current vacuum the true ground state, or do we sit in a metastable local minimum? Current measurements of the Higgs and top quark masses possibly suggest the latter, but the calculation rests on assumptions about physics beyond what we can experimentally test.
- Are there regions in a larger multiverse where fields selected different vacuum configurations, with different natural constants and possibly fundamentally different effective physics?
- Does dark energy — which behaves like a constant vacuum energy — change over time, or is it truly a fixed property of space? The answer to this question partly determines the distant future of the universe.
