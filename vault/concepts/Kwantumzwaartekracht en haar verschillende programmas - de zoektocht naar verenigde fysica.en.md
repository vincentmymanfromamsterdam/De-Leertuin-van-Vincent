# Quantum gravity and its various programmes - the search for unified physics

## Core
General relativity describes gravity as geometry of a classical spacetime; quantum field theory describes the other fundamental forces as interactions of quantum fields. Both theories are empirically extraordinarily successful in their own domain but irreconcilable in their fundamental structure. A **theory of quantum gravity** would have to unify both — typically by quantising gravity itself or by finding a deeper foundation from which both classical spacetime and quantum fields emerge. After nearly a century of work, various serious programmes have been developed — string theory, loop quantum gravity, asymptotic safety, causal dynamical triangulations, causal sets, and emergent-spacetime approaches — that each tackle the problem with different strategies. None of these has been experimentally confirmed; the relevant energy scale (Planck energy, 10¹⁹ GeV) lies presumably forever outside direct laboratory reach. Recently however we see a remarkable **convergence** between different programmes via insights from quantum information theory and holography, with the central idea that spacetime itself may not be a fundamental entity but emerges from deeper-lying quantum entanglement. This changes the nature of what a "theory of quantum gravity" should be.

## Explanation
The problem of quantum gravity is in essence simple to state: our best theory of gravity (general relativity) and our best theory of all other forces (quantum field theory) are mathematically irreconcilable in extreme situations. General relativity assumes that spacetime is a smooth classical background; quantum field theory assumes that everything physical must be quantised. When we want to apply both simultaneously — at Planck-scale energy, inside black holes, or in the very early universe — we get mathematical nonsense: infinities that cannot be removed via standard renormalisation.

For most physics this inability is no practical problem. At energies we can produce in laboratories, gravity is so weak compared to other forces that we can simply ignore its quantum character. But conceptually it is a fundamental shortcoming. A complete theory of nature should encompass both aspects of reality, and the fact that we cannot formulate that suggests we are missing something deeper.

## The Planck scale as goal

The energy scale where quantum gravity becomes indispensable is the **Planck scale**, characterised by the Planck energy of about 1.22 × 10¹⁹ GeV and the corresponding Planck length of 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ metres. This scale is about 10¹⁵ times higher than what LHC can reach and about 10²⁰ times smaller than a proton. It is the scale where all theoretical programmes predict their specific effects manifest.

The problem is practically unreachable for direct experiment. A hypothetical particle accelerator that could reach Planck energy via current technology would have to be approximately the size of the solar system. Indirect effects — cosmic radiation, early-universe signatures, black-hole physics — offer our only experimental access, and even that is extremely limited. This makes quantum gravity research largely **theoretically driven**, with empirical limitations that provide more constraints than confirmations.

## String theory

The oldest and most elaborated programme is **string theory**, originating in the 1970s and heavily developed in the 1980s and 1990s. The basic idea is startlingly simple: replace point particles with one-dimensional vibrating **strings**. The different vibration modes of a string correspond to different particles — different masses, charges, spins. One of those modes turns out automatically to be a massless spin-2 particle, which has precisely the properties of the graviton. String theory thus produces **gravity automatically** instead of postulating it, which attracted many theorists.

The price is high. String theory requires for mathematical consistency ten (or in its supergravity extension M-theory eleven) spacetime dimensions. The extra six or seven dimensions must be compactified on a microscopic scale that we cannot observe. Exactly how that compactification happens — the "shape" the extra dimensions have taken — determines what low-energy physics we observe. The problem is that there is an enormous number of possible compactifications, estimated to be of order 10⁵⁰⁰ different possibilities — the notorious "string landscape". The theory thereby makes no unique prediction for low-energy physics, and can in principle produce virtually any conceivable particle physics by choosing the right compactification.

String theory has nevertheless provided deep insights. The **AdS/CFT correspondence**, discovered by Juan Maldacena in 1997, proved a deep equivalence between quantum gravity in anti-de Sitter space and a quantum field theory without gravity at the boundary of that space. This is more than a mathematical curiosity — it is the first explicit example of **holography**, the idea that a theory with gravity in d+1 dimensions can be equivalent to a theory without gravity in d dimensions. String theory has also provided microscopic explanations of the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of certain black holes, an important consistency check.

Critics point out that string theory has not produced a testable prediction after fifty years, that the landscape problem undermines predictive power, and that the required extra dimensions and supersymmetry have received no empirical confirmation. The programme remains dominant in academic research but is less self-evident than it was a few decades ago.

## Loop quantum gravity

A radically different approach is **loop quantum gravity** (LQG), developed especially by Carlo Rovelli, Lee Smolin and Abhay Ashtekar from the late 1980s. Instead of starting with particles in a spacetime background, LQG tries to directly quantise spacetime itself — **without** a pre-given background. This is a fundamental difference: string theory postulates a ten-dimensional spacetime in which strings move; LQG tries to build up spacetime from fundamental building blocks.

The result is a discrete spacetime picture at the Planck scale. Space is built from "spin networks" — abstract graphs whose nodes and lines quanta of space (volume and area) correspond. Volume and area have fundamental minimum sizes, comparable to how energy levels of an atom are quantised. Classical smooth spacetime then emerges as emergent description when you average over enormous numbers of spin-network elements.

LQG works in ordinary 3+1 dimensions — no extra dimensions, no supersymmetry required. It is **background-independent**, which many theorists find elegant: the geometry is not a pre-given setting but emerges from quantum dynamics. An interesting consequence is that the Big Bang singularity in LQG cosmology (LQC) is often replaced by a "big bounce" — a finite transition from an earlier universe to the current one, without the real infinity of the classical description.

The problems of LQG lie elsewhere. It is difficult to show that the programme reproduces general relativity in the classical limit — this crucial consistency criterion has not yet been definitively established. The theory has not yet developed a convincing quantum dynamics for matter alongside gravity. And like string theory it has no experimental confirmation, although there are recent proposals for CMB signatures or black-hole evidence.

## Asymptotic safety

A third important programme is **asymptotic safety**, originally proposed by Steven Weinberg in 1979 but only from the 1990s seriously elaborated by Martin Reuter and others. The starting point is counterintuitive: perhaps gravity **is** a normal quantum field theory, just not renormalisable in the standard perturbative sense. The quantum theory of gravity could have a **non-Gaussian fixed point** in the renormalisation group flow, an ultraviolet fixed point that makes the theory predictive at high energies without introducing new particles or structures.

This programme is in a sense the most conservative approach: it holds onto standard quantum field theory and ordinary spacetime, and tries to solve the renormalisation problem in a non-perturbative way. Recent numerical calculations with functional renormalisation group techniques have produced evidence for the existence of such a fixed point, both with and without matter fields. Whether this evidence is definitive is contested — the calculations require approximations whose validity at the relevant scale is uncertain.

Asymptotic safety has the attractive property of being directly connected to effective field theory methods we use for all other physics. Critics argue that it perhaps does not go far enough — that the real solution requires structural change unavailable within the framework of quantum field theory.

## Discrete approaches: causal dynamical triangulations and causal sets

Two related programmes assume that spacetime at the Planck scale is fundamentally **discrete**.

**Causal dynamical triangulations** (CDT), developed from 1998 by Renate Loll, Jan Ambjørn and Jerzy Jurkiewicz, builds up spacetime from simple discrete building blocks (simplices) and sums over all possible configurations, with causal order preserved. Numerical simulations show that this programme on large scale automatically produces four-dimensional, flat spacetime — a remarkable result for a theory beginning with arbitrary discrete configurations.

**Causal set theory** (especially developed by Rafael Sorkin) goes even more radically. Here spacetime is fundamentally a **partially ordered set** of events — a set of points with only the relation "this event comes before that event". No size, no metric, no geometry at fundamental level. Classical spacetime with all its geometrical properties then emerges as statistical approximation of an ultimately discrete causal pattern.

Both programmes are mathematically elegant and philosophically interesting, but empirically virtually untested. They remain minority approaches compared to string theory and LQG, although their ideas increasingly influence other programmes.

## The holographic revolution and emergent spacetime

The most exciting recent development is perhaps the insight that spacetime itself may not be fundamental, but **emerges** from deeper-lying quantum degrees of freedom that have no spacetime structure.

The idea traces back to the **holographic principle** of Gerard 't Hooft (1993) and Leonard Susskind (1995): the maximum information that fits in a spacetime region is proportional to its surface area, not its volume. This suggests that physics in d dimensions can essentially be encoded in d-1 dimensions — a thought that was made explicit through AdS/CFT correspondence.

Mark Van Raamsdonk's work from 2010 has argued that **spacetime is made from entanglement** — that the connectedness between different spacetime regions just is their quantum entanglement, and that spacetime itself falls apart if that entanglement disappears. Later developments via "entanglement wedge reconstruction" and "complexity geometry" have further elaborated this picture. According to an increasing number of researchers, the right way to look at quantum gravity is not "how do we quantise spacetime?" but "how does spacetime emerge from non-spatial quantum degrees of freedom?"

This changes the nature of the whole programme. Not all programmes accept this turn — LQG and asymptotic safety remain closer to traditional quantisation approaches — but the holographic angle has gained so much theoretical traction that in 2026 it counts as one of the central paradigms.

A corresponding recent insight: the [[The end of a black hole - why evaporation need not be a mystery|black-hole information paradox]] we discussed earlier has been substantially resolved in 2019-2020 via "replica wormhole" calculations that make use of precisely these emergent-spacetime ideas. This is a direct application of quantum information theory to quantum gravity, and is seen by many as the first real progress in this direction in decades.

## Empirical limitations

What all programmes have in common is that none has been experimentally confirmed. The questions remain whether we can ever test which programme is correct. Various strategies are being investigated:

**Black hole physics.** Different programmes predict different microscopic descriptions of black holes, with different end stages of Hawking evaporation. Future gravitational-wave observations of late-stage mergers could in principle distinguish.

**Cosmological signatures.** The very early universe offers natural access to Planck-scale physics. Loop quantum cosmology predicts specific modifications of CMB spectra that are in principle measurable. String cosmology can make predictions via "swampland" criteria constraining low-energy physics.

**Lorentz-invariance tests.** Various theories predict subtle violations of Lorentz invariance at high energy. Observations of extremely high-energy cosmic radiation already strongly constrain these violations; future observations may go further.

**Tabletop experiments with massive quantum superpositions.** Recent proposals suggest we could produce quantum superpositions of macroscopic masses and measure their gravitational field. A successful observation of a **quantised** gravitational field would be direct evidence that gravity itself must be quantised, regardless of which specific theory proves correct.

Still the empirical situation remains precarious. It is well possible that none of these tests will be able to definitively choose between the main candidates in the coming decades.

## Why this matters
Quantum gravity is perhaps the most fundamental open problem in modern physics. The question what gravity and quantum mechanics mean together is not only technical but conceptual: it touches on what spacetime really is, how causality works at the most basic scale, and whether the smooth geometric reality we experience is even fundamental or just a macroscopic appearance. What makes this research area special is that it is simultaneously extraordinarily technical and extraordinarily philosophical. String theory requires the highest level of modern mathematics; LQG touches on deep questions about what it means for spacetime to be "quantum"; emergent-spacetime approaches open new philosophical questions about the ontological status of space and time. Moreover, quantum gravity illustrates a broader scientific reality we have discussed elsewhere in this cluster: that apparent irreconcilability between successful theories sometimes reveals a deeper underlying structure. Not by declaring the existing theories wrong — general relativity and quantum field theory remain empirically extraordinarily successful — but by showing that both are effective descriptions of something more fundamental. The current convergence of different programmes around holography and emergent spacetime suggests we may be making progress in this direction, even without a definitive theory. This is also a good touchstone for the methodological sharpness developed in our other discussions. Many quantum-gravity programmes face serious [[Valid versus useful - why an irrefutable argument need not explain anything|valid-versus-useful]] problems — string theory's landscape, LQG's classical-limit problem, asymptotic safety's dependence on approximations. They are all serious scientific programmes, but for some the distance between "mathematically consistent" and "empirically testable" is so large that the dividing line with speculation becomes thin. This is no criticism of the work but a sober observation about the limits of what we currently know.

## Open questions
- Which approach — if any single one — is ultimately correct, or is a synthesis of different programmes the ultimate theory? Recent convergence via holography suggests the latter, but this is not yet definitively established.
- How do we test quantum-gravity predictions empirically? The Planck scale appears unreachable; we need new strategies that can detect indirect signatures or low-energy effects. Tabletop quantum-gravity experiments are a promising new direction.
- Is spacetime really emergent from quantum entanglement? If yes, what is the "backgroundless" level from which spacetime arises? AdS/CFT gives an example here for anti-de Sitter space, but our universe seems rather de Sitter-like, and the corresponding holographic description is much less understood.

- Joseph Polchinski, *String Theory* (vol. 1 & 2, 1998) — classic textbook on string theory, technical but definitive
- Sean Carroll, *Something Deeply Hidden* (2019) — accessible explanation with attention to the relationship between quantum mechanics, spacetime and information
