# Leibniz and the monads: the best of all possible worlds

## Core
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716) was perhaps the most universal mind of the seventeenth century — mathematician, jurist, diplomat, theologian, and philosopher in one person. Independently of Newton he developed calculus, and his philosophical system postulated a reality consisting of infinitely many **monads** — simple, independent substances without parts, without windows to each other. How these monads still form a coherent world he explained via the **pre-established harmony**: God had coordinated them so that they evolve in parallel without ever interacting with each other. And because God is perfect, we live in the **best of all possible worlds**. Russell has a dual picture: he highly values Leibniz' mathematical and logical work, but finds his metaphysics either brilliant or absurd, depending on which version you read — and he discovered something fascinating about the underlying structure.

## Explanation
Leibniz was the son of a Leipzig professor and proved early a prodigy. He studied law, philosophy and mathematics and worked mainly as diplomat and court librarian for the dukes of Hanover. He wrote mainly in letters and short pieces — no large magnum opus like Descartes or Spinoza, but thousands of letters, memoranda and notes that have only been gradually ordered posthumously. Russell points out that this decentralised oeuvre complicates the interpretation of Leibniz: there is no authoritative text saying what he really thought.

**Russell's central discovery.** As a young philosopher Russell made a remarkable discovery about Leibniz that colours his treatment. When Russell first studied Leibniz' unpublished writings around 1900, he discovered that there were in fact **two Leibnizes**. The public Leibniz wrote for princes and theologians about monads, pre-established harmony and the best of all possible worlds — a metaphysics that was popular but according to Russell untenable on specific points. The private Leibniz, in his notes and philosophical diaries, developed a much sharper and more consistent logical analysis that was partly in conflict with the public version. Russell concluded that Leibniz hid his best work out of caution for his patrons and the church.

This discovery makes it difficult to speak of "Leibniz' philosophy" without specifying which version. The popular monad doctrine is better known; the logical analyses Russell discovered are deeper. Both aspects deserve attention.

**The monads.** Leibniz' most famous concept are the **monads** — fundamental substances of reality. A monad is a unit (hence the name, from the Greek *monas*), without parts, without extension. Every monad is a kind of spiritual atom — not material but real, and each has a kind of perception or consciousness, although very simple in most monads.

The world consists of an infinite number of these monads. A stone is a collection of very simple monads. An animal is a group of monads with a dominant "soul monad". A human is a highly developed monad surrounded by billions of lower monads forming his body. No monad can change anything about another — Leibniz even says monads "have no windows" — but each reflects the entire universe from its own perspective.

Russell finds this a strange result. How can independent monads reach a coherent world if they cannot interact with each other? Leibniz' answer is the **pre-established harmony**: God has programmed every monad so that their perspectives match each other. When my hand touches the glass and the glass falls, it seems as if my hand causally moved the glass, but in reality both monads evolved independently according to their own pre-established programme. Russell compares this with two clocks God has wound in perfect synchronisation — they run alike not because they communicate with each other, but because they were set up that way from the beginning.

**The best of all possible worlds.** Here lies Leibniz' most famous and most mocked claim. If God is perfect — almighty, all-knowing, all-good — he must have chosen the best from infinitely many possible worlds. So we live in the best of all possible worlds. Suffering, evil and pain exist, but every alternative would have contained even more suffering, evil or pain, or would have had to lack other important goods.

Voltaire put this thesis to shame in *Candide* (1759), where the naive Pangloss (a caricature of Leibniz) maintains that everything is for the best while earthquakes, wars and plagues happen around him. Russell admires Voltaire's satire and shares much of his scepticism. Leibniz' optimism can be read either as a deep theological position or as a thoughtless rationalisation of existing evil.

But Russell also points out that Leibniz' position is subtler than Voltaire suggests. Leibniz did not claim that everything in every situation is best for those involved. He claimed that the whole of the world, considered as one coherent system extending over all times and all beings, contains the maximally possible good that can be realised in a consistent world. That is a much weaker claim than "everything is good", and it is hard to refute because we cannot compare with other possible worlds.

**The principle of sufficient reason.** At the base of Leibniz' system lies the **principle of sufficient reason**: nothing exists without a reason for it. Nothing happens without cause or motive. This metaphysical premise leads Leibniz to a form of **logical determinism**: everything that happens must happen so because there is sufficient reason for it.

Here Russell discovers the logical depth in Leibniz' work. The principle of sufficient reason is a remarkably powerful assumption. Russell points out that it is in fact a strengthening of the claim that everything has a cause — sufficient reason requires not only that there is a cause, but that the cause is sufficient to cause precisely this and nothing else. Russell shows that Leibniz can draw conclusions from this about necessity, identity and the nature of substance that are not explicitly worked out in his popular work but stand clearly in his private notes.

**Logic and language.** Leibniz' deepest philosophical work may have lain in the field of logic and language. He dreamed of a **universal characteristic** — an ideal symbol system in which all thoughts could be exactly represented, and in which all philosophical disputes could be solved by simply calculating. When two philosophers disagreed, they no longer needed to discuss — they would say "*Calculemus*" — "Let us calculate" — and the calculation would reveal the truth.

Russell admires this project as vision. It anticipates modern logic, symbolic logic, and aspects of computer science. Leibniz developed concrete proposals for logical notation that extended Aristotle's syllogistics. Russell sees here the real Leibniz: not the inventor of monads but the pioneer of logical analysis.

**Russell's own judgement.** Russell concludes that Leibniz' philosophy as a whole is not tenable, but that its parts are valuable. The monad doctrine is curious but impossible to prove or refute — a metaphysical speculation that explains little other than what it postulates itself. The pre-established harmony is an ad hoc solution to a problem Leibniz himself created. The optimism about the best of all possible worlds is hard to reconcile with lived experience.

But the logical analyses, the principle of sufficient reason, the dream of a universal characteristic, and the mathematical work that parallel to Newton produced calculus — all of that is of lasting value. Russell argues that Leibniz may have been the first to foresee modern logic in its fundamental structure. For that he is lastingly important, even when his monads fall into oblivion.

## Why this matters
Leibniz shows what the seventeenth-century synthesis between science, theology and philosophy could still attempt before it definitively fell apart in the following centuries. A philosopher could still invent mathematics and write theology, could still write about politics and metaphysics at the same time, could still dream of a universal language that would solve all conflicts. Much of that may seem naive to us now — especially the optimism about the best possible world — but it also shows what ambition modern philosophy once had. Leibniz' specific conclusions are largely outdated, but his methodological habits — strict logical analysis, belief in a deeper rational order, willingness to pose questions across all fields at once — work on. Added to this is something personal that touches Russell: Leibniz shows that a philosopher can write something different for public consumption than what he thinks privately. That is a warning for how we read philosophers — we must pay attention to what they do not write, to what they tone down, to what remains hidden between the lines.

## Open questions
- Which Leibniz is the "real" one — the public metaphysician of monads and harmony, or the private logician Russell discovered? Or were they two aspects of one mind, both necessary to understand him?
- Is the pre-established harmony as solution to the interaction problem really an improvement over Descartes' dualism, or merely a shifting of the problem to a greater mystery (God's coordinating work)?
- Can Leibniz' optimism about the best possible world be saved, or does it inevitably fall prey to Voltaire's mockery? Recent reconstructions by analytic philosophers suggest a refined version is defensible, but the discussion remains open.

## Further reading
- Bertrand Russell, *A Critical Exposition of the Philosophy of Leibniz* (1900) — Russell's own groundbreaking study, the book in which he discovered the "two Leibnizes"
- Russell, *History of Western Philosophy* (1945), Book 3 Part 1 chapter 11 — shorter treatment with the same key points
- Leibniz, *Monadology* (1714) — Leibniz' most concise philosophical work, a good starting point
- Leibniz, *Discourse on Metaphysics* (1686) — more elaborate working out, more readable than the *Monadology*
- Maria Rosa Antognazza, *Leibniz: An Intellectual Biography* (2009) — extensive modern biography showing the complexity of his work
