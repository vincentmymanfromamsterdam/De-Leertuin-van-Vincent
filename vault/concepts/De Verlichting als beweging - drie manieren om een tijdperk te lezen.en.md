# The Enlightenment as a movement - three ways of reading an era

## Core
The **Enlightenment** is the period from roughly 1680 to 1800 in which European thinkers began to test the authority of church, tradition and monarch against reason and experience. Kant summed it up in 1784 as man's emergence from an immaturity he has brought on himself, with the motto: dare to use your own understanding. There are three readings of what the Enlightenment actually was. The first sees it as a **programme of ideas**: reason, toleration, rights, progress. The second sees it as a **social change**: a reading and debating public arose outside church and university, and that public became a new source of authority. The third, from the historian Jonathan Israel, distinguishes **two Enlightenments**: a radical current beginning with Spinoza and a moderate one that left the existing order largely in place. The readings do not exclude one another. Together they explain why ideas that were partly older changed society in this century in particular.

## Explanation

**The period in outline.**

There is no sharp beginning or end, but three phases can be distinguished.

- **Early Enlightenment, about 1680 to 1720.** Newton publishes his *Principia* (1687) and shows that one set of laws describes heaven and earth. [[Locke and the tabula rasa - the beginning of empiricism|Locke]] writes on knowledge, on government and on toleration. Pierre Bayle publishes his critical dictionary in Rotterdam (1697). The background is political: in 1685 Louis XIV revokes the Edict of Nantes and drives the Protestants out of France, and in 1688 England gets a monarch bound to parliament.
- **High Enlightenment, about 1720 to 1770.** The centre of gravity lies in France, with the *philosophes*. Voltaire makes English thought known on the continent, Montesquieu dissects forms of government, Diderot and d'Alembert publish the *Encyclopédie* (from 1751), Rousseau turns against his friends' faith in progress. In Scotland [[Hume and the limit of empiricism - scepticism habit and the disappearing self|Hume]] and Adam Smith are at work.
- **Late Enlightenment, about 1770 to 1800.** The ideas become political. The American Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) use the language of natural rights. [[Kant and the Copernican revolution - how the mind shapes the world|Kant]] examines the limits of reason itself.

**First reading: a programme of ideas.**

This is the classic picture, worked out by Ernst Cassirer (1932) and Peter Gay (1960s). Here the Enlightenment is a shared way of thinking.

- **Reason and experience above authority.** A claim holds because it can be argued or observed, not because a church father or king pronounces it. Newton's physics was the model.
- **Criticism of revelation.** Many Enlightenment thinkers were deists: there is a creator, but he does not intervene and has not revealed himself in a book. A minority were atheists.
- **Toleration.** After a century and a half of wars of religion, freedom of conscience became a central demand.
- **Natural rights and limited power.** Government rests on consent, and power must be divided and checked.
- **Progress and usefulness.** Knowledge should improve life: better agriculture, more humane criminal law, education.

The weakness of this reading is that it suggests unity. Voltaire despised democracy, Rousseau distrusted science, Hume doubted that reason can do much. Nor were most of the ideas new. Toleration, criticism of miracles and the social contract are seventeenth-century. The question then is why they only took effect in the eighteenth.

**Second reading: a social change.**

This is where historians such as Jürgen Habermas and Robert Darnton find the answer. What was new was not so much what was thought as where and by whom. In coffeehouses, salons, reading societies, journals and learned societies a public arose that read, debated and judged without permission from church or court. Writers no longer addressed scholars in Latin but that public in the vernacular. "Public opinion" became a power monarchs had to reckon with.

On this reading the printer, the bookseller and the hostess of a salon matter as much as the philosopher. It also explains the geography: the movement flourished where censorship was weak or could be evaded, as in the Dutch Republic and England. This connects with the note on the [[Philosophy and its institutional context - why secular thinking needed a safe haven|institutional context of philosophy]] and is worked out in the note on [[The public sphere - how coffeehouses printing presses and societies made a judging public|the public sphere]].

The weakness is that a public as such has no direction. The same channels also spread gossip, pietism and conspiracy theories.

**Third reading: two Enlightenments.**

Since 2001 Jonathan Israel has argued that there were two currents fighting each other.

- The **radical Enlightenment** begins with [[Spinoza and the one substance - God or nature|Spinoza]] and his circle in the Dutch Republic. There is one substance and therefore no revelation, no miracles and no divinely ordained ranks. From this follow democracy, equality and full freedom of expression. Diderot, d'Holbach and later Condorcet belong here.
- The **moderate Enlightenment** of Locke, Newton, Voltaire and Montesquieu wanted to reconcile reason and faith, to reform without overthrowing, and left monarchy and ranks in place.

According to Israel the modern values of democracy and equal rights come from the radical line. The thesis has prompted a great deal of research and made the Dutch contribution visible. Critics find the division too rigid: many thinkers fit neither box, and that a metaphysics produces a politics is assumed more than shown. Rousseau, who was no Spinozist, shaped the democratic idea at least as strongly.

**Reservations about the whole.**

- **More than one Enlightenment.** In France the movement was anticlerical and stood opposed to the state. In Scotland and Germany its thinkers often worked within university and church. Historians therefore also speak of Enlightenments in the plural.
- **Enlightened monarchs.** Frederick the Great, Catherine the Great and Joseph II carried out reforms from above without giving up power.
- **Limits.** In practice the rights of man applied to men with property. Slavery and colonialism long went unchallenged, though there were fierce critics. Around 1790 Olympe de Gouges and Mary Wollstonecraft demanded that the principles apply to women too.

## Why this matters
The Enlightenment is the link between the system-builders of the seventeenth century and modern philosophy. Descartes, Spinoza and [[Leibniz and the monads - the best of all possible worlds|Leibniz]] wrote for scholars; the Enlightenment made philosophy a public matter. Anyone who wants to understand Kant, modern democracy or the idea of science as authoritative knowledge ends up here. The three readings are also an exercise in how to read history. The same events look different depending on whether you look at ideas, at institutions or at the struggle between factions. For the question of authority over knowledge the second reading matters most: authority shifted from office to the argument that has to prove itself before a public. Whether authority thereby disappeared or only changed bearer is an open question. In the terms of [[Religion as plausibility structure - Peter Berger and the social construction of sacredness|Berger]] one could ask whether the salons and societies themselves formed a new structure that made certain convictions self-evident.

## Open questions
- Were the ideas the cause of the social change, or did the social change give existing ideas their reach? And can that distinction be drawn at all?
- Is Israel right that democracy and equality derive from Spinoza's metaphysics, or does politics work by other routes than philosophical systems?
- Did the Enlightenment replace one authority with another, the church with "reason" and "the public", or did it really change how authority comes about?

- Dorinda Outram, *The Enlightenment* (1995, revised editions) — short survey centred on the social reading

- Anthony Pagden, *The Enlightenment and Why It Still Matters* (2013) — a defence of the legacy
