# Logical positivism - the Vienna Circle and the ideal of a science without metaphysics

## Core
**Logical positivism** is the movement that, from the 1920s to the 1950s, defined what philosophy of science was. Its core is a thesis about meaning: a statement says something only if it is either logically true, as in mathematics, or testable by observation. This is the **verification principle**. Whatever falls outside it, such as metaphysics, theology and much of ethics, is on the positivist view not false but meaningless. Science was seen as a logical structure resting on neutral observations and growing step by step. The movement arose in the **Vienna Circle** around Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap and Otto Neurath, moved to the United States because of Nazism, and was usually called **logical empiricism** there. It was undone by problems its own members were the first to expose: the verification principle could not be formulated in a way that held, and the neutral observation on which everything was to rest turned out not to exist. Its demand for clarity and testability lives on, in philosophy and in physics.

## Explanation

**Where it came from.**

From 1924 a group of philosophers, mathematicians and physicists met on Thursday evenings in Vienna around Moritz Schlick. In 1929 the group presented itself with a manifesto, *Wissenschaftliche Weltauffassung: Der Wiener Kreis*. In Berlin a kindred group worked around Hans Reichenbach. Three sources came together:

- The empiricism of [[Hume and the limit of empiricism - scepticism habit and the disappearing self|Hume]] and the physicist Ernst Mach: knowledge comes from experience, and concepts that cannot be traced back to experience do not belong in science.
- The new logic of Frege and [[Russell and logical analysis - the end of the great system|Russell]], which made it possible to analyse statements exactly.
- The *Tractatus* of [[Wittgenstein and the two faces of language - from logical purification to language game|Wittgenstein]], which the Circle went through sentence by sentence and read as an attack on metaphysics. Wittgenstein himself meant it differently.

To this came Einstein's theory of relativity. Einstein had shown that "simultaneous" only means something once you say how you measure it. For the positivists that was the model: a concept derives its content from the way you test it.

The programme was also political. In interwar Vienna, with its rising nationalism and its obscure philosophy of people and destiny, Neurath and Carnap saw clear language as a weapon. Someone who cannot hide behind untestable words has to say what he means.

**The four pillars.**

- **Meaning is verifiability.** There are two kinds of meaningful statement. *Analytic* statements are true by the meaning of their terms: logic and mathematics. *Synthetic* statements say something about the world and are meaningful in so far as observation can confirm or undermine them. "The Absolute is perfect" is neither and therefore says nothing.
- **A neutral observation language.** Beneath every theory lies a layer of pure observations, such as "the pointer stands at five". Theoretical terms like "electron" get their meaning through rules connecting them to that layer.
- **Justification separate from discovery.** How a researcher arrives at an idea is a matter for psychologists and historians. Philosophy examines only the logical relation between theory and evidence. The distinction is Reichenbach's.
- **Unity and accumulation.** All sciences can in principle be expressed in one language, that of physics. Knowledge grows as new theories absorb the old.

From this followed a picture of explanation that long remained standard. According to Carl Hempel a phenomenon is explained when you can derive it logically from general laws and initial conditions.

**How it spread.**

In 1936 Schlick was shot dead on the steps of the university by a former student. The Circle fell apart and its members fled Nazism. Carnap went to Chicago, Reichenbach via Istanbul to Los Angeles, Hempel and Feigl to American universities. Neurath ended up in England by way of The Hague. In the English-speaking world A.J. Ayer made the programme known with *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936). After the war logical empiricism was the self-evident way of doing philosophy of science at American universities.

**What brought it down.**

- **The principle hits itself.** The thesis that only analytic and verifiable statements have meaning is itself neither analytic nor verifiable. The positivists replied that it was a proposal for the use of language and not a claim. That saves the principle but makes it optional.
- **Laws cannot be verified.** "All metals expand when heated" concerns infinitely many cases. No series of observations proves it. Strictly speaking physics itself would become meaningless. Carnap therefore replaced verification with **confirmation**: observations make a law more probable. Each new formulation, however, either excluded science or let metaphysics back in. In 1950 Hempel took stock and concluded that no version worked.
- **Confirmation yields paradoxes.** Hempel himself showed that "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "everything that is not black is not a raven". A white shoe then confirms that ravens are black.
- **The analytic-synthetic divide wobbles.** W.V.O. Quine argued in 1951 that the boundary cannot be drawn sharply and that statements face experience not one by one but as a connected whole.
- **Observation is not neutral.** What someone sees depends on what he knows. N.R. Hanson worked this out in 1958, and [[Paradigm and scientific revolution - Thomas Kuhn and the social structure of knowledge|Kuhn]] made it the heart of his book in 1962.

[[Popper and falsification - why science advances by trying to refute its own theories|Popper]] had attacked the programme from outside as early as 1934. Kuhn later gave the final push, although his book appeared in the positivists' own series, with Carnap as editor. In 1967 the philosopher John Passmore wrote that logical positivism "is dead, or as dead as a philosophical movement ever becomes". Ayer later said of the movement that had made his name that its main defect was that nearly all of it was false.

**What remained.**

- The norm that a philosophical claim must be clear and checkable. All of analytic philosophy bears that stamp.
- The formal study of confirmation, which continues in today's probabilistic theory of evidence.
- An attitude in physics. Heisenberg built his quantum mechanics in 1925 explicitly on observable quantities, and the Copenhagen habit of rejecting questions about what "really" happens is positivist in spirit. See the note on the [[Interpretations of quantum mechanics - what they share where they differ and what is testable|interpretations]].

## Why this matters
Logical positivism is the background against which almost all later philosophy of science defines itself. Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos and Feyerabend can only be understood properly if you know which picture they were fighting. It is also a rare example of a philosophical school that took its own standard seriously. Its theses were formulated so precisely that they could be refuted, and the refutations came largely from within, from Hempel, Quine and the late Carnap. Finally, the underlying intuition has not gone away. Whoever asks of a confident claim which observation could undermine it is asking a positivist question. The movement failed in its attempt to turn that into a watertight criterion. That does not touch the question itself.

## Open questions
- If no criterion separates meaningful from meaningless statements, is there still a difference in kind between science and metaphysics, or only one of degree?
- The positivists wanted to fight ideology with clear language. Can a demand for testability do that work, or does political persuasiveness rest on something that escapes testing?
- Did the late Carnap, who saw linguistic frameworks as choices on practical grounds, stand as far from Kuhn as the usual story has it?

## Further reading
- A.J. Ayer, *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) — the programme at its most confident, short and readable
- Rudolf Carnap, "The Elimination of Metaphysics Through Logical Analysis of Language" (1932) — the famous attack on Heidegger
- Carl Hempel, "Problems and Changes in the Empiricist Criterion of Meaning" (1950) — taking stock from within
- W.V.O. Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951)
- David Edmonds, *The Murder of Professor Schlick* (2020) — accessible history of the Circle and its time
- Karl Sigmund, *Exact Thinking in Demented Times* (2017)
- Michael Friedman, *Reconsidering Logical Positivism* (1999) — reassessment, including the relation to Kant and Kuhn
