# Interpretations of quantum mechanics - what they share, where they differ and what is testable

## Core
All interpretations of quantum mechanics use the same mathematics and make the same prediction for virtually every feasible experiment. They differ about what that mathematics describes: is the **wave function** a real physical thing or an expression of what an observer expects, does the **Schrödinger equation** always hold or is it interrupted by measurement, and is the wave function the whole story or is something missing? The major families answer these three questions differently: **Copenhagen**, **Everett** (many-worlds, called "austere quantum mechanics" by Sean Carroll), **Bohmian mechanics**, **objective collapse**, and the **epistemic** approaches such as **QBism** and the relational interpretation. It is not a purely philosophical debate. One family, objective collapse, predicts measurably different outcomes and is now being actively tested; the simplest variants have already been ruled out. For the other interpretations tests exist in principle, but they are technically still far off or require conditions we cannot create. A hundred years after the theory emerged there is no consensus: in a 2025 *Nature* survey of more than 1,100 physicists no interpretation received a majority.

## Explanation

**What all interpretations share.**

The agreement is larger than the debate suggests. All interpretations accept:

- The formalism: states as vectors, evolution according to the Schrödinger equation as long as no measurement is made, and the Born rule giving probabilities of measurement outcomes.
- All existing experimental results, including interference of ever larger objects and the violation of the Bell inequalities.
- [[Decoherence and the transition from quantum to classical|Decoherence]] as a real physical process explaining why superpositions become invisible in practice at large scales.

A physicist can therefore calculate for an entire working life without choosing an interpretation.

**The three questions that divide them.**

The [[The measurement problem and its interpretations - why a definite outcome does not automatically follow|measurement problem]] can be summarised as three statements that cannot all be true. The formulation is due to the philosopher Tim Maudlin:

1. The wave function describes a system completely.
2. The wave function always evolves according to the Schrödinger equation.
3. A measurement has one definite outcome.

Each interpretation gives up one of them, or rejects the assumption that the wave function describes the system itself.

**The families.**

- **Copenhagen.** Not a sharply defined doctrine but an attitude going back to Bohr and Heisenberg. The theory predicts outcomes of measurements with classically described apparatus; questions about what "really" happens in between are rejected or left open. In textbooks this is the version with a collapse on measurement. Strength: always works. Weakness: does not say where the boundary between quantum system and measuring apparatus lies.
- **Everett, many-worlds.** Gives up statement 3. There is only the wave function and it always follows the Schrödinger equation. On measurement the world branches; every outcome occurs, in its own branch. Strength: the most economical formalism, with no extra rules. Weakness: whether the Born rule follows from it is disputed, and the ontology is enormous.
- **Bohmian mechanics.** Gives up statement 1. Besides the wave function there are particles with a definite position at every moment, guided by the wave function. The theory is deterministic; chance arises from ignorance of the initial positions. Strength: a clear ontology with no special role for measurement. Weakness: explicitly non-local, and hard to reconcile with relativity.
- **Objective collapse.** Gives up statement 2. The Schrödinger equation is modified with a spontaneous, random collapse process that grows stronger as an object gets heavier. Examples are GRW (1986), CSL and the proposal of Diósi and Penrose, in which gravity causes the collapse. Strength: one dynamics for small and large, and testable. Weakness: new constants of nature without further justification.
- **Epistemic and relational.** Reject the assumption behind all three statements. In **QBism** the wave function expresses an observer's personal expectations, and a measurement is the updating of them. In Rovelli's **relational interpretation** a system only has a state relative to another system. Strength: no measurement problem and no non-locality. Weakness: the theory no longer says what the world is like independently of observers.

**What experiments have already settled.**

One group of theories has been ruled out, and this holds for all interpretations at once: **local hidden variables**. John Bell showed in 1964 that such theories impose a limit on correlations between entangled particles. Experiments violate that limit, since 2015 also without the known loopholes; in 2022 Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger received the Nobel Prize for this. Each interpretation pays its own price: Bohm accepts non-locality, Everett gives up the single outcome, QBism and the relational interpretation give up that outcomes are the same facts for everyone.

**Where the predictions diverge.**

*Objective collapse.* This is the only family that clearly deviates in feasible experiments. There are two routes:

- **Making ever heavier objects interfere.** If collapse exists, interference must disappear above a certain mass. Markus Arndt's group in Vienna reported in early 2026 interference with metal clusters of more than 170,000 atomic mass units. Each step up rules out part of the collapse models.
- **Looking for side effects.** Collapse should heat matter very slightly and make it emit faint radiation. An experiment deep under the Gran Sasso did not find that radiation, and thereby ruled out the simplest version of the Diósi-Penrose model in 2021. For CSL the permitted strength has been strongly constrained; the original GRW values have not yet been reached.

Collapse models cannot adjust their parameters indefinitely: if the collapse becomes too weak, it no longer explains why large objects have one position. The space is thus bounded on two sides, which makes these models genuinely refutable. Experiments with levitated nanoparticles and nanodiamonds should search the remaining region further in the coming years. If collapse is found, Everett and the standard form of Bohm are refuted, since both assume an unmodified Schrödinger equation.

*Bohm.* Under the usual assumption about the distribution of initial positions the theory predicts exactly the same as standard quantum mechanics. Antony Valentini has proposed that this assumption did not hold in the early universe, which could leave traces in the cosmic background radiation. No indication of this has been found. There are also proposals to measure arrival times of particles, where Bohm makes a sharper prediction than the standard theory; this has not yet been carried out.

*Everett.* The difference from a theory in which collapse really happens is measurable in principle. If there is never a collapse, a measurement by an observer must be reversible, after which interference between the branches becomes visible. David Deutsch described such an experiment in 1985. It requires full quantum control over an observer, for instance a thinking program on a quantum computer, and lies far beyond what is now possible. Between Everett and Bohm no test is known.

*QBism and relational.* Extended versions of the "Wigner's friend" thought experiment yield inequalities that hold if measurement outcomes are absolute facts, nature is local and experimenters can choose freely. Experiments with photons in the role of observer violated those inequalities in 2019 and 2020. Whether a photon counts as an observer is the weak point; a convincing version requires the same as Deutsch's test.

*Gravity.* Penrose's proposal links collapse to gravity. Proposed experiments in which two small masses become entangled through gravity alone would show whether gravity behaves quantum mechanically. That touches the interpretation question sideways and the question of [[Quantum gravity and its various programmes - the search for unified physics|quantum gravity]] directly.

**The state of opinion.**

In the 2025 *Nature* survey 36 per cent chose Copenhagen, about 17 per cent an epistemic view, 15 per cent many-worlds and 7 per cent Bohm. Only 24 per cent thought their own preference was also the correct one; the rest called it useful or the least problematic.

## Why this matters
The interpretations are one of the few places in physics where it becomes visible that the same mathematics and the same measurements do not fix what exists. You can have a deterministic world of particles with definite trajectories, a world that constantly branches, or a theory that is only about expectations, and design the same semiconductor with all three. That is the problem of underdetermination in its purest form. For the note [[Valid versus useful - why an irrefutable argument need not explain anything|Valid versus useful]] this is a sharp case: the collapse models are testable now and are being constrained step by step, Everett is testable in principle but out of reach for the time being, and the difference between Everett and Bohm is, as far as is known, not testable at all. Those are three different kinds of untestability within one debate. It also shows how [[Paradigm and scientific revolution - Thomas Kuhn and the social structure of knowledge|Kuhn's]] model works and where it chafes. The calculating is normal science within one shared framework, while the foundations look like competing schools each putting different virtues of a theory first: simplicity of the equations, clarity about what exists, or economy in assumptions about reality. Who wins may depend less on arguments than on which family first makes a prediction that comes out right, or which proves most fruitful for a theory of quantum gravity.

## Open questions
- If experiments with ever heavier objects never find a collapse, when is objective collapse refuted? The models have a lower bound, but where exactly it lies depends on what you count as a "large object with one position".
- Does the Born rule really follow from the many-worlds theory, as Deutsch, Wallace and Carroll argue, or is it tacitly put in? There is no agreement on this, not even among proponents of Everett.
- Is the difference between two interpretations that make exactly the same predictions a physical difference, or only a difference in manner of speaking? And if the latter, why does the choice still feel to physicists like a question about reality?
