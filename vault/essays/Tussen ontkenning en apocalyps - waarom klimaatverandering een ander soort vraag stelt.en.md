# Between denial and apocalypse: why climate change asks a different kind of question

## Question/premise
Climate change is, in public debate, a subject mostly shouted about — by both sides. Sceptics shout "hoax", activists shout "extinction". One side points to manipulation of data and political agendas. The other to coral reefs melting away and polar bears drowning. Both sides assemble their evidence, both sides sincerely believe in their framing, and both sides usually cannot even hear each other's arguments.

What if both framings betray the subject? Not because there are no facts — there are overwhelmingly many — but because the facts require a different kind of thinking than either side offers. What changes in our understanding when we place climate change simultaneously in geological perspective *and* take the most legitimate sceptical arguments seriously? When we lay the three dimensions side by side — what we know about the earth, what we know about the future, and what we do not know?

The question is not *whether* climate change is happening. That question is scientifically settled — the earth is warming, humans are the main cause, the pace is exceptional. But that does not get us very far. Because the questions *how serious*, *where exactly do we end up*, and *what to do* are different questions, and uncertainty deserves a more central place there than it usually gets. Perhaps climate change is above all a test of whether we as a society can think in ranges rather than certainties, in scenarios rather than outcomes, and in acting under uncertainty rather than waiting for clarity.

## Synthesis

Begin with what geological perspective changes about the story. The earth has done this before. The Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum, 56 million years ago, was a climate crisis with a total CO₂ emission comparable to what humanity would produce if we burned all remaining fossil fuels. The temperature rise was 5-8 degrees, the oceans acidified, ecosystems shifted, species went extinct. And yet — this is important — after 100,000 to 200,000 years the system recovered. New ecosystems, new species, new equilibria. The earth survived, life survived, and geological history went on. At the Permian-Triassic boundary, 252 million years ago, the extinction was greater — 95% of marine species disappeared, recovery took 30 million years — but there too the earth eventually built up a new biosphere.

This perspective does something unexpected to the alarmist framing. Because the real point is not that the earth will "vanish" or "perish" — that language is geological nonsense. The real point is that the specific biosphere we know, with the specific species we depend on, is a product of the current climate zones. Changing those zones means losing that specific configuration. Not the end of life, but the end of *this* life. That is an important distinction, and it is precisely the distinction lost in apocalyptic language.

At the same time, the same geological perspective does something sobering to the sceptical framing. Because the earth has done this before, yes — but never so fast. The PETM spread its CO₂ emission over 3,000 to 20,000 years. Humanity is emitting a comparable amount in about 200 years — twenty to two hundred times faster. Even the Siberian Traps, the volcanic disaster that caused the Permian-Triassic extinction, probably had a lower annual emission rate than we have now. As far as geological history can determine, we are the fastest carbon emitter ever. Not the largest, not the worst in absolute terms, but the fastest.

And precisely in that speed lies the challenge. Species can adapt to much when they have time. Genetic selection, behavioural shifts, geographical migration — all these mechanisms work, but they need time. A species that gets ten thousand years to shift northwards usually makes it. A species that has to do it in two hundred years often does not. Trees under natural conditions migrate about a hundred metres per year. Climate zones now shift hundreds of kilometres in a human lifetime. That arithmetic simply does not work out.

So the geological framing offers a double lesson. It neither maximises nor minimises; it specifies. We are doing something the earth has done before — in total volume — but at a pace without precedent. The earth will survive. The biosphere will adapt. But the specific ecosystems we evolved in are not all going to make it. That is not alarmism; that is what the geology says.

What then about the future? Here it becomes more complex, and here uncertainty deserves a larger role than it usually gets. Current scenario projections show three possible paths. With rapid decarbonisation — warming limited to +1.5 to 2 degrees — we end up in an extinction comparable to or slightly above PETM. Five to fifteen percent of species disappear, coral reefs mostly gone, some polar ecosystems too, recovery takes 100,000 years. With moderate paths — +2.5 to 3.5 degrees — that shifts to 25-30%, feedback loops become active, recovery takes millions of years. With high-emission paths — +4 to 5 degrees — scientists begin to seriously make the comparison with Permian-Triassic. 50-75% of species, entire biomes vanish, recovery 10-30 million years.

Which path we follow we do not know. That is not scientific immaturity; that is fundamental uncertainty. The physics of CO₂ and temperature we know reasonably well; the physics of clouds and feedback loops less so; the political and economic developments that determine the emission rate not at all. What we know is that we sit somewhere among those paths, and that the choices of the coming decades determine where exactly. This is something other than saying "we are going extinct" or "it will be fine". It is saying: the outcome is a range, and the range narrows as policy becomes clearer.

Here epistemology enters. Because how do we know this in the first place? How can we make predictions about a world in 2200 based on physical models and historical data? David Hume would ask his old question here — what justifies inferring the future from the past? — and that question is sharper here than elsewhere, because the consequences of being wrong are enormous. Yet the answer is not "we know nothing" but something more nuanced: we know the physics of CO₂ and temperature with great certainty, we know the response of the climate system with moderate certainty, and we know the behavioural response of billions of people and hundreds of governments with very limited certainty. Different questions have different uncertainty levels, and honest science communication should make that distinction rather than hanging all conclusions on the same certainty.

This is precisely where the more serious climate sceptics have their best arguments. A Judith Curry — formerly an IPCC contributor, now critical of her own mainstream — does not point to a great conspiracy or scientific fraud. She points to the breadth of the range for climate sensitivity, to the communication of uncertainties, to the tendency of policymakers to narrow broad ranges for readability. A Richard Lindzen — MIT physicist — debates feedback mechanisms in tropical cloudiness. A Nicholas Lewis publishes statistical analyses suggesting that climate sensitivity may lie at the lower end of the IPCC range. These people are contradicted by the mainstream, and possibly they are wrong. But they raise scientifically valid questions, with data, in peer-reviewed publications. That is something fundamentally different from claiming the earth is flat.

This brings us to an important distinction that is rarely made in public debate: there are four different kinds of climate scepticism, each with its own validity status. Trend scepticism (is the earth actually warming?) is virtually refuted within mainstream science — the evidence is overwhelming. Attribution scepticism (is the human the cause?) is largely refuted, although smaller debates about relative contributions of solar variability and ocean cycles are legitimate. Impact scepticism (how serious will it become?) touches on uncertainties that mainstream researchers also acknowledge — climate sensitivity, regional impact, tipping points all have serious uncertainty ranges. And policy scepticism (is this policy the right response?) is not even scientific discussion but political-economic — a nuclear-energy advocate and a renewable-energy advocate can fully agree on the physics and still fundamentally disagree on the solution.

This distinction is more than academic subtlety. It makes the difference between "we have legitimate scientific discussions to conduct" and "we have political preferences to weigh". It makes the difference between sceptics who argue with data and sceptics who argue with rhetoric. It makes the difference between uncertainty ranges in IPCC reports and the summaries for policymakers that often simplify those ranges. And it makes the difference between science that makes its doubts explicit and science that suppresses its doubts because it fears political exploitation.

Because there is an uncomfortable paradoxical point here. Mainstream climate science generally acknowledges more uncertainty than the public communication of that same science suggests. IPCC reports give wide probability ranges; press releases give point estimates. Researchers are cautious in their papers; activists are emphatic in their pamphlets. This translation from science to public debate loses information, and scepticism subsequently profits from it — by pointing to the uncertainties that were in the original science but were lost in the communication. Sceptics thus become, sometimes unintentionally, the messengers of something science itself should have communicated but does not always.

Acknowledging this is no capitulation to climate scepticism. It is scientific honesty. And it is precisely what makes a third position between denial and apocalypse possible: a position that takes the facts as accurately as possible — including the uncertainty ranges — and on that basis acts under uncertainty rather than waiting for certainty.

What would such a third position look like? Not as a compromise between alarmism and denial — that would be intellectually cowardly, taking an average of two incorrect positions. But as a position that judges each argument on its own merits. That acknowledges the earth is warming, the human is the main cause, and the consequences will be serious. That also acknowledges the exact sensitivity of the climate system is uncertain, that some tipping points are better understood than others, and that policy debates about how to respond contain legitimate disagreement. That considers it possible we end up closer to PETM than to Permian-Triassic — or the other way round — and that acts on the basis of that range without pretending the outcome is settled.

This is a difficult position. More difficult than denial, because you must take science seriously. More difficult than alarmism, because you must take uncertainty seriously. More difficult than "waiting for more research", because the physics is clear enough to know that waiting costs more than acting. It is a position that demands intellectual work, continuous reconsideration, willingness to test your own assumptions — everything Russell called "civilised thinking", and what in Kahneman terms requires System 2 on a question where System 1 would gladly give a quick answer. It is precisely the kind of thinking rarely conducted in public discussions, because it requires more patience than a tweet can carry.

## What this makes visible

The most interesting climate discussions are not about whether-or-not questions but about where-exactly questions. Not "is there climate change" but "where in the range between PETM and Permian-Triassic do we end up". Not "what causes it" but "what is the exact climate sensitivity and what are the tipping points". Not "should we do something" but "what combination of mitigation and adaptation, in what proportion, on what timescale". That is a different kind of conversation than public debate usually conducts, and it is exactly the conversation that should be taking place.

This touches something deeper about how people deal with uncertainty. Earlier in the Leertuin we distinguished five strategies — religion, science, philosophy, scepticism, myth — that each have their own relation to the unknown. Climate change calls primarily for the scientific and philosophical strategies, with scepticism as healthy control. But in public discussion both sides often function in a more mythic register — the sceptic who distrusts the government as a kind of archetypal story, the alarmist who paints the apocalypse as a kind of revelation. Those are both older forms of meaning-making that are inappropriate in this specific case. It is no revelation story; it is a scientific prediction with uncertainty ranges.

What this also makes visible is a peculiar human position. For the first time in history, a species is aware of the climate disturbance it is causing. The Siberian Traps did not "know" what they were doing. The methane hydrates that partly caused the PETM did not "choose" their emission. We do. That is no question of guilt — guilt requires a choice we did not collectively make — but it is a new position. And it creates a unique responsibility that only our species has: not as victim of a natural disaster, and not as perpetrator of an intentional act, but as the first participant in her own climate crisis who can in principle choose to stop.

Whether we make that choice depends not on whether we understand the problem — we largely do. It depends on politics, economics and culture. And it is exactly there that it is unfortunate that most discussions about climate focus on the physics while the problem operates at other levels. The physics is clear enough. What is unclear — and what makes the scenario projections diverge so widely — is how billions of people and hundreds of governments will respond to a crisis that extends across timescales no human institutions are designed to survey.

This makes climate change ultimately not a climate question but a question about what kind of beings we are. Whether we are capable of collectively thinking in centuries rather than electoral terms. Whether we can act under uncertainty without paralysing in doubt or getting stuck in dogma. Whether we can follow science where it is clear and accept doubt where it is legitimate. These are questions no climate model answers, but they are what the entire climate problem ultimately turns on.

There is then a curious hope in this position. Not the optimistic hope of "it will be fine" — there is no scientific basis for that. And not the resigned hope of "we are doing our best under poor circumstances" — that leaves too much room for inertia. But the hope that precisely the difficulty of climate change forces us to develop thinking skills we would otherwise never develop. Thinking in ranges rather than certainties. Acting under uncertainty rather than waiting. Distinguishing between what we know, what we suspect, and what we do not know — and drawing a different conclusion from each. Those are skills Russell would have loved, that Hume would have approved, and that Popper would have identified as core to the open society. Climate change is in that sense not only a crisis but also a learning process — or it becomes one, if we draw the right lessons.

## Further reading

- Naomi Oreskes & Erik Conway, *Merchants of Doubt* (2010) — indispensable for understanding how scepticism was organised, but at the same time useful to read against Curry or Lindzen to see what it does not capture
- David Wallace-Wells, *The Uninhabitable Earth* (2019) — elaborated high-emission scenario, honest about its own alarmist register
- Bjorn Lomborg, *False Alarm* (2020) — policy sceptic who despite controversial conclusions is worth reading for his rigorous cost-benefit approach
- IPCC AR6, [Working Group I report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/) — for those who want to see for themselves how science presents its uncertainty ranges, in contrast to press releases and summaries
- Peter Brannen, *The Ends of the World* (2017) — paleontological perspective that makes the geological timescale palpable in a way discussions about 2100 cannot
