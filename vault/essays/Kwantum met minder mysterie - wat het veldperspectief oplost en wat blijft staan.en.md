# Quantum with less mystery — what the field perspective resolves and what remains

## Question/premise

What is left of the famous mystery of quantum mechanics when we look at it soberly? Popular books teach that particles go through two slits at once, that consciousness influences reality and that looking changes nature. Much of that is untenable or misleadingly put. But the opposite claim, that clear thinking leaves no puzzle at all, also goes too far. This essay tries to separate what the field perspective and decoherence really resolve from what they leave standing, and takes a position on the remainder without pretending it is the only one.

## Synthesis

**What the field perspective resolves**

The old picture works with particles that sometimes behave as waves and mysteriously "choose" when someone looks. Physicists no longer think that way. In quantum field theory electrons and photons are excited states of fields that fill all of spacetime. In the double-slit experiment no little ball goes through two slits. A disturbance of the field propagates and interferes with itself. With that, the question whether something is "really" a particle or a wave falls away.

The observer also loses its special role. A measurement is an ordinary interaction between the photon field and the fields the detector consists of. A photodetector works just as well when nobody is looking, and consciousness does not appear in the description.

One reservation belongs here. A quantum field is not a classical wave in space. For two or more particles the theory does not describe waves at separate places but one state for the whole, and [[Entanglement - the non-classical connection unique to quantum physics|entanglement]] cannot be drawn as ripples in ordinary space. The field picture clears away the apparent paradoxes, but it does not make the theory classical.

**What decoherence resolves**

[[Decoherence and the transition from quantum to classical|Decoherence]] explains why we see no interference at large scales. Every system is coupled to its environment: there are always thermal radiation, air molecules and other influences interacting with it. Each interaction transfers a little information about the state of the system to the environment. Once there are enough of them, the branches of a superposition can be told apart in the environment and can no longer interfere with one another.

This is a quantitative theory. For a baseball in a superposition of two visibly different positions the decoherence time is around 10⁻²⁰ seconds or shorter, for a protein in a cell around 10⁻¹³ to 10⁻¹⁵ seconds. A large molecule in ultra-high vacuum lasts milliseconds, an atom in a good ion trap seconds to minutes, a single photon in vacuum almost indefinitely. The exact numbers depend on how far apart the states in the superposition are, but the orders of magnitude make the point. There is no sharp boundary between quantum and classical. There is one theory, and there are degrees of isolation that determine how much of it remains visible.

**Where it chafes**

Decoherence explains why we see no interference. It does not explain why we see one outcome. That distinction is the core of what remains.

Take a photon falling on a detector. The photon field has amplitude at all the atoms of the detector, and the interaction couples it to all of them. What the equations deliver is a superposition: atom one excited, plus atom two excited, and so on. Because of decoherence those branches can no longer interfere, but they are all still there. Nowhere does the mathematics say that it happened here and not there. Yet the detector clicks in one place.

It is tempting to put the chance in the interaction itself. It is not that simple, because most interactions choose nothing. A photon passing through glass or bouncing off a mirror interacts with countless atoms and still remains in superposition; otherwise no interferometer would work. A quantum computer computes by coupling qubits to each other while the superposition is preserved. Coupling therefore gives entanglement, not an outcome. Whoever says that the interaction chooses the place must say which interactions do so and which do not. That is the [[The measurement problem and its interpretations - why a definite outcome does not automatically follow|measurement problem]], now in the language of fields.

Radioactive decay is often cited as an example of chance that nobody loses sleep over. It is the same problem. According to the equations an atomic nucleus ends up in a superposition of decayed and not decayed. Schrödinger devised his cat in 1935 precisely to show how strange that becomes when you extend it to something large.

**What Bell does and does not say**

In classical physics chance is always ignorance: a die is unpredictable because we do not know the initial conditions. Quantum mechanics seems to break with that, and Bell's theorem of 1964 is often cited as proof that [[Chance in nature - intrinsic uncertainty and what it means|chance lies in nature itself]]. That says too much. Bell and the experiments that followed rule out that nature is both local and has outcomes fixed in advance. They do not rule out determinism. In Bohmian mechanics every outcome is fixed in advance, at the price of direct influence at a distance. In the many-worlds theory the wave function evolves fully deterministically and chance is a matter of perspective within one branch. Fundamental chance is therefore one possible answer to the experiments, and not the only one.

**A position: chance in the interaction**

From the field picture the most natural reading is still this: where a field hands over its quantum is fundamentally random, with probabilities given by the Born rule. The probability of a click at a given place is proportional to the square of the field amplitude there. That is a defensible position, provided it answers the question of which interactions count. There are two ways of working it out.

- **Objective collapse.** The choice is a real natural process that is rare for single particles and almost immediate for large objects. The outcome is the same for everyone. This version makes deviating predictions and is now being tested; the simplest versions have already been ruled out.
- **Relational.** Every interaction yields an outcome, but only relative to the system interacted with. For the detector the photon has a definite place; for an outsider, photon and detector together are still in superposition. No criterion is needed then, but there is also no fact that holds for everyone.

Other answers reject the starting point. According to Everett all interactions take place, each in its own branch. According to Bohm the place of the click was already fixed. This essay settles on chance in the interaction, aware that this choice is a preference and not a result of the theory. What separates the versions from one another is set out in the note on the [[Interpretations of quantum mechanics - what they share where they differ and what is testable|interpretations]].

## What this makes visible

The popular mysteries were largely the result of a formulation. Whoever speaks of particles that sometimes behave as waves and of observation that changes reality gets paradoxes thrown in. Whoever speaks of fields and their interactions loses most of them: the duality, the conscious observer and the hard boundary between quantum and classical.

What remains is smaller than the popular picture and larger than a technical detail. It is two connected questions: why is there one outcome, and why does the Born rule give the right probabilities? It is not a crisis, because the theory predicts every measurement flawlessly. But the answers in circulation differ about what exists: one world with real chance, many branches, particles with definite trajectories, or only facts relative to something.

Nor is the question purely philosophical. By the criterion from [[Valid versus useful - why an irrefutable argument need not explain anything|Valid versus useful]] the answers fall into three kinds. Objective collapse is testable now. The difference between Everett and real collapse is testable in principle but far out of reach. Between some other pairs no test is known.

There is also a lesson here about framing, and it cuts both ways. A question like "how can a particle be in two places at once?" presupposes little balls and makes the theory more puzzling than it is. But "there is no mystery" is just as much a framing, one that makes a real problem smaller than it is. An earlier version of this essay did that: it claimed that the interaction between fields explains the collapse. That is not right, for the reasons given above, and the essay was revised accordingly in November 2026.
