# Divided power, shared language — why thought flourishes where nobody has the last word

## The observation

In the Dutch Republic of the seventeenth century a book banned in one city could appear in the next. There was no monarch and no central censorship. Power lay with provinces and cities that competed with one another. Reading that, one soon thinks of classical Greece. There too power was divided among hundreds of small states, and there too an unusual amount of new thinking arose in a short time.

The question of this essay is whether that resemblance is a coincidence. Does thought flourish where power is divided? And if so, what else has to be present?

## Hume already saw it

The idea is old. In 1742 David Hume wrote an essay on the rise of the arts and sciences. His thesis: nothing is more favourable to civilisation and learning than a number of neighbouring, independent states connected by commerce and policy. Greece, he held, was such a group of small states. The Europe of his own day he called an enlarged copy of what Greece had been in miniature.

Hume gave two reasons. Small states check one another's power, and they check one another's authority. The second is the interesting one. In a large empire with one language and one law the doctrine of a single master spreads easily from one corner to the other, and nobody dares contradict it. Hume pointed to China and the authority of Confucius. Among small states this does not work. What is admired in one city is checked over in the neighbouring city out of jealousy.

## The mechanism

Why would divided power help? Three routes can be distinguished.

**Exit.** Whoever has one government against him does not have all governments against him. The thinker can move, or his book can be printed elsewhere. That makes a ban costly and not very effective.

**Competition for talent.** Monarchs and cities want prestige, and scholars, artists and printers supply it. A ruler who is too strict loses them to the neighbour.

**No single point of decision.** In an empire one decision can end a whole line of inquiry. The best-known example is the Chinese fleet of Zheng He, which sailed as far as East Africa in the early fifteenth century. After 1433 the court put an end to the voyages, and that was that. In the same century Columbus was turned down by Portugal and then found a backer in Spain. In a divided system an idea needs to hear yes only once.

## What else is needed

Divided power alone is not enough. Early medieval Europe was as politically fragmented as it could be, and little that was new was thought. From the cases where it did work, four further conditions can be read off.

- **A shared language and culture.** Ideas must be able to cross the border while authority cannot. The Greeks had their language, their poets and their common games. Europe had Latin, later French, and the correspondence of the Republic of Letters. This is probably the most important condition, and it explains the title of this essay.
- **Cities and trade.** Trade brings prosperity, leisure and strangers. Whoever sees daily that others do things differently finds his own ways less self-evident.
- **A medium and readers.** Writing, schools, later the printing press. Without a way to preserve and move thoughts every conversation stays local.
- **No monopoly on doctrine.** There must be no body that determines in all states at once what is true.

## Four cases

**Classical Greece.** Around 600 BC the search for natural explanations began in the trading cities of Ionia. The Greek world counted some thousand city-states, with no common king and no priesthood with a sacred book. Thinkers travelled. Anaxagoras was charged with impiety in Athens and worked on in Lampsacus. Aristotle withdrew to Chalcis after the death of Alexander. That [[Socrates and methodical doubt - I know that I know nothing|Socrates]] stayed and drank the hemlock shows that the freedom did not lie in the cities themselves. It lay in the space between them.

**China in the Warring States period.** From the sixth to the third century BC a handful of states fought for supremacy. It was the time of Confucius, Mozi, Mencius, Zhuangzi and the Legalists, later called the "hundred schools". Itinerant scholars offered their services to the court that paid best, and the state of Qi maintained an academy where rival schools worked side by side. In 221 BC Qin unified the realm. Eight years later the first emperor had the writings of the schools burned, and under the Han Confucianism became state doctrine. Thinking did not stop, but the plurality did.

**The Italian city-states.** From the fourteenth to the sixteenth century Florence, Venice, Milan, Rome and Naples competed for artists and scholars. Leonardo worked in turn for Florence, Milan, Rome and the French king. Galileo taught for eighteen years in Padua, under the authority of Venice, which paid little heed to Rome. In 1610 he moved to Florence for a better post. There the Inquisition could later reach him. The flourishing ended when the peninsula came under Spanish domination and the church, after the Council of Trent, could act everywhere at once with the Index and the Inquisition.

**The Dutch Republic and the Europe of the Republic of Letters.** Here the mechanism worked at two levels. Within the Republic cities and provinces competed. Beyond it the European states competed, and the Republic was the place where what was forbidden elsewhere got printed. Descartes, Locke and Bayle worked there as foreigners or exiles. In the German Empire the Elector of Saxony protected Luther against emperor and pope. The philosopher Christian Wolff was driven out of Prussian Halle in 1723 and went on teaching in Marburg, in another principality.

## Who worked this out

After Hume, Montesquieu pointed to geography: Europe is divided by mountains and seas into medium-sized pieces and is hard to gather into one empire. In the twentieth century Eric Jones made the European states system the explanation for the continent's economic lead. Joel Mokyr has focused the idea on knowledge. He speaks of a market for ideas and calls the combination of political fragmentation and cultural unity the core. Walter Scheidel argues that the best thing that happened to Europe is that the Roman Empire never returned.

The sociologist Randall Collins arrives at the same place by another road. He studied the networks of philosophers in all the major traditions and found that innovation occurs where three to six rival schools compete for attention. With only one, thought ossifies. With too many, it falls apart. Karl Jaspers noted that the great flowering in Greece, India and China fell in the same centuries, and each time in a world of small, warring states.

## Where it chafes

The thesis is attractive, and for that very reason caution is in order.

**Empires have flourished too.** Abbasid Baghdad was the centre of science in the ninth century, with caliphs who paid for translations from Greek. Song China had printing, the compass and a revival of philosophy and technology, within one empire with one civil-service examination. Hume would reply that such flourishing depends on the favour of the court and can end with a change of guard. For Baghdad that is partly right: when the caliphate broke up, a scholar like Avicenna moved from court to court, and science flourished for centuries more in a divided Islamic world with Arabic as its shared language. That fits the pattern again. But it does not refute that an empire with money and interest can produce a great deal.

**Fragmentation means war.** The Greek cities exhausted one another in the Peloponnesian War and then fell prey to Macedon. Italy became the battlefield of France and Spain. The Thirty Years' War cost parts of Germany a third of their population. Competition between states is no peaceful contest. The flourishing often falls in the pauses for breath.

**The cases are chosen after the fact.** Whoever starts from eras we admire and then looks for what they have in common will always find something. A fair test would count all fragmented regions, including those where nothing happened.

**Cause and effect are hard to separate.** Trade makes cities rich and independent, and the same trade brings foreign ideas. Perhaps divided power is not a cause of the flourishing, but both have the same root.

**Innovating is not the same as building up.** After Alexander the centre of gravity of Greek science lay in Alexandria, under kings, with a library and salaried scholars. Euclid and Eratosthenes worked there. It may be that new thoughts arise in the friction between small states, and that patient elaboration does better under a wealthy patron.

## What remains

The strong version of the thesis, that thought flourishes only where power is divided, does not hold. The counterexamples are too large.

A more modest version is tenable. Divided power within a shared culture makes it hard to smother an idea. It does not guarantee that anything new is thought, but what has been thought can survive and spread. An empire can produce much as long as the ruler wishes it. A divided system does not need that goodwill. The difference therefore lies less in the height of the flourishing than in how vulnerable it is.

That makes it the same thought as in the notes on the [[Philosophy and its institutional context - why secular thinking needed a safe haven|institutional context of philosophy]] and [[The public sphere - how coffeehouses printing presses and societies made a judging public|the public sphere]], one level up. What can be thought and said depends on how authority is organised. [[Religion as plausibility structure - Peter Berger and the social construction of sacredness|Berger]] describes how pluralism strips a conviction of its self-evidence, because there are always neighbours who believe something else. Hume's small states do the same to authority. No master is master everywhere.

Two things stand out. In none of the four cases was the freedom intended. It was a by-product of rulers who distrusted one another and merchants who wanted to earn. The principle of free expression was formulated only later, by people who already knew the practice. And the conditions do not last. Each of the four periods ended when one power took over the whole or war swallowed everything.

For the present this yields questions and no conclusions. The internet is a shared space in which ideas travel almost without borders. Whether authority within it is divided depends on how many platforms, states and institutions really decide alongside one another. Hume's standard is simple to apply: is there still a neighbour with whom a rejected thought can find a home?
