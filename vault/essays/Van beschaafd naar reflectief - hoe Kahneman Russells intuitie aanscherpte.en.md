# From civilised to reflective: how Kahneman sharpened Russell's intuition

## Question/premise
Bertrand Russell opened his *History of Western Philosophy* in 1945 with a distinction I used in [[Between myth and method - how humans handle intrinsic uncertainty|the first essay]]: between "barbaric" and "civilised" thinking. Not as a cultural hierarchy, he emphasised, but as a psychological distinction between reacting to direct impulses and the capacity to hold on to abstract long-term goals. The gesture of sowing-in-March-to-harvest-in-September was for Russell the core gesture of civilisation — the willingness to do something now whose outcome is uncertain.

The question is whether this distinction holds. Because on closer inspection, everyone is constantly both. A mathematician who works out a complex proof then jumps in fright at a snake in the garden. A philosopher who meditates for hours on moral dilemmas reacts grumpily when someone takes his parking spot. Russell describes the difference as if it runs between people or cultures. But the honest observation is that the difference runs through every individual, at every moment, in every situation. No one is consistently civilised. No one is consistently barbaric. We constantly switch between modes, often without noticing.

Daniel Kahneman published *Thinking, Fast and Slow* in 2011, a synthesis of decades of research with Amos Tversky. He introduces a vocabulary that captures precisely that switching: two systems that work in parallel in every human mind. What if Kahneman in fact completes what Russell intuitively saw, but without the instruments to develop it?

## Synthesis
Kahneman's central distinction is operationally simple. **System 1** works fast, automatically, in parallel, effortlessly, emotionally charged. It recognises patterns, makes intuitive estimates, fills in missing information spontaneously. It is always working, cannot be switched off, and is responsible for the vast majority of our daily decision-making. **System 2** works slowly, deliberately, serially, expending energy. It analyses, calculates, compares options, weighs consequences. It is selectively active, only when System 1 hands it a problem it cannot solve itself, or when we consciously engage it.

The parallel with Russell is obvious. Russell's "barbaric" thinking — direct response to impulse, fast pattern recognition, little forward looking — sounds strongly like System 1. Russell's "civilised" thinking — delayed gratification, abstraction, long-term planning — sounds like System 2. The sowing example is precisely what System 2 makes possible: a pure System 1 creature would eat the seeds because they contain calories now. Only when System 2 intervenes — "these seeds represent many more calories over six months provided I now put them in the ground" — does agriculture become possible.

Russell had the right intuition. But his formulation did three things that Kahneman corrects.

First: Russell suggested that the distinction ran between people. He denied that explicitly, but the terms "barbaric" and "civilised" carry that suggestion anyway. Kahneman makes empirically clear that the distinction runs within every individual. Everyone has both systems, they operate constantly, they regularly produce different answers. Which system is dominant depends on the situation, fatigue, emotional state, familiarity of the context. Not on the kind of person you are.

Second: Russell implicitly treated System 1 as a shortcoming, something to be overcome by civilisation. Kahneman shows that System 1 is not a primitive remnant but essential. If you had to make every decision with System 2 — what you eat or do not eat, which side to cross, how to recognise a face — you would cognitively collapse within an hour. System 1 is not a shortcoming but a function. It becomes problematic only when deployed for problems where System 2 is needed. The distinction is not good-versus-bad, but appropriate-versus-inappropriate.

Third: Russell spoke from historical observation and philosophical intuition. Kahneman grounds his frame in hundreds of experiments — anchoring effects (an arbitrary number influences later estimates), framing effects (the same choice in different words yields different decisions), loss aversion (loss weighs psychologically about twice as heavily as equivalent gain), the availability heuristic (we estimate probabilities by how easily we can think of examples). Russell had no vocabulary for these specific mechanisms. He knew *that* people thought unreliably, not precisely *how*.

**The five strategies from the first essay gain a new layer through Kahneman.** Religion, science, philosophy, scepticism, myth — they are not only cultural coping strategies for uncertainty, they are also **cognitive interventions** each establishing a specific relation between System 1 and System 2.

Religion offers System 1 peace through a fixed structure: rituals, beliefs, community. It does not necessarily require System 2 work; you do not have to have thought through the theology to experience the psychological effect. Kahneman's research shows that repetition and social confirmation strongly reassure System 1. A person with religious habits experiences less cognitive load on existential questions, because System 1 no longer has to search — the answer is ready. That is not a weakening of thought, it is a withholding of thought on specific questions, in favour of action and peace of mind.

Science is a System 2 discipline par excellence. Peer review, replicability, falsification — these are all ways to force System 1's quick conclusions into proof. A scientist who "knows for sure" her hypothesis is right, before she analyses the data, is using System 1. The method forces her to activate System 2 anyway. This explains why science is so unnatural and laborious — System 2 is energy-consuming, and most people avoid it where possible. [[Hume's problem of induction|Hume]] actually saw this: he asked why we jump from isolated observations to general laws, and the answer lies largely in System 1 — we *cannot but* assume patterns, even where we cannot justify them.

Philosophy sits somewhere in between. It tries to apply System 2 to questions where System 1 normally has the last word — what is good, how should I live, what do I really know. But because the answers cannot be empirically tested, there remains room for System 1 intuitions that philosophers then try to underpin with System 2. Russell himself was aware of this. In *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912) he acknowledges that many philosophical positions begin as intuitions and only afterwards receive argumentation. He did not call it that, but it is in fact System 1 hiring System 2 as advocate.

Scepticism can come from either system, and that is precisely where it gets interesting. Methodological scepticism — Curry's uncertainty analysis I described in the [[Four forms of climate scepticism - what sceptics actually say|climate scepticism note]] — is System 2 at its best, explicitly slowing down to test assumptions. Psychological scepticism — "I just don't trust those experts" — is often System 1 disguising itself as deliberation. Kahneman's research shows how hard it is to distinguish these two, even for the sceptic himself. What feels like critical thinking can in reality be a System 1 response that rationalises itself. [[Occam's razor|Occam's razor]] has the same double character: used as a System 2 discipline it is powerful, used as a System 1 prejudice ("simpler is always true") it is misleading.

Myth and art speak to System 1 directly, in a way System 2 cannot replicate. A good story about loss makes loss bearable without argument. A ritual around a life transition works psychologically, not logically. This is not a shortcoming but a function — there are aspects of the human condition that can only be processed via System 1. It is a mistake of Enlightenment thinking to assume that System 2 can take over the work here. Russell saw this partly; in his later essays he acknowledged that secular alternatives to religious rituals are difficult to construct precisely because System 2 does not fulfil the need that System 1 has.

## What this makes visible
The deeper observation is that the real problem is not that some people are "barbaric" and others "civilised". The problem is that **the same person, in different situations, activates different systems** — and that we rarely consciously choose which system we deploy. We think we are using System 2 while System 1 does the work. We engage System 2 for trivial decisions and use System 1 for life decisions. We rationalise post-hoc what System 1 has already decided.

A climate sceptic who reacts emotionally to an argument about CO₂ is not doing something fundamentally different from a climate activist who reacts emotionally to fossil fuels. Both activate System 1, and both think they are using System 2. The real distinction in a debate is not "who is rational" but "who has the discipline to systematically apply System 2 to their own convictions, precisely when System 1 disagrees with the outcome". That is a higher bar than "being rational" — it requires recognising the moment when System 1 disguises itself as System 2.

Russell would, I think, have acknowledged this had he been able to read Kahneman. His "mature handling of uncertainty" — intellectual honesty, moral courage, lifelong willingness to correct — describes in fact a **discipline of System 2 over System 1**. Not the elimination of System 1, which cannot happen and would be undesirable, but the habit of deploying System 2 precisely where System 1 would normally have the last word.

Here also lies a nuance on Kahneman himself. His frame is not uncontested. Some cognitive psychologists find the two-systems metaphor too schematic — cognition probably does not run through two discrete systems but through a continuum from automatic to deliberative processes. Kahneman himself acknowledges this in later editions and interviews; he uses "System 1" and "System 2" as useful conceptual tools, not as anatomical reality. Keith Stanovich has argued that the decisive distinction lies not between fast and slow cognition but between **reflective** and **non-reflective** thinking — the willingness to examine your own intuitions, regardless of how quickly they arise. Someone can think fast and reflectively; someone can think slowly and non-reflectively. That is closer to Russell's "intellectual honesty" than the Kahneman formulation suggests.

It is intriguing that Russell, Stanovich and Kahneman ultimately identify the same fundamental gesture, from three different disciplines and with three different vocabularies. What Russell called "civilised" thinking, what Kahneman calls "System 2", what Stanovich calls "reflective" thinking — these are three attempts to describe the same phenomenon: the capacity to slow down your own thinking, examine it, correct it. None of the three has the last word. But together they show the phenomenon is real, whatever you call it.

What this means in practice is modest but important. The work of "civilised" thinking — of System 2, of reflective thinking — is never done. You never reach a plateau where you are henceforth rational. You live in constant tension between System 1 and System 2, and the only progress possible is recognising better when System 1 has taken the wheel while System 2 should have been driving. That sounds disappointing relative to the ideal of pure rationality. But it is more honest, and it makes the ideal approachable at all — as a habit of self-examination rather than as an attainable state.

## Further reading
- Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) — the original synthesis, still the best introduction to the two-systems model
- Keith Stanovich, *Rationality and the Reflective Mind* (2011) — critical elaboration refining the distinction to reflective versus non-reflective thinking
- Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912) — Russell's own reflection on how philosophical positions arise: [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5827)
- Jonathan Haidt, *The Righteous Mind* (2012) — shows how moral judgments almost always arise in System 1 and are only afterwards rationalised by System 2
